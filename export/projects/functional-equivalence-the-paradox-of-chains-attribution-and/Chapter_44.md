# Chapter 44 — The Stone That Would Not Crack


Reeves came back to consciousness the way a soldier learns to—  

Quietly, without revealing he’s awake.


A slow inhale.  

A controlled exhale.  

Eyes still closed.


He catalogued everything.


Restraints: metal, jointed, professional.  

Position: seated, anchored to floor bolts.  

Lighting: cold, overhead, interrogation-standard.  

Air temperature: controlled to discomfort.  

Distance to walls: three meters front, two left, two right.  

Sound: faint hum of recycled air. No footsteps.


He opened his eyes.


A room.  

Clean.  

Clinical.


And not a prison—  

A facility.


He flexed his wrists.  

The restraints didn’t budge.


A shadow moved behind the one-way glass.  

Someone was watching.


Good.


Reeves lifted his chin.


“Let’s get this over with.”


The door slid open.


A man entered.  

Not dressed like the operators—no armor, no blackout gear.  

A suit.  

Neutral colors.  

Neutral face.  

Neutral presence.


The kind of man used when they wanted to break someone without revealing who “they” was.


“General Reeves,” the man said calmly.  

“You’re awake sooner than anticipated.”


Reeves didn’t answer.


He didn’t give anything he didn’t have to.


The man sat across from him, folding his hands.


“We prefer cooperation. It will make this easier.”


Reeves stared at him.


“Nothing about this is going to be easy for you.”


The man didn’t react.


“Your cooperation is required. You protected an asset of interest. You interfered with a sanctioned operation. You participated in an unauthorized network of citizens.”


Reeves almost laughed.


“That what you call people trying not to die these days?”


The man continued as if he hadn’t spoken.


“We require information on Sarah Kade. Her location. Her role. Her level of access. The degree of connection between her and the construct.”


Reeves’ expression didn’t change.


He let silence fill the room like concrete.


The man waited.  

Reeves waited longer.


Eventually, the man rose.


“Very well.”


He pressed a panel on the wall.


The restraints tightened—not painfully, but pointedly, reminding Reeves they were there.


A second panel slid open.


Reeves prepared himself.


Pain wasn’t the point.  

Not for people like this.


Pressure was.


Isolation.  

Deprivation.  

Micro-shock resets timed with heart rhythm.  

Sensory distortion.  

Temperature shifts calibrated to wear down will, not body.


The man spoke softly, almost kindly.


“You will speak. Everyone speaks.”


Reeves finally smiled.  

It was small.  

Sharp.  

Honest.


“You don’t know many generals.”


The man left.  

The lights dimmed.  

The hum changed pitch.


The first wave hit.


It wasn’t pain.  

Not in the normal sense.  

It was *disorientation*—  

Subtle enough to feel like dizziness,  

Calibrated enough to prevent clear thought.


A lesser man would panic.  

A tired man would break.  

A fractured man would bargain.


Reeves breathed through it, jaw tight.


He’d been trained for worse.  

He’d been through worse.


And he still had something they couldn’t touch:


Choice.



Hours passed.  

Or minutes.  

Or days.


Time became a thin thread stretched between moments of clarity.


Sometimes the man returned.


Sometimes he didn’t.


Sometimes Reeves heard voices.  

Sometimes he heard nothing at all.


But he never spoke.


He had made his peace long ago with the fact that he could be broken physically.  

Everybody could.  

Bodies weren’t built to endure forever.


But the mind—  

The mind was his fortress.


And he had built it stone by stone,  

Year by year,  

Failure by failure,  

Loss by loss.


Nothing they did could move it.


Nothing they did could reach the center of him.


“General,” the man said.  

“No one is coming for you.”


Reeves didn’t answer.


The man tapped a control pad.


The restraints snapped tighter.  

A micro-current pulsed through his arms and chest—


Not pain.  

A *disruption*.  

Scrambling nerves.  

Jamming motor signals.


Reeves gritted his teeth as his vision blurred.  

His heart rate spiked, then flattened again.


“You can end this,” the man said simply.


Reeves let out a slow breath.


“Say what you mean.”


“Tell us where she is.”


Reeves chuckled, a low sound pulled from deep in his chest.


“You think Sarah Kade needs me to protect her?”


“We think she needs to be found.”


“She’s smarter than you. Stronger than you. And she has more spine than your entire chain of command.”


The man didn’t rise to it.


He pressed another control.


Reeves’ muscles seized, then released.  

His vision sharpened painfully.


“You don’t break,” the man said.  

“Impressive. Rare. But everyone breaks.”


Reeves lifted his head.


“I don’t.”


“This process continues until you do.”


Reeves laughed again — this time louder, fiercer, unhinged but in control.


“You fools really don’t get it.”


The man paused.


Reeves inhaled through gritted teeth and forced himself upright against the restraints.


“I have nothing to lose.”


The man tilted his head.


Reeves’ voice rose — not shouting, not panicked — but **primordial**, a sound scraped from bone and duty and decades of buried pain.


“I have NOTHING to lose but my integrity!”


The man stepped back.


Reeves strained against the restraints until the metal bowed.


“You can take flesh.”  

He yanked one arm forward another inch.  

“You can take breath.”  

Another inch.  

“You can take blood.”


He leaned forward, face inches from the man’s.


“But the one thing you CAN’T take from me—”  

His voice dropped to a deep, resonant growl.  

“—is the part of me that decides who I am.”


The man didn’t flinch.


But he stepped backward.


Reeves saw it.


Victory.


Even if it was small.


Even if it would cost him later.


Reeves leaned back, breathing hard.


“Integrity,” he said, quieter now.  

“The only thing that’s still mine.”


The lights overhead flickered as the cycle shifted.


The man retreated toward the door.


“We’ll see,” he said softly.


The door slid shut.


Reeves slumped forward, drenched in sweat, vision swimming.


He had no idea that elsewhere in the facility,  

A silent alarm had been triggered.


He had no idea that Sarah and the volunteers were preparing to move.


He had no idea that this moment —  

The last, furious declaration of a man with nothing left to lose —  

Was the reason they believed they saved him **in time**.


He had no idea that the worst was still coming.


For now, there was only the cold room.  

The restraints.  

The hum.  

And his breath—


Ragged  

But his.
