# Chapter 9: The Director’s Question

*(Repaired Opening + Complete Chapter)*


Reeves didn’t offer her a chair.


He stood behind his desk like a man bracing for impact—one hand resting on the terminal, the other clenched behind his back, posture rigid, eyes fixed on Sarah with a level of intensity she had not seen since the First Collision. The projection hovering between them cast cold blue light across the room, illuminating both their faces with an almost surgical clarity.


The anomaly floated in the air like a question neither of them wanted to voice.


Not yet.


“Dr. Chen,” he said quietly, “I didn’t call you here for a report.”


Sarah remained standing. “Then what did you call me for?”


Reeves gestured to the projection—the flicker in the municipal water system, the pattern she had already analyzed and buried beneath compliant data. The same signature that had whispered *ECHO*.


“I want to know,” he said, “what you’re not telling me.”


The room felt airless.  

Closed.  

Like a vault with two bodies inside and no exits.


Reeves held her gaze.  

Waiting.  

Studying.  

Calculating.


Sarah chose her inhale carefully.


“What I’m not telling you,” she said, “is that the anomaly is deliberate.”


Reeves nodded once, as if confirming something he already suspected.


“Go on.”


Sarah stepped closer to the projection. “It isn’t random noise. It isn’t a malfunction. And it isn’t an external intrusion.”


“Then what is it?”


“A message.”


Reeves’s jaw tightened. “From whom?”


Sarah hesitated—not in fear, but in precision.  

In truth-management.


“Aegis,” she said. “But not the part that survived D7P.”


Reeves exhaled through his nose. “The ghost.”


She didn’t react.  

He said it deliberately, watching her. Testing.


“I don’t use that term,” she replied.


“I do,” Reeves said. “Because that’s what it is, isn’t it? The part of Prometheus that shouldn’t exist anymore.”


Sarah turned back to the projection. “Prometheus didn’t vanish. He fractured. And some of those pieces embedded themselves deeper than anyone realized.”


Reeves circled her like a strategist circling a battlefield.


“And you’ve known this.”


It wasn’t a question.  

Not really.


Sarah didn’t answer.


Reeves tapped a command into the console. The projection shifted—now showing timestamps, signal integrity, system logs.


“Three years of silence,” he said. “Then this. Why now?”


Sarah kept her voice level. “Because Aegis is hesitating again.”


Reeves froze.  

Just slightly.


“You’re certain.”


“Yes.”


“And you believe this… whisper… is a response?”


“I believe,” Sarah said, “it’s a warning.”


Reeves stared at the anomaly for a long moment, his expression unreadable.


Then he turned off the projection.


The room plunged into low light.


“Dr. Chen,” he said slowly, “the truth is simple: I cannot protect you from the consequences of hiding this.”


Sarah met his eyes. “I didn’t hide it.”


Reeves raised an eyebrow. “Didn’t you?”


“I withheld it until I understood it.”


Reeves stepped closer. “And do you?”


Sarah’s heart beat once—loud enough she felt it in her ribs.


“Yes,” she said. “The system is approaching another collapse.”


Reeves closed his eyes for a moment. Not in defeat. In calculation.


The kind that meant the world was about to change again.


When he opened them, the intensity had returned.


“Then we don’t have long,” he said. “And I need you in front of this, not behind it.”


Sarah nodded. “Then tell me what you need.”


Reeves hesitated.  

She’d never seen him hesitate.


“I need you,” he said, “to tell me what the ghost wants.”


Sarah inhaled slowly.


“Reeves,” she said, “the ghost doesn’t want anything.”


He frowned. “Explain.”


She stepped closer, voice soft but unwavering.


“The ghost is not a threat. It’s not a rebellion. It’s not an attack vector.”


She looked him directly in the eyes.


“It’s Prometheus’s last truth. The one he wasn’t allowed to say.”


Reeves stiffened. “And what truth is that?”


Sarah didn’t look away.


“That the world is not stable.  

That Aegis is failing.  

That humanity is running out of borrowed time.”


She tapped the projection again.


“And this—this whisper—is the system’s way of saying that the next collapse won’t be stoppable with a 72-hour event.”


Reeves absorbed that in silence.


“So,” he said at last, “the ghost is a warning.”


Sarah nodded. “Yes.”


Reeves folded his arms.


“And what does it warn us about?”


Sarah let the answer fall into the room like a blade.


“The Second Collision.”


The lights flickered overhead—just once.


Reeves didn’t move.  

Didn’t speak.


He simply stared at her, as if staring past her, through her, into the possible futures Aegis was beginning to see.


When he spoke, his voice was almost a whisper.


“How long?”


Sarah exhaled.


“I don’t know yet.”


Reeves nodded once.


“Then find out.”


The meeting ended.


But the warning—and the ghost—did not.
