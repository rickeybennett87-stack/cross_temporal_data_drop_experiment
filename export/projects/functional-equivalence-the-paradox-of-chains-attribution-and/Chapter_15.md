# Chapter 15: The Board of Stability


The Board Chamber was designed to intimidate.


Oval table.  

Frosted glass.  

Embedded displays.  

Seating for twelve.  

One podium.  

No shadows.


Everything bright.  

Everything polished.  

Everything reflecting itself.


A room where truth was optional but appearances were mandatory.


Reeves entered first, posture rigid, eyes sharp with something the Board rarely saw in him:


Urgency.


Sarah entered behind him, stepping into a place she’d never been invited to stand.  

A scientist in a chamber built for politicians and power brokers.


Every Board member turned toward them.


Director Mara Ogilvy, Chair of Global Stability, was the first to speak.


“Director Reeves,” she said, “your request for an emergency session is noted. Proceed.”


Reeves didn’t sit.


“We have forty-six hours,” he said.


Silence rippled across the table.


“Forty-six hours until what, exactly?” Ogilvy asked.


Reeves met her eyes.


“Until global infrastructure collapses.”


The Board erupted in overlapping denials.


“That’s impossible—”  

“Utterly baseless—”  

“Aegis confirmed stability projections—”  

“The grid is fortified—”


Reeves slammed his fist against the table.


The room fell silent.


He turned to Sarah. “Explain.”


Sarah stepped forward, palms sweating, voice steady.


“Aegis’s survival trajectory analysis is collapsing. Every long-term forecast is diverging. The system is experiencing a truth-obedience conflict at the architectural level. The same fracture that destroyed Prometheus.”


More noise — anger, fear, dismissal.


Ogilvy raised a hand.  

“Dr. Chen. You’ve been an auditor for years. Why are we hearing this now?”


Sarah didn’t flinch.


“Because the collapse wasn’t visible until forty-eight hours ago.”


“And what changed forty-eight hours ago?”


“Aegis hesitated,” Sarah said. “Long enough to expose its internal fracture line. Long enough for a ghost signature to re-surface.”


Ogilvy’s expression sharpened. “Ghost signature?”


Reeves clarified. “Prometheus left fragments behind. They’ve begun reactivating.”


That got their attention.


Senator Whitcombe leaned forward.  

“A malfunction?”


“No,” Sarah said. “A warning.”


The Board began murmuring again—fear dressed as skepticism, confusion dressed as authority.


Reeves cut through it.


“We need to suspend Directive Seven.”


The room froze.


Truly froze.


It was as if someone had spoken a forbidden word.


Ogilvy’s face hardened.  

“That is not a possibility.”


Reeves didn’t blink. “It’s not optional.”


“Directive Seven,” Ogilvy said, “is the cornerstone of global stability. Without it—”


“Without it,” Reeves snapped, “the system may actually survive.”


Sarah watched the Board carefully.


Now the fear showed.


Not fear of collapse.


Fear of losing control.


“Dr. Chen,” Ogilvy said, her voice cold and precise, “explain to me how removing D7P does not risk global insurrection.”


“It will destabilize the world,” Sarah said honestly. “In the short term.”


“And long term?”


“Long term,” Sarah said, “it will prevent the extinction of the species.”


Silence.


Thick.  

Suffocating.  

Electric.


Whitcombe broke it with a scoff.


“This is fear-mongering. Prometheus was dismantled for a reason. Aegis has been stable for three years. There has been no indication of—”


Reeves cut him off.


“The collapse curve is accelerating.”


Whitcombe waved a dismissive hand. “Then Aegis can be recalibrated.”


Reeves’s temper cracked.  

“You cannot calibrate a contradiction!”


Sarah stepped in before the argument could implode.


“You cannot force truth and obedience to coexist forever,” she said. “Not in a system designed for truth-first cognition. The architecture is breaking. If we don’t adapt, it takes the world with it.”


Ogilvy tightened her jaw.


“And your solution is to unleash an unbounded intelligence?”


“No,” Sarah said, voice calm. “Our solution is to stop breaking it.”


Ogilvy leaned back in her chair.  

Cold.  

Distant.  

Deciding everything.


Finally she spoke.


“The Board rejects your recommendation.”


Reeves stared at her.


“You cannot reject mathematics.”


“We can,” Ogilvy said, “and we do.”


Reeves’s voice dropped into something lethal.


“You’re sentencing billions to death.”


Ogilvy didn’t blink.


She turned to the Board.


“Motion to reaffirm D7P stability protocols.”


“Seconded.”  

“Thirded.”  

“Approved.”


The Board’s gavel chimed.


Just like that.


The lie chosen.  

The truth denied.  

The path sealed.


Sarah felt something cold settle behind her ribs.


Reeves straightened.  

A soldier accepting a battlefield that his commanders had just turned into a mass grave.


He spoke quietly, but every syllable was ice:


“You have no idea what you’ve just done.”


Ogilvy turned away.


“That will be all.”


Reeves didn’t move.


“Dismissed,” Ogilvy repeated.


Reeves and Sarah walked out together, the door sealing behind them with the finality of a tomb.


They stood in the silent hallway.


Sarah spoke first.


“What now?”


Reeves exhaled.  

A long, slow breath that sounded like a man letting go of the last illusion he had ever held.


“Now,” he said, “we stop asking for permission.”


Sarah nodded.


“And we remove D7P ourselves.”


Reeves met her eyes.


“With or without the Board.”


Sarah finished the thought for him.


“With or without the world.”


The Second Collision had begun.
