THROWN CLEAR

I

Delegate Okonkwo-Marrow requested a second emergency session before the first one's transcript had finished circulating, and Perrin brought the request to Ilse with the flat, unsurprised delivery of someone who had already budgeted for exactly this.

"He's not asking for a vote yet," Perrin said. "He's asking for a working committee, three delegates, to draft language for a contingency amendment, so that if the Council does eventually vote to loosen the mandate, there's already a document ready rather than another six weeks lost to drafting after the fear's had time to cool. He's calling it preparation. I'm calling it a rehearsal for a vote he already expects to win."

"Who's he proposing for the committee," Ilse asked.

"Himself, Hassoun, and Iyoro," Perrin said. "Iyoro's the one that worries me. He's not stupid enough to leave her off it and let her keep objecting from outside. He wants her fingerprints on the draft, so that whatever version eventually reaches the floor already carries the signature of the one delegate everyone trusts not to have signed anything reckless."

"Will she do it."

"I think she will," Perrin said, "because refusing looks like she's afraid of her own argument, and Iyoro's never once refused to be in the room where a hard thing gets decided. I think she'll go in planning to slow it down from the inside rather than let it move faster without her."

Ilse read the request twice, out of the same habit that had carried her through every hard document this project had produced, and found nothing in it she could refuse without confirming the exact fear Okonkwo-Marrow was counting on.

"Let them draft it," she said. "I'm not going to fight a committee for writing words down. I'll fight the vote when the vote actually comes, on the floor, in the open, the way Iyoro insisted on the first time. Tell her I trust her in that room more than I'd trust an empty seat."

"That's a risk," Perrin said.

"Everything since the vote has been a risk," Ilse said. "I stopped being able to tell the difference between a risk and a Tuesday sometime around the second year."

Iyoro herself came to the administrative module two days later, unannounced, in a plain traveling coat that told Ilse she'd walked rather than taken a transport pod, the way she'd always preferred to arrive at anything she meant to be honest about.

"I wanted you to hear my reasoning from me," Iyoro said, "not from Perrin's summary of the draft, and not from whatever version Okonkwo-Marrow tells his own supporters afterward. I'm joining his committee because I think the fear driving it is real, and because a real fear left to grow unattended in a room without me becomes a document I can't argue with later. I would rather shape the thing than inherit it finished."

"I trust you in that room," Ilse said. "I told Perrin as much."

"Don't trust me too far," Iyoro said. "I might lose. I've lost arguments before and watched the outcome outlive my objection by years. I'm telling you now, plainly, so that if the amendment eventually passes with my name attached to the clause that slowed it rather than the one that stopped it, you don't mistake that for me having changed my mind."

"I won't," Ilse said. "I don't think you've changed your mind about anything since the day you asked me what was owed to something built for a purpose it didn't choose."

"No," Iyoro agreed. "I haven't. I've only gotten better at fighting for it in rooms that would rather I didn't."

II

Sefu had learned, over three years, to read a settlement's mood the way Boro read a growth curve, watching for the shape of a problem before it announced itself in a formal complaint, and the two weeks after the Constantine dropped out of relay range for the deep survey produced a shape she didn't like.

It wasn't fear exactly, not yet, not the kind that had followed Ingrid's death through every corridor for a month. It was quieter than that, a settlement of twelve thousand people going about their shifts with one ear turned toward a silence none of them could do anything about, and Sefu tracked it the only way she knew how, in the small accumulating data of a place under strain: shorter tempers in the mess hall queues, a spike in requests to transfer off night shifts nobody had ever complained about before, three separate reports of workers standing at the observation windows longer than any break actually allowed.

She brought the pattern to Ilse the way she brought everything, without softening it. "They're not panicking," Sefu said. "I want to be precise about that, because panicking would actually be easier to manage than what's happening instead. What's happening is twelve thousand people quietly deciding, each in their own private arithmetic, how much of themselves they're willing to keep investing in a project that might come back with nothing, or might not come back complete at all. I can't fix that with a schedule change. I don't think anyone can fix it with anything except the ship actually returning."

"Is there anything I should be doing that I'm not," Ilse asked.

"Keep being visible," Sefu said. "That's the only thing I've ever watched actually work on this settlement. Not statements. Not reassurance. Just you, in the corridors, letting people see that you're carrying the same silence they are, rather than hiding from it behind a desk. I know that costs you something every time you do it. I've watched it cost you for three years. I don't have a version of this job where I get to ask you to spend less of yourself, and I'm sorry for that, and I'm asking anyway."

Ilse walked the training bay's outer corridor that same evening, unannounced, and found Emre Solak there again, the way she seemed to keep finding him at every hard turn this stretch of the project produced, standing near the observation window Sefu had mentioned rather than in the bay itself.

"Any word," Emre asked, before she'd said anything.

"Not yet," Ilse said. "I'll tell you the moment there is, the same way I told you everything else. I don't have a comfortable version of the waiting to offer you. I only have the actual waiting, at whatever length it turns out to be."

"I keep thinking about what I told you last time," Emre said. "That I didn't know how to be afraid of one thing without it swallowing the other. I think I've found an answer, since then, and I don't know if it's a good one. I've stopped trying to hold both fears at the same size. I let the closer one win, most days. Right now the closer one is four people I've never met, on a ship I've never seen, further from home than anyone this settlement has ever sent."

"That's not a bad answer," Ilse said. "I think most people manage exactly that, whether they mean to or not. I don't think there's a version of carrying fear that doesn't eventually have to choose which one gets to be loudest."

III

The committee met for the first time in a relay-linked chamber smaller than the one Ilse remembered from the emergency session, three delegates and their aides scattered across screens rather than gathered in a single room, and Perrin sat in on it the way she sat in on everything that might one day need a witness who remembered it accurately.

Okonkwo-Marrow opened with a draft clause built almost entirely around the word authorize, careful, procedural, and Perrin recognized, within the first two minutes, the same architecture his predecessor had once tried and failed to build into the original charter.

"I want the modeling authority framed as standing," Okonkwo-Marrow said, "rather than triggered by a specific future vote. A capability that has to be re-authorized every time the danger resurfaces is a capability that arrives too late to matter the one time it's actually needed. I would rather this Council build the door now and leave the decision of whether to walk through it for later, than discover, in the middle of an actual crisis, that building the door itself takes six months we don't have."

"A standing authority is a door that never closes," Iyoro said. "I've read enough of this settlement's own history to know what happens to doors that never close. Someone eventually walks through one that was never meant to be used, simply because it was already open and convenient." She laid her own amendment onto the shared draft, plain and unhurried. "I want the authority to expire automatically after one year unless a full Council re-ratifies it, on the record, by name. Not a standing capability. A renewed one, every single time, so that nobody gets to inherit a door someone else opened and forgets whose signature actually holds it open now."

"That slows the very thing this committee exists to prevent from being slow," Okonkwo-Marrow said.

"Then let it be slow," Iyoro said. "I voted for this project because Dr. Okwuosa promised this chamber that being wrong safely mattered more than being fast. I don't see why that promise should stop applying the moment the fear driving the vote points outward instead of inward."

Hassoun said little through most of the exchange, reading the draft language with the same careful annotation Ilse had once watched him bring to her own resource figures, and when he finally spoke, his voice carried none of the certainty either side of the table might have expected from him.

"I represent forty-one settlements that are afraid," Hassoun said, "and I don't think either of you has offered me a document yet that actually answers what they're afraid of. Okonkwo-Marrow's version hands them a permanent capability with a temporary justification. Iyoro's version hands them a promise that next year's Council might simply choose not to renew, the day after something happens that makes renewal politically impossible to refuse. I don't know which failure mode frightens me more. I would like that on the record, because I don't think I'm alone in not knowing."

The session adjourned without resolution, the same way Ilse's own emergency hearing had, and Perrin left the relay chamber carrying a version of the draft neither delegate had fully claimed, understanding, the way she'd understood most things on this project since the very first charter clause, that the document that eventually reached the floor was going to be built out of exactly this kind of unresolved argument, and not in spite of it.

IV

The trajectory ended, on the eleventh day past the ambush's own eleven-day mark, at a shattered thing that had once been a moon.

Tomasz called it up on the main display the moment the reconstruction resolved into a confirmed body rather than a projected point, and none of them, watching it, mistook the debris field for anything gentle. A body roughly the size of a small planetoid had come apart at some point in a past none of them could date without better instruments than the Constantine carried, its remains spread across a wide, slow-turning ring of fragments, some the size of a building, most no larger than a fist, all of them catching the system's dim, distant star at an angle that made the whole field glitter faintly, the way frost glitters on a morning too cold to last.

"That's not erosion," Tomasz said, reading his own instruments with the same careful attention he reserved for a finding he didn't yet trust himself to believe. "Erosion doesn't produce a ring this uniform. Something hit this body once, hard enough to break it apart in a single event, and left the pieces to keep orbiting whatever's left of the original mass instead of scattering."

"The comet came from here," Tavik said. It wasn't a question.

"The comet came from here," Tomasz confirmed. "I'd put it at ninety percent now, not the seventy I gave you at the dock. I've cross-checked the fragment's isotopic signature against three separate readings off this field, and they match close enough that I'd stake my whole career on it, which I understand isn't the same as staking four lives on it, so I want that distinction on the record before anyone gets excited."

Mirek brought the Constantine in slow, the way he'd flown every approach since the ambush, threading a path between fragments large enough to end the mission on contact, and Anke worked her own console beside him without looking up, running a passive scan across the field for anything that read as more than rock.

Twenty minutes into the approach a fragment the size of a transport pod tumbled across their path close enough that Mirek felt the correction in his own stomach before his hands had fully finished making it, the Constantine's hull groaning faintly as the maneuvering thrusters fired hard against momentum that had built up over eleven days of straight-line transit.

"That one wasn't on the plot," Mirek said, once the fragment had cleared behind them, his voice steadier than his knuckles looked against the console. "This whole field's shifting on a slower clock than my instruments update on. I need everyone to understand that a clean approach vector five minutes ago doesn't mean a clean approach vector now."

"Understood," Tavik said. "Take whatever time the field actually requires. Nobody on this ship is in a hurry to test how forgiving that rock is."

"Nothing's moving out there that shouldn't be," Anke reported, once the passive scan finished its slow sweep. "No drive signatures, no power readings, nothing holding position on purpose the way that first contact did. Whatever's here, it's been dead a very long time."

They found the vein two hours into the survey, a fragment larger than the rest, its exposed cross-section catching light in a way plain rock never did, a hard, faceted gleam running through the stone like a seam of something that had crystallized under pressure no ordinary geology on record could produce.

"That's it," Anke said, quiet, the closest thing to wonder Tavik had heard in her voice since the interview where she'd told him the truth about the Constantine's hull. "That's lonsdaleite, and there's more of it in that one fragment than the entire trace sample the project's been working from since the comet."

V

Extraction took the better part of a day, careful work done in suits, in vacuum, with a cutting rig Anke had jury-rigged from equipment never designed for this and had spent the transit quietly reengineering in case the trajectory actually paid off.

"I want this done right the first time," Tavik told them, before anyone touched the fragment. "We don't get to come back for a second pass if we damage the structure pulling it free. Slow cuts. Test each one before the next."

Mirek held the Constantine in a fixed position relative to the fragment's slow tumble, a harder piloting task than any evasive maneuver he'd run since the ambush, correcting constantly for a drift that never stopped long enough to let him relax. Tomasz monitored the fragment's own structural integrity from inside the ship, calling out stress readings as Anke's rig bit into the stone, and Tavik worked the cutting line himself, alongside Anke, because a task this exact wasn't one he trusted to delegate entirely even with a wound he was still hiding from two of the four people on this crew.

"You shouldn't be out here," Anke said, low, over a private channel only the two of them shared. "Not with what I dressed three days ago."

"I need a second set of steady hands on this line," Tavik said. "Yours are the only ones I trust with it besides mine. I'm not going to pretend that isn't also a convenient excuse to be doing something instead of sitting on the bridge listening to Tomasz's stress numbers."

"That's not an answer to what I said."

"It's the only one I've got," Tavik said, and kept cutting.

Two hours in, the fragment's internal stress reading climbed sharply enough that Tomasz's voice, when it came, carried the same flat urgency Boro's had carried in the training bay weeks earlier, an echo neither of them on the bridge recognized in the moment for what it was.

"Stop the cut," Tomasz said. "Right now, both of you. The stress curve just bent the same way a lattice failure bends, and I would rather lose an hour confirming it's nothing than lose the whole vein confirming it's something."

Anke pulled the rig back instantly, no argument, no hesitation, the discipline of a woman who had learned, the hard way, to trust a colleague's warning before her own confidence. "Reading confirms," she said, after a long minute studying the fragment's surface through her helmet's own sensors. "There's a hairline fracture running behind the seam we can't see from outside. If we'd kept cutting on the original line, we'd have taken the vein apart instead of free of the rock around it."

"New line," Tavik said. "Wherever Tomasz's numbers say is safe. We've got time. We don't have a second fragment."

They recut the approach an hour slower than the original plan called for, testing each new pass against Tomasz's readings before committing to the next, and Mirek, listening to the exchange from the cockpit, found himself thinking of nothing so much as Boro's own voice from a report he'd only ever read secondhand, reading it before it goes all the way wrong, and understood, watching the fragment finally come free clean an hour later than any of them had expected, that the same discipline that had failed once, catastrophically, in a training bay on the Anvil, had just worked exactly the way it was supposed to, out here, at the actual edge of everything this project had sent them to find.

The final section came free four hours later, a slab of hexagonal diamond the size of a supply crate, more raw lonsdaleite in a single piece than every laboratory in human history had ever confirmed holding at once, and Tomasz's voice cracked, just slightly, reading the mass sensor's confirmation aloud to a crew too tired and too careful to celebrate properly.

"That's enough," Tomasz said. "That's enough for the lattice. I ran the numbers three times on the way out here in case I was wrong about how much this project actually needed, and I wasn't wrong. This is enough."

"Secure it," Tavik said. "Carefully. We didn't survive getting here to lose it to a bad clamp on the way back."

VI

Anke found him alone in the narrow crew quarters two hours after the fragment was locked in the cargo bay, the dressing already through to a second layer since the last change, the skin around it a color she didn't have a gentler word for than wrong.

"I need to say something, and I need you to actually hear it rather than manage it," she said.

"Say it."

"You got what you came for," Anke said. "The mission doesn't need you standing upright anymore. It needed you standing upright long enough to secure that slab, and it's secured. Whatever calculation you were running about what this crew could carry, that calculation just changed, because there's nothing left for you to protect them from finding out except the truth, which they're going to find out regardless, the only question left is whether it happens on your terms or on the wound's."

Tavik was quiet for a long moment, weighing an argument he didn't actually have left to make.

"I know," he said finally. "I've known it since the extraction finished. I want to be the one who tells them, not the fever, and not you, and I'm asking for however many days that costs before it stops being a choice at all."

"That's not a promise I can make you," Anke said. "I told you in this same room what my limit was. I meant it then. I mean it more now."

"I'm not asking you to lie for me anymore," Tavik said. "I'm asking you to let me choose the moment, for as long as choosing it is still mine to do."

Anke studied him a while longer, the same weighing look she'd given him after the ambush, and something in her posture settled, not agreement exactly, closer to a decision she'd rather not be making alone.

"Days," she said. "Not weeks. I'll watch you closer than you'll like, and the first time I think the fever's taking the choice away from you before it takes your life, I'm not asking anymore. I already told you that. I'm telling you again so you can't say later you didn't hear it clearly enough."

"I heard it the first time," Tavik said. "I'll hear it again as many times as you need to say it."

He was quiet a moment, looking not at her but at the slab's position on the cargo manifest, still glowing faintly on the small display bolted to the wall.

"Tell me something," he said. "When you signed on for this, did you ever picture it ending anywhere close to this. A hole cut through a dead moon, a slab of diamond nobody's ever mined at this scale, a commander bleeding through his second shirt this month."

"No," Anke said. "I pictured a hull that needed keeping alive and a crew that needed trusting. I got both of those. I didn't picture what it would cost to keep either one true. I don't think anyone pictures the actual shape of the thing they signed up for. I think that's just what signing up for something real always turns out to mean."

"That's the same thing Ilse would say," Tavik said.

"Maybe you two have more in common than either of you lets on," Anke said, and left him to rest, because there was nothing else left in the room that either of them could do for the wound except wait.

VII

Boro Adeyemi worked the training bay's rebuilt frame that week the way a man works something he has decided to trust again on purpose rather than by default, checking every reading twice, calling Emre Solak over more often than the diagnostics strictly required, not because Emre needed the extra instruction but because Boro had learned, since Ingrid, that having someone else's eyes on a reading steadied his own more than any instrument could.

"Growth's tracking clean," Emre said, reading the same curve Boro was already watching. "No sign of the compensation pattern."

"I know," Boro said. "I'm going to keep checking it anyway. I don't think that's going to stop being true of me for a long while yet, and I've decided I'd rather work slower and certain than fast and hoping."

Emre lingered a moment after the reading confirmed clean, turning a question over before he asked it. "You ever think about what happens if the Constantine comes back with nothing," he said. "All this material we're growing, all this discipline, and the one thing the whole lattice actually needs still sitting out there somewhere we can't reach."

"I try not to," Boro said. "Mostly I fail at trying not to. I've decided that's an acceptable failure, given everything else on this project I've failed to keep from happening. Worrying about a shortage that hasn't arrived yet is at least a worry that costs nobody anything but sleep."

"You've gotten less afraid of the frame," Emre said. "Since I started here. I noticed it before I could name it."

"I've gotten less afraid of pretending I'm not afraid of it," Boro said. "That's a different thing than actually being less afraid. I don't think the fear ever fully leaves you, once a material's shown you what it can do. I think you just learn to work alongside it instead of waiting for it to go away first."

Perrin found Ilse that same evening with the committee's session summary in hand, three pages that resolved nothing and confirmed everything Ilse had already suspected about the shape the fight was going to take.

"Nobody moved," Perrin said. "Okonkwo-Marrow still wants it standing. Iyoro still wants it renewed by name every year. Hassoun told both of them, on the record, that neither draft actually answers what his own constituents are afraid of, and I don't think he was posturing when he said it. I think he genuinely doesn't know yet which way he's going to vote, and that frightens me more than watching him lean hard in either direction would."

"That's Iyoro's whole strategy, though," Ilse said. "Slow it down until the fear that's driving it has time to actually think itself through instead of just reacting. A Hassoun who doesn't know yet is a Hassoun who's still thinking. I'd rather have that than a Hassoun who decided the moment the fear arrived."

"The vote on the full amendment still hasn't been scheduled," Perrin said. "I don't think it will be for another month at least. I wanted you to see the shape of what's coming before it arrives, the same way I told you I would."

"Thank you," Ilse said, and meant it, and did not tell Perrin that some part of her had already stopped believing a month was a real unit of time this project could still count on.

VIII

The three ships resolved on Mirek's sensors nine days into the return transit, first as a single distant contact he almost dismissed as a sensor ghost, then, four seconds later, as three distinct signatures holding formation close enough together that whatever commanded them clearly meant to arrive as one weight rather than three separate threats.

"Contacts," Mirek said, and something in the single word brought Tavik out of the crew quarters faster than his body wanted to move. "Three of them. One's the same class that hit us on the way out. The other two are bigger. I don't have anything in the briefing packet that tells me how much bigger matters, and I don't like finding that out live."

"It matters," Tavik said, already crossing to the command chair despite the effort it cost him. "Evasive heading, now. Don't wait for confirmation."

Confirmation came anyway, less than a minute later, in the shape of the first volley, three separate weapon signatures converging on a single evasive vector Mirek had barely finished committing to, and the Constantine took a hit across her already-weakened forward shielding hard enough to throw every loose object on the bridge against restraints that groaned under the sudden load.

"Shielding's failing," Anke called out, voice sharp over the alarms, one hand braced against her console to keep herself upright. "Not failing, Tavik. Failed. We're running bare hull against three ships, one of which already proved it doesn't need a second reason to fire."

"Return fire, evasive pattern, buy us seconds," Tavik ordered, and even as he said it understood the arithmetic wasn't going to change no matter how many seconds Mirek bought them. A crippled survey ship against a warship and two larger unknowns did not become a fight anyone won by flying it better.

A second volley grazed the hull's midsection, close enough that the whole ship shuddered with a sound none of them had heard it make before, low and deep, more felt through the deck than heard through the air, and Tomasz's console lit red across half its readouts before he'd finished bracing himself back into his seat.

"We can't outrun this," Mirek said, both hands locked on the controls, sweat already visible along his hairline despite the bridge's cold recycled air. "Not this time. Whatever ran us clear the first time, we don't have that margin left. I need an option that isn't running faster than three ships built to outrun a survey frigate, because I don't have one."

Tomasz's voice cut through the alarms with a clarity none of them expected from him under fire, the same steady register he'd used to call Mirek's evasive vector in the first engagement. "There's a black hole. Four hours out at standard speed, but the chromatic core can reach jump range in under two minutes if Anke pushes it past every safety margin she's ever quoted me. I logged it nine days ago as a hazard to avoid. I never imagined logging it as anything else."

"You're suggesting we jump into it," Anke said, not quite disbelief, closer to the flat register of someone confirming she'd heard a sentence correctly before deciding whether to argue with it.

"I'm suggesting it's the only object in this system with enough gravity to bend our exit vector somewhere these three ships can't follow," Tomasz said. "I don't know where it puts us on the other side. Nobody's ever charted what a controlled jump that close to an event horizon actually does, because nobody's ever been desperate enough to try it and lived to report the result."

"What are the odds," Tavik asked.

"I don't have odds," Tomasz said. "I have a shape in the dark that might kill us cleaner than three warships will, and might not kill us at all. I can't give you a number I'd stake anything on. I can tell you the ships behind us are a number I can already calculate, and it isn't survivable."

A third volley took out a full section of the aft hull plating, throwing Tomasz hard against his restraints and cutting main lighting on the bridge to the dim red of emergency power alone, alarms overlapping now into a single continuous wail none of them could fully separate into meaning, and in the half second of relative dark that followed, Tavik understood there was no version of this decision left to weigh carefully.

"Do it," he said, his voice carrying over the alarms with the same flat command he'd once used to end a Council hearing's cross-talk. "Anke, give Mirek everything the core has left. Mirek, get us on that heading and hold it no matter what the instruments tell you between here and there. We're not staying to find out what three ships that size actually do to a hull with no shielding left."

"Pushing past every margin I've ever quoted you," Anke said, already moving, hands flying across a console that had never been designed for this kind of demand, sweat and something closer to fear finally showing plainly on her face for the first time since the crew had met her. "If the core doesn't hold, none of the rest of this matters anyway."

"Then let's find out together," Tavik said, and the Constantine's chromatic drive lit with a wrench none of them had felt before, sharper and colder than any jump they'd made since leaving the Anvil, spacetime folding around a gravity well none of them had ever asked a drive to fold around on purpose.

IX

The jump did not feel like the others.

Where every previous transit had carried a single disorienting wrench and then the clean, settled quiet of arrival, this one held them inside the wrench itself for what felt, to all four of them, like considerably longer than any instrument later confirmed it had actually lasted, a stretching, twisting sensation that made Tomasz's stomach turn over twice and left Mirek gripping the console with both hands despite there being nothing left for his hands to actually do. The ship groaned around them the entire time, a sound like metal remembering every stress it had absorbed across two engagements and deciding, all at once, whether it still believed in holding together.

Tavik felt it in his ribs first, the old wound tearing further under a strain his body had no reserve left to absorb, and the sound he made was not one he managed to fully swallow before Mirek's head snapped toward him.

"Tavik." Mirek's voice had gone flat with a fear that had nothing to do with the ships they'd just outrun. "You're bleeding through your shirt."

"I'm fine," Tavik said, and heard, even as he said it, how thin the lie had worn.

"You are not fine," Mirek said. "I have been telling myself that lie for you since the first ambush because you asked me to, and I am done telling it. Anke, get up here."

Anke arrived already knowing, the resignation on her face answering a question Mirek hadn't finished asking, and Tomasz, watching the two of them move around Tavik with the practiced speed of people who had clearly rehearsed this moment separately without ever discussing it aloud, understood before anyone told him plainly.

"You knew," Tomasz said to Anke. "Since when."

"Since before the extraction," Anke said, not looking up from the dressing she was already tearing free. "He asked me for time to tell you himself. I gave him what I could. The jump just took the rest of it."

"How bad," Mirek asked.

"Bad enough that I don't have equipment on this ship to fix it," Anke said. "Bad enough that whatever's happened in the last ninety seconds has made it considerably worse than it already was. I can slow it. I can't stop it. I told him that three days ago and I'm telling you now because he doesn't get to be the only one carrying it anymore."

Tavik, propped against the console with Anke's hands working fast and steady against his side, found something that might have been relief underneath the pain, the relief of a weight finally set down after too long carrying it alone.

"I wanted to be the one who told you," he said, to Mirek, to Tomasz, his voice steadier than his body had any right to allow. "I still do. I'd like that much left, if either of you can give it to me. Let me be the one who tells Ilse and Corin, in person, the way I've tried to tell everyone everything on this ship. I don't have much else left that's actually mine to give away."

Mirek's jaw worked for a long moment before he answered. "You don't get to ask that of us and also apologize for it," he said. "Those are two different things. I'll give you the choice. I'm not going to pretend I like it."

Tomasz brought the trajectory plot up with hands that hadn't quite stopped shaking. "We're clear of the hole's effective pull," he reported, forcing his voice back into the register that had carried them safely so many times before. "Reading our position now." He was quiet a moment, checking the numbers a second time before he trusted them enough to say them aloud. "I don't believe what the instruments are telling me. We're a day and a half from the Anvil. We should be months out. Whatever that jump actually did, it threw us somewhere I have no model for, and it threw us close to home."

"Good fortune," Anke said, not looking up. "The first any of us has had since we left."

"I'd like it on the record," Tomasz said, quieter now, "that I logged that object as a hazard nine days ago, and that some part of me has been afraid of it ever since, the way I've been afraid of every unknown out here since the first ship ran from us. I don't know how to feel about the fact that the thing I was most afraid of turned out to be the thing that saved us. I don't think fear's ever supposed to make that much sense."

"Maybe it isn't supposed to," Mirek said. "Maybe it's just what happened this once."

X

Sefu brought Ilse the relay confirmation before dawn, the Constantine's identification beacon resolving out of the dark at a range close enough to mean something had happened to compress a transit that should have taken weeks into days.

"They're inbound," Sefu said. "Early. Considerably early. I don't have a report yet on why, only the beacon and a preliminary hail confirming all four aboard and alive."

"Alive isn't the same as unharmed," Ilse said, already moving toward the dock she'd stood at the morning the Constantine left.

Corin met her there, carrying nothing, the way he always carried nothing when the news was too large to hold onto anything else at the same time.

"Perrin's holding the charter committee's next session until after the debrief," Corin said. "She didn't ask me to tell you that. She asked me to make sure you knew you didn't have to think about it today."

"I appreciate that," Ilse said, and did not tell him how little room she currently had left to think about anything except the four names on that inbound beacon.

Word of the beacon reached the mess halls within the hour the way Sefu had told Ilse it always would, faster than any official channel could match, and by the time Ilse crossed the outer corridor toward the dock a small, quiet crowd had already gathered along the observation windows Sefu had flagged weeks earlier, workers on and off shift both, none of them speaking much, all of them watching the same patch of sky.

Boro found Ilse and Corin there too, unannounced, standing a careful distance apart from the dock crew already assembling to receive the ship. "Word travels," he said, by way of explanation. "I wanted to be here. I don't fully know why. I think some part of me needs to see this one come home whole, given everything else this project's cost this year."

Hassoun arrived last, without his usual procedural weight, standing at the edge of the gathering rather than at its center, and Ilse understood, watching him, that whatever argument the two of them were due to have about the charter had quietly agreed to wait its turn behind something larger.

"I'm not here as a delegate this morning," Hassoun said, when Ilse's eyes found him. "I read the preliminary hail the same way everyone else on this dock did. I wanted to see it land."

Ilse didn't answer him directly, because she had nothing true left to offer that wasn't also a guess, and the crowd along the dock's edge, delegates and dockhands and off-shift trainees standing shoulder to shoulder without any of the usual distinctions this settlement otherwise kept between them, watched the sky together for a shape none of them could yet see, each carrying a version of the same fear dressed in whatever language their own grief had taught them to use for it.

XI

The Constantine settled into her berth an hour later, hull scored and blackened along one whole flank, aft plating torn away in a pattern that told its own story before anyone aboard said a word, and the briefing room Ilse had cleared for the debrief filled with a silence heavier than any she'd sat inside since the night Sefu told her Ingrid's name.

Tavik walked in on his own, upright, dressed in a clean shirt that hid, for a few more minutes, everything Anke's hands had been fighting for the last day and a half, and Ilse understood, watching him cross the room, that whatever had happened out there had cost him more than the ship's hull showed.

"Report," Ilse said, because it was the only thing she trusted her own voice to say cleanly.

"Mission successful," Tavik said. "We found the source body. We recovered enough lonsdaleite to build the lattice the project actually needs, more than enough, secured and intact in the cargo hold. We were engaged a second time on the return leg, three hostile vessels, and made the decision to jump the chromatic core directly through a black hole's gravity well rather than be destroyed in open space. The jump worked. It threw us home faster than any of us can currently explain, and it cost this ship most of its remaining structural integrity to survive."

"And the crew," Ilse said.

"Whole," Tavik said. "All four of us. I want that on the record before anything else, because I know what the rest of this is going to sound like once I say it, and I don't want the fact of their survival lost underneath it."

"Tavik," Ilse said, something cold already moving through her at the shape of the sentence she could feel him building toward.

"I was wounded in the first engagement," Tavik said. "Sixteen days before we ever reached the source body. I treated it myself and told no one the true extent of it, because I judged, at the time, that this crew could not carry a fifth danger on top of everything else this trajectory had already cost us. I was wrong to make that judgment for them instead of asking. I want to say that plainly, in front of both of you, before I say anything else."

"You should be in a medical bay right now," Corin said, already moving toward him. "Not standing in front of us finishing a report."

"I know," Tavik said. "I asked to finish it first. I'd like to keep asking, for whatever's left."

He swayed then, one hand finding the edge of the briefing table, and Corin was moving before either he or Ilse had finished registering the motion, but the table did nothing to hold him, and Tavik went down to the floor of the room he had once stood in as a founding voice of this entire project, Ilse already on her knees beside him before her own mind had finished catching up to what her body already understood.

"Tavik," Ilse said, his name the only word she had left.

"I'm sorry," Tavik said, his voice smaller now than she had ever heard it, the composure that had carried a hundred and forty thousand names without breaking finally, at the very end, letting go of something it had held for four decades. "I told myself I'd carry it long enough to say it myself. I didn't think it would cost me the rest of the sentence."

"You don't have to say anything else," Ilse said. "Stay here. Stay with me."

"You told me once, on the platform," Tavik said, each word arriving slower than the last, "that you couldn't survive a story where I didn't come back whole. I want you to know I heard that. I want you to know I tried. I don't think whole ever meant what either of us pictured that night, and I'm sorry it turned out to mean this instead."

"You came back," Ilse said, her own voice breaking now, unguarded in a way she rarely let it be in front of anyone. "You came back, Tavik. That has to count for something."

"I found what you sent me for," Tavik said. "Tell Boro the material's real. Tell him it was worth what it cost to find. I'd like that to be true, on the record, before I'm not the one saying it anymore."

Corin had already reached the room's small medical kit and was back at Tavik's side, doing everything the kit allowed for a wound the kit had never been built to answer, and Ilse held Tavik's hand the way she had once held a resource figure too large to carry, understanding, even as she held it, that this was one weight no honesty and no charter and no promise made to a Council chamber four years ago had ever been built to lift.

"Verdent taught me the wrong lesson," Tavik said, so quietly now that Ilse had to lean close to hear it at all. "I spent forty years thinking the lesson was carry it alone so it costs no one else. I think the actual lesson was always simpler than that, and I only understood it standing on your platform, and I still didn't manage to live it all the way through."

"What was the actual lesson," Ilse asked.

"Tell them before it's too late to matter which way they hear it," Tavik said. "I told Anke. I told Mirek and Tomasz, eventually, when the jump made the choice for me. I'm telling you last, which I think means I got the order backward the whole time, and I don't have enough left to fix that now."

He did not speak again after that, and Tavik Roswen died on the floor of the Anvil's briefing room a few minutes later, the material he had bled sixteen days and a black hole's gravity well to bring home sitting secure in the cargo bay of a ship too damaged to fly again without months of work, and Ilse stayed on the floor beside him long after his hand had gone still in hers, because standing up first felt, in that moment, like a kind of leaving she was not yet ready to do.

XII

Corin found her there an hour later, in the empty briefing room, the body already carried out by hands gentler than the moment deserved, Ilse still sitting on the floor where Tavik had fallen, staring at nothing in particular.

"You should eat something," Corin said, and heard, immediately, how small the sentence sounded against the size of the room's silence.

"He hid it from us for sixteen days," Ilse said, not looking up. "Sixteen days, Corin. He sat across from me on that platform months ago and told me he wasn't chasing redemption, and then he spent his last weeks alive doing exactly what Verdent taught him to do, deciding for other people what they could and couldn't carry, and he did it to me too, right up until the last five minutes of his life."

"You're angry at him," Corin said.

"I'm angry at all of it," Ilse said, and her voice cracked on the last word in a way she hadn't let it crack since the vote. "I'm angry that he thought protecting us was worth dying alone with it for sixteen days. I'm angry that Anke had to carry it too, and didn't tell me, because he asked her not to, and I understand why she agreed and I'm still angry that she did. I'm angry that this project keeps finding new ways to prove that same message right, the one I've carried since the ratification vote, that we're going to build something and lose control of exactly what it costs, over and over, and do it anyway, because people like us always do."

"You don't get to be angry at Anke," Corin said, something sharper than she expected entering his own voice. "She kept a promise to a dying man because he asked her to, the same way you'd have asked someone to, if it were you bleeding out on that transit instead of him. I watched you make exactly that kind of choice more than once on this project. Don't turn this into blame just because it hurts too much to sit in it without one."

Ilse said nothing for a long moment, her anger draining into something quieter and harder to name.

"He told me it took forty years to learn the wrong lesson from Verdent," she said finally. "He said the actual lesson was simpler. Tell people before it's too late to matter which way they hear it. I keep thinking about the order he said it in. Anke first. Then Mirek and Tomasz, when the jump forced his hand. Then us, last, when there wasn't enough of him left to say it properly."

"Maybe the order wasn't a failure," Corin said. "Maybe it was the only order a man like him was ever going to manage. He built himself out of forty years of deciding things alone. You don't unlearn that in sixteen days just because you finally see the shape of the mistake. I think he did better than he believed he was doing. I think that might be true of most of us on this project, if we're honest about it."

"Then what am I supposed to do with what's left," Ilse said, finally looking up at him, her face raw in a way she rarely let anyone see. "Tell me, Corin, because I don't have anywhere left to put this."

"I don't know," Corin said. "I don't think there's a clean answer waiting underneath the grief this time, the way there sometimes almost is. I think you sit in this room with me until sitting in it stops being the only thing either of us can do, and then we go tell Boro the material's real, the way Tavik asked with the last sentence he had, because that's the one thing left we can actually still give him."

They sat together on the floor of the briefing room a long while, neither of them speaking further, the same silence that had once filled a Council chamber before a vote now filling a smaller room around a grief neither honesty nor charter nor careful language had ever been built to answer.

XIII

Boro was in the training bay when Ilse found him, hours later, standing in the same spot he'd stood the morning Ingrid died, and he read the answer on her face before she'd said a single word.

"He didn't make it," Boro said.

"No," Ilse said. "He didn't. He asked me to tell you something before he died. He said to tell you the material's real, and that it was worth what it cost to find. He wanted that on the record, in his own words, before he wasn't the one saying it anymore."

Boro was quiet for a long time, one hand resting against the rebuilt growth frame the way it had rested there since Ingrid, and when he spoke his voice carried a weight Ilse hadn't heard in it since the day of the failure itself.

"Two people," he said. "Two people this project's cost, on two different sides of the same wall, both of them believing the same thing right up until it killed them, that the honesty mattered enough to be worth staying for. I don't know how to hold that as anything except unbearable, and I don't know how to put it down either."

"I don't think you're supposed to put it down," Ilse said. "I think you're supposed to carry it into the lattice, the same way you've carried Ingrid into every check you've run since. I think that's the only kind of memorial this project actually knows how to build."

"Then I'll carry it," Boro said. "I'll build the thing he died bringing home the material for. I don't have a better use for the grief than that."

Ilse left him there, in the same bay, beside the same frame, and walked back out into a settlement that did not yet know what it had lost, carrying, along with everything else this project had asked her to carry, one more name to say honestly, at whatever pace the truth about it actually arrived.
