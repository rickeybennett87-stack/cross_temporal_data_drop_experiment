GROWTH PATTERNS

I

Sixteen days past the Vy'keen sighting, the Constantine had settled into a rhythm none of them spoke about directly, the wariness of a crew who had already learned, once, that the space around them was not as empty as the charts promised.

Tomasz had stopped calling his own confidence intervals out loud after the first week, not because he'd lost faith in the reconstruction but because Tavik had asked him, quietly, to keep the exact numbers between the two of them from here forward. "Morale doesn't need decimal points," Tavik had told him. "It needs to know we're still moving toward something real. Give me the truth. I'll decide what the rest of the crew needs to carry."

Mirek flew the ship the way he'd told Tavik he would, like the debris field had already killed someone he loved, checking sensor returns twice for every course correction, and it was Mirek who caught the first anomaly, a gravitational shadow too regular to be a rock, sitting directly along the trajectory's projected line.

"Something's out there," Mirek said. "Not moving. Could be debris, could be a station, could be nothing. I can't get a clean read at this range."

"Hold approach speed," Tavik said. "Whatever it is, we go in slow enough to change our minds."

Anke had spent the sixteen days since the sighting running a diagnostic sweep of every system the encounter with the fleeing Vy'keen ship had reminded her to worry about, not because anything had actually failed, but because the discipline she'd promised Tavik in her interview months earlier had never included a clause for stopping once the ship was already underway. She found him on the bridge that morning going over Mirek's sensor logs a second time, and set down a tablet of her own findings without being asked.

"Chromatic core's stable," she reported. "Containment field's holding tighter tolerances than I expected from a decade-dormant system, which I'll take as a compliment to my own refit rather than luck. If we take real damage out here, I want it on record now that the core itself should hold. I can't promise the same for anything else on this hull. It's a good ship. It was never actually built for the kind of survey work we're asking of it."

"Noted," Tavik said. "I'd rather have an honest limit than a comfortable guess."

"That's the only kind of answer I know how to give anyone," Anke said, and returned to her station without waiting to see how the words landed, already three steps into her next check before Tavik had finished responding.

The gravitational shadow held its position on Mirek's display through two more sensor sweeps, close enough now that its lack of drift had stopped reading as ambiguous and started reading, to all four of them at once, as deliberate.

"It's not debris," Mirek said finally. "Debris drifts. This has been holding a fixed position relative to the system's own gravity well for as long as I've had a clean read on it. That's not a natural object. That's something maintaining its own position on purpose."

Tomasz had come forward from his own station without being asked, unable to leave an unexplained reading alone the way he'd never once been able to in his whole career, and studied the shadow's outline over Mirek's shoulder. "If it's a station, it's not one on any chart I've ever cross-referenced against this trajectory, and I've cross-referenced every chart I could find before we left. If it's a ship, it's holding a position no drifting hull would hold on its own, which means someone's keeping it there deliberately, which means someone already knows we're coming before we've decided whether we're going any closer."

"You're saying it's watching us," Tavik said.

"I'm saying I can't rule it out, and I'd rather tell you the version that keeps us cautious than the version that lets us relax early," Tomasz said. "I've been wrong about worse things by assuming the calmer read was the honest one. I'm not doing that again."

Tavik felt a familiar tightening in his chest, the same one that had once, decades earlier, preceded every worst day of his career, and did not let it reach his voice. "Then we treat it as occupied," he said, "and we approach it the same way we'd approach anything we don't understand yet. Carefully, and ready to leave the moment careful stops being an option."

II

They did not get the chance to change their minds.

The Constantine cleared the last stretch of dead space at reduced speed, exactly as Tavik had ordered, and the object resolved on sensors into a shape none of them had seen before and all of them, in the half second before the first strike landed, somehow already understood: a warship, alien, angular in a way no human design had ever produced, weapons already tracking before the Constantine's own systems had finished classifying the contact as a threat.

The first hit took out a third of the forward shielding before Mirek had even finished shouting the word contact, no hail preceding it, no warning shot visible to any of them in the half second they had to register one. The unknown vessel fired the way a hand pulls back from something already recognized as an enemy, immediate and total, and Mirek threw the Constantine into an evasive roll a half second after the first impact rocked the hull hard enough to throw Tomasz out of his seat.

"Return fire," Tavik ordered, voice level in a way none of the crew would later be able to explain to themselves, "and get us clear. We are not staying to find out what they think we are."

A half second into the exchange, Anke's board threw up a warning she hadn't seen before, a burst transmission arriving in the same instant as the first strike, dense and compressed and gone almost as fast as it had come. "Getting a transmission," she called out, fingers already moving over the console. "Encoded, whatever it is. Not in anything our translation library recognizes. I can't crack it live, there's no time to try."

"Log it and move on," Tavik said. "Whatever they're saying, they said it after they'd already started shooting. I don't need a translation to know what that means right now."

Anke flagged the burst for the ship's own recorder without another word and turned back to the failing systems in front of her, and the raw, unreadable signal sat there in the Constantine's logs the rest of the engagement, waiting on a translation none of them had time, that day, to go looking for.

Anke's engineering board lit with failures faster than she could read them, hull breaches in two compartments, the chromatic core's containment field fluctuating under stress it had never been designed to sustain mid-combat. "Core's holding," she called out, over the noise, "but I can't promise that past another hit like the last one."

The Constantine returned fire on instinct more than strategy, weapons systems none of them had trained on beyond the mandatory certification, and scored a hit that did nothing visible to the enemy craft's own shielding, a warship built, unlike the Constantine, for exactly this kind of engagement.

"We can't win this," Mirek said, flat, over the alarms. "We can only outrun it."

Tomasz pulled himself back into his seat with blood already running freely from a gash above his eyebrow, one hand pressed to the wound and the other still working his own console, feeding Mirek the cleanest escape vector his shaking instruments could produce. "There's a gap in its firing arc," he called out, voice unsteady but the numbers underneath it precise. "Two seconds, maybe three, if you cut hard to starboard the moment I mark it."

"Mark it," Mirek said.

"Mark," Tomasz said, and Mirek threw the Constantine into a turn tight enough to slam every one of them against their restraints, the enemy's next volley passing close enough that the hull groaned under the near miss's own displaced energy before the gap Tomasz had found actually opened between them.

"Then outrun it," Tavik said, into the half second of relative quiet the turn had bought them. "Full burn, current heading, don't wait for a clean vector."

III

The second hit caught the Constantine broadside as Mirek executed the burn, a glancing strike along the hull's midsection that threw every loose object and every person not strapped down hard against whatever surface was nearest. Tavik went down against the edge of a support strut, a sharp, tearing pain along his lower ribs that he registered, in the compressed half second there was no time to feel anything fully in, as bad but not immediately disabling, and pushed himself back upright before anyone on the bridge had turned to look for him.

"Report," he said, voice steadier than the pain underneath it had any right to let it be.

"Clear of engagement range," Mirek said, reading instruments with hands that hadn't quite stopped shaking. "It's not pursuing. I think we outran its effective range, or it decided we weren't worth chasing into open space."

"Casualties," Tavik said.

"None fatal," Anke reported, running a fast check on herself and the others from her own station. "Tomasz has a head wound, looks worse than it probably is. I've got a burn on my hand from a shorted panel. Structural damage to compartments four and six, hull integrity holding but thin. We got lucky."

"We got lucky," Tavik agreed, and did not mention, in that moment or in the hour that followed, the tearing pain along his ribs that had not faded the way adrenaline usually let pain fade, choosing instead to run his own hand once, briefly, along his side, out of view of the others, and feel the wetness there that told him more than he wanted to know before he'd even looked at it directly.

"You're bleeding," Mirek said, catching sight of Tavik's hand before he could fully hide the motion.

"Scraped it against the strut," Tavik said. "I'm fine. Get Tomasz to the medical bay and check that hull integrity yourself before you worry about a scratch."

Mirek held his gaze a half second longer than the answer strictly required, and then turned to do exactly as told. It was the deference of a crew still too shaken by what had just happened to push past a commander's first answer, and Tavik understood, in the part of his mind still capable of understanding anything beyond the immediate pain, that he was relying on more than he had any right to.

He waited until Mirek had turned fully away before letting his own face show, for one unguarded second, the actual weight of what had just happened along his ribs, and then smoothed it back into the same steady composure he'd worn since the first strike landed. It had nothing to do with courage and everything to do with arithmetic: four people, one working ship, and a decision, made months ago on a platform he still thought about more often than he told anyone, that he would rather carry a wound alone than let it become a fifth thing this crew had to manage on top of a hull that was already asking too much of them.

He walked the length of the bridge afterward on legs that held steadier than he privately expected them to, checking each console in turn, the way he had once walked the length of a Council hearing without letting a hundred and forty thousand names show in his face, and found Tomasz already in the medical bay with Anke pressing a field dressing to his forehead.

"You flew that vector clean," Tavik told him. "Under fire, bleeding, and you still gave Mirek numbers precise enough to actually save us. I want that on the record before either of us forgets to say it once the shock wears off."

"I don't feel like it was clean," Tomasz said. "I feel like I got lucky the math held together at all, with everything happening at once."

"That's what clean actually looks like, from inside it," Tavik said. "Nobody who's ever done something well under fire felt calm doing it. They just kept doing the work anyway. You did the work. That's the only part of tonight I need you to hold onto."

Anke looked up briefly from the dressing, meeting Tavik's eyes for a beat longer than the moment required, the same weighing look she would return to an hour later, and said nothing yet, filing the sight of him standing steadier than the last twenty minutes should have allowed for later consideration rather than immediate challenge.

IV

On the Anvil, sixteen days had passed the way most days passed now, in the accumulating grind of a settlement pushing past twelve thousand people, and Boro Adeyemi stood in the training bay's newest growth chamber watching a section of containment lattice extend itself along a geometry his team had only finalized three weeks earlier.

Ingrid had become, over two years, the closest thing the training bay had to a second instructor, promoted past her original cohort by a combination of steady hands and a patience Boro valued more than raw technical speed in anyone he trusted near an active growth cycle. Three other trainees worked the chamber's outer instrumentation that morning, far enough from the growth frame itself to be clear of any immediate risk, close enough that Boro had trained all of them, Ingrid included, to treat every session as a live exercise rather than a demonstration.

"Growth rate's ahead of projection," Ingrid Bakke said, reading the chamber's instrumentation over his shoulder with the ease of two years spent learning to read this exact kind of readout. "Is that good or is that the thing you told us to watch for."

"Depends on why," Boro said, frowning slightly at a curve on the display that didn't quite match the shape he'd expect from an accelerated but healthy growth cycle. "Ahead of projection because the seed crystal took cleaner than expected, that's good. Ahead of projection because something's forcing the lattice to compensate for a defect we haven't spotted yet, that's the beginning of exactly the failure mode I've spent two years training this crew to catch."

"Which is it," Ingrid asked.

"I don't know yet," Boro said, and pulled up a secondary diagnostic without hurrying, the same unhurried caution he'd carried since telling a room full of trainees, once, that the only honest answer available to him was that they wouldn't know the material well enough to trust it until the geometry was finalized. He had spent every day since trying to make that honesty count for something more than a good line in a training session.

Ingrid stayed close while the diagnostic ran, the way she always did, and glanced back once at the three trainees working the outer instrumentation, a habit Boro had noticed in her months earlier and never asked her to explain. She had appointed herself responsible for people who technically outranked no one and answered to her only because she'd earned it, session by session. "You taught us the phrase for this," she said. "Reading it before it's gone all the way wrong. I always thought that was a way of talking about caution in general. I don't think I understood until right now that you meant an actual moment, a real line you could stand on one side or the other of."

"I meant both," Boro said, not looking away from the display. "Caution in general is what keeps you standing close enough to catch the moment when it comes. You don't get that moment without the general habit underneath it. That's the whole discipline, in one sentence, if you ever need it shorter than the version I gave the room."

The secondary diagnostic took eleven minutes to resolve, and in that eleven minutes, the growth rate climbed further past projection, a curve that had started as merely fast now beginning to bend into a shape Boro had seen only once before, in a simulation run months earlier, the unmistakable signature of a lattice compensating for a microscopic flaw in its own seed geometry by growing faster around it rather than stopping, a structure racing to complete itself around a weakness instead of failing safely at the point where the weakness actually was.

"Clear the chamber," Boro said, voice changing in a way Ingrid had never heard from him before. "Now. Not urgently, just now, calm, but now."

The three trainees at the outer instrumentation moved immediately, the drilled, unhesitating response of people who had run this exact evacuation sequence a dozen times in training without ever once needing it in earnest, and were through the chamber's outer hatch within seconds of the order. Ingrid, closer to the growth frame itself, three meters from the exit rather than the eight the outer stations sat at, turned to follow them, reaching for the tablet she'd been reading from a moment earlier out of the same instinct that made anyone reluctant to leave equipment behind in a drill that had never, until this exact morning, turned out to be anything other than a drill.

"Leave it," Boro said, already moving toward her rather than toward the exit himself. "Ingrid. Leave the tablet. Just go, right now."

V

Tavik treated the wound himself, in the narrow privacy of his own quarters, three hours after the engagement, working with the methodical calm of a man who had learned, over four decades, exactly how to do necessary things his body did not want him to do.

The wound was worse than a scrape, worse than he had let Mirek see, a shard of the strut's shattered housing having opened a gash along his lower ribs deep enough that he suspected, without the equipment to confirm it, that it had nicked something under the muscle that was going to make itself known more insistently in the days ahead. He cleaned it as thoroughly as his own field kit allowed, closed it with the same kit's basic suture strip, and wrapped it tightly enough to control the bleeding without wrapping it so visibly that a shirt wouldn't cover the evidence entirely.

He did not tell himself, doing this, that he was making the right choice. He told himself, the way he had told himself many things across four decades of choices that hadn't felt clean in the moment, that a commander who reported his own wound to a medical bay with no real surgical capability, sixteen days from home and further still from wherever the trajectory actually ended, would accomplish nothing except making three other people carry a fear he could carry alone instead.

He thought, working the suture strip closed with hands that had gone steadier than his own racing pulse had any right to allow, of the eleven candidates he'd turned away before choosing this crew, of the grief answers he'd gone looking for specifically because he trusted them over tactical ones, and understood, with a clarity that only ever arrived in moments like this one, that he was doing to his own crew the exact thing Verdent had once done to him: making a decision about what they could bear without ever actually asking them to bear it themselves. He did not stop working the dressing. He had made his choice long before this exact night, on a platform months ago, telling Ilse he wanted to be the one who went and found something rather than the one who arrived after it was already lost, and a wound he'd chosen not to report was, he told himself, simply the shape that choice had finally taken.

Anke found him an hour later, checking on the crew's condition the way she'd made a habit of doing since the engagement, and studied him for a moment longer than a casual check would have required.

"You're moving like something hurts more than a scrape," she said.

"Ribs took a hit against the strut," Tavik said. "Bruised, not broken. I'd know if it were broken."

"Would you," Anke asked, not quite a challenge, closer to an honest question from someone whose whole professional life had been built on not trusting a system's own self-report when a direct check was available.

"I would," Tavik said, meeting her eyes without flinching from the lie. He had once told a room of grieving delegates the hardest truths of his life without his voice breaking, and could, when the situation required it, hold a much smaller untruth just as steadily. "I need you focused on the hull and the core, Anke, not on me. I'm asking you to trust that I'll tell you if that changes."

Anke held his gaze a moment longer, weighing a reading she couldn't fully verify against the point past which pursuing it stopped being useful, and let it go, the way Tavik had gambled she would, because trusting her commander's word was, in the small and fragile economy of a four-person crew a hostile encounter from home, the only currency any of them actually had left to spend on each other.

VI

The chamber did not clear in time.

Boro would tell Ilse later, in the flat register of a man reciting facts because reciting them was the only thing left he could offer, that eleven minutes had felt like both far too long and nowhere near enough, that he had called the evacuation the moment the diagnostic confirmed what the growth curve had already been telling him, and that Ingrid had been three meters from the chamber's exit, not the farthest person in the room, not the closest, when the lattice section reached the critical mass of its own uncontrolled compensation and released, all at once, the accumulated structural stress of eleven minutes' worth of growth trying to outrace a flaw it could never actually outgrow.

The failure was not an explosion in any way the survivors could later describe cleanly. It was closer to a structure remembering, all at once, every constraint it had been straining against, a section of carbon-monofilament lattice the length of a person's arm shearing loose from its growth frame at a velocity the chamber's safety protocols had never been designed to anticipate, because nobody, in two years of training sessions, had ever produced a failure this fast, this total, from a growth rate that had looked, right up until the final minute, merely ahead of projection rather than catastrophically wrong.

The three trainees who had cleared through the outer hatch heard it rather than saw it, a single sound none of them would later manage to describe the same way twice, and turned back toward the chamber already understanding, before anyone confirmed it aloud, that the sound had cost them something the drilled evacuation sequence had never actually prepared any of them to survive intact.

Ingrid Bakke died in the training bay on a morning that had begun, for her, with nothing more unusual than an early shift and a readout she'd found interesting enough to ask a question about, and Boro Adeyemi, three meters closer to the exit than she had been, would spend the rest of his life, he told Ilse later, unable to fully separate the arithmetic of those three meters from every conversation he'd ever had with a trainee about reading a growth pattern before it went all the way wrong.

The three trainees who reached the chamber first found Boro on his knees beside the wreckage of the growth frame, hands raw from clearing debris he had no training or equipment to be clearing, calling Ingrid's name in a voice none of them recognized as belonging to the same steady instructor who had walked them through evacuation drills a dozen times without once raising it. None of them moved to stop him. None of them knew yet what there was left to say that wouldn't sound, in that moment, like an insult to the eleven minutes he had spent trying to prevent exactly this.

VII

Ilse learned of it the way she had learned every hard thing this project had produced so far, through Sefu, in person, within the hour, because Sefu had made a promise to herself, months earlier, never to let this kind of news reach Ilse secondhand if she could help it.

"Her name was Ingrid Bakke," Sefu said, before Ilse had finished processing the shape of what she was being told. "I want you to hear the name before you hear anything else about how it happened, because I've watched this project describe hard things in careful, structural language before, and I don't want this to become an incident report before it's finished being a person."

Ilse sat with the name for a long moment, and recognized it immediately, the fitter from the training bay, the woman who had asked her the hardest question of the first town session, who had told her, months later in that same training bay, that she'd stay regardless of how long the wait got, because the honesty mattered more to her than the machine finishing.

"How," Ilse asked, and Sefu told her, as much as was known yet, Boro's account, the growth curve, the eleven minutes, the three meters.

"Boro's not speaking to anyone right now," Sefu said. "I don't think that's shock exactly. I think it's the silence of a man doing arithmetic that won't come out any other way no matter how many times he runs it."

Ilse found him an hour later, in the same training bay, sitting on the floor beside the ruined chamber, still enough that he might not have moved since the evacuation call he'd given too late to matter.

"I taught them to read it before it went wrong," Boro said, not looking up. "I told her exactly that, months ago, in her first session. I stood in front of her and told her the truth I actually believed, that watching for the failure was the whole discipline, and then I watched it happen faster than any of my training ever prepared anyone to react to, and I called the evacuation the second I understood what I was looking at, and it wasn't enough, and I don't know what I'm supposed to have done differently, and I don't know how to stop running the eleven minutes back through my head trying to find the place where I could have."

Ilse sat down on the floor beside him, the way she had once sat with a resource figure too large to hold in her head as anything but an abstraction, and did not offer him the comfort of a lie.

"I don't know either," she said. "I don't think there's a version of this where I tell you something that makes the eleven minutes feel different than they actually were. I think the only honest thing I can offer you is that I don't believe you did this. I believe the material did this, the same material we all knew, going in, didn't fully exist yet in any form anyone could fully trust. I told a training bay full of people, months ago, that I didn't have anything to refill their belief with beyond another honest answer. I think this is the day that honesty stops being enough, for you and for a lot of people down there, and I don't have anything better to give you than the truth that I know that, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise to make either of us feel less exposed tonight."

Boro was quiet a long moment, and when he spoke again his voice had lost the flat register it had carried since Ilse arrived, replaced by something rawer underneath it. "She asked me once whether the machine finishing would ever be worth what it cost to get there," he said. "I told her I didn't know, the same answer I've given every hard question anyone's ever asked me on this project. I keep thinking, if I'd had a better answer that day, whether it would have changed anything about this morning. I know it wouldn't have. I can't stop the thought from arriving anyway."

"I've had that same thought about a hundred different conversations since the vote," Ilse said. "I don't think it ever stops arriving. I think you learn to let it arrive without believing it's telling you the truth about what actually happened. The truth is the lattice failed. The truth is you called the evacuation the moment you understood what you were looking at. Those two things happening close enough together to cost her the three meters isn't a failure of anything you said to her, months ago or this morning. It's the unbearable arithmetic of a material we're all still learning, and I won't let you carry it as though it were something else."

She stayed with him until the light in the training bay's overhead fixtures had cycled twice, neither of them speaking further, because there was nothing left to say that either honesty or comfort could actually improve, and left only when Sefu came to tell her the first of Ingrid's cohort had asked to see the chamber, and that someone with actual standing needed to be there when they did.

The three trainees who had cleared the hatch stood together near the entrance when Ilse arrived with Sefu, none of them yet able to look directly at the ruined growth frame, and it was the youngest of them, a young man Ilse didn't recognize by name, who finally asked the question the other two had clearly been carrying since the moment the sound reached them outside.

"Did we do something wrong," he asked. "We cleared when Boro told us to. We didn't look back. Is that why we're the ones standing here and she isn't."

"No," Ilse said, before Sefu could answer, because she understood, looking at him, that this was not a question that could wait for a more careful drafting than the truth itself. "You did exactly what you were trained to do, the moment you were told to do it. The three meters between where you were standing and where she was standing wasn't a test either of you passed or failed. It was where you happened to be when a piece of equipment failed in a way none of us yet understand well enough to have prevented. I need you to hear that as fact, not comfort, because I believe it's true, and because I think you're going to need to believe it's true longer than tonight."

The young man didn't answer, but something in his shoulders eased fractionally, the relief of someone given permission to grieve without also carrying guilt he'd had no real claim to in the first place, and Ilse understood, watching it happen, that this was going to be the shape of the coming weeks: not one conversation but dozens, each of them requiring the same plain refusal to let blame settle where it didn't belong.

VIII

Word reached Hassoun's committee within two days, exactly as fast as news had always traveled across a settlement this size once it started moving at all, and Perrin brought Ilse the committee's first formal response before Ilse had finished drafting her own account of what had happened.

"They're not calling for a shutdown," Perrin said. "Not yet. I want you to understand how close that line actually is, though. Hassoun's own statement to the committee, which I'm quoting directly because I think you need the exact words rather than my summary of them, says the project has now converted an abstract resource argument into an actual human cost, and that his patience for absorbing that cost without a commensurate result is measured in months, not years, going forward."

"He's not wrong," Ilse said.

"No," Perrin agreed. "He isn't. I don't have a way to make this easier for you to hear, so I'm not going to try. I do have one piece of information that might matter to how you handle the next month. Boro's asked to keep working. Not tomorrow, but soon, on his own timeline, which he hasn't specified yet. I think how you respond to that request is going to say more to the rest of the settlement about what kind of project this actually is than anything you say in a formal statement."

Perrin laid a second document on the table between them, drafted in the same careful procedural language she'd once used to translate a proposal into charter clauses, this time to translate a death into the obligations a project like this one now owed. "I've drafted an actual accountability statement," she said. "Not a press release. Something the settlement can read that says plainly what happened, what's being done to determine why, and what changes as a result. I want your name on it, publicly, before Hassoun's committee gets to characterize this for you in whatever language serves them best. You've spent three years being the person who tells the truth first, in the room, before anyone else gets to shape it. I don't think this is the moment to stop."

Ilse read the first page of the statement where it lay, the careful weight of Perrin's language already visible in the opening line, and looked up before finishing it. "You wrote this fast," she said.

"I started drafting it before I finished walking here," Perrin said. "I've learned, on this project, that the gap between an event and the settlement's own account of it is the gap where every rumor Sefu's ever had to chase down actually gets born. I wasn't going to leave that gap open for Hassoun's committee to fill on our behalf, not this time, not with a name attached to it."

"You're protecting the project," Ilse said.

"I'm protecting the truth's chance of arriving first," Perrin said. "Those aren't always the same thing, and I want you to trust that I know the difference, because there may come a day on this project when they pull apart from each other for good, and when that day comes I intend to have already told you which one I chose. Today isn't that day. Today the truth and the project's own survival happen to want the same thing, which is for people to hear what actually happened before they hear a version shaped by someone with a reason to shape it."

"Send it to me tonight," Ilse said. "I'll have it back to you with my changes before morning."

"There's one more thing," Perrin said, "and I want to say it plainly rather than let it sit unspoken the way too much on this project already does. Ingrid's family needs to be found and told, properly, by someone from this project rather than by a settlement registry notice. I don't know who she has. I'll find out. I think it should be you who tells them, if she has anyone close enough to receive it, because I think a name like yours attached to that conversation means something different than a stranger's would, and I think you already know that, and I think you'd rather do it than delegate it."

"I would," Ilse said. "Find out who she has. I'll go myself."

"There's a settlement registry entry," Perrin said. "A brother, listed as next of kin when she signed on. I don't know if they're close. I don't know if he even knows she took this posting rather than something safer back on the outer ring. I'll have an address by tomorrow. I want you to prepare for the possibility that whatever you find him doing when you arrive, it won't have anything to do with the version of this project either of us has spent the last three years describing to a settlement full of strangers. Grief doesn't care how carefully we've built the language around it."

"I know," Ilse said. "I've learned that lesson more than once already. I don't think it gets easier to remember before it happens again."

Aboard the Constantine, sixteen more days out from the ambush, the crew processed what the engagement had actually cost them in the currency that mattered most to the mission itself.

"Hull's holding, core's stable, we're still on the trajectory," Anke reported, in a debrief that had become, since the attack, more clipped and more careful than any of their earlier conversations. "We lost time we can't get back, and I don't fully trust the containment field's long-term integrity after what it absorbed during that engagement, but we can keep going."

"Then we keep going," Tavik said, the wound along his ribs a steady, insistent presence he had learned, over sixteen days, to fold into the background of every decision without letting it change the decision itself. "We didn't come this far to turn back over a hull that's merely damaged rather than destroyed."

Tomasz, his head wound closed and his confidence in his own reconstruction shaken in a way none of his earlier honest disclosures had prepared him for, looked at the trajectory plot a long moment before speaking. "We're closer than we were," he said. "I want that said plainly, the way Tavik's asked all of us to say things plainly since we left. Whatever's waiting at the end of this thread, we're closer to it today than we were yesterday, and I don't know if that's a comfort or the exact reason to be more afraid, but it's the truth, and I'd rather give you the truth than a comfortable version of it."

Mirek said little through the debrief, running his own post-engagement check on the flight systems with the focus of a man who needed his hands occupied more than he needed to speak, and it was only afterward, alone with Tavik in the narrow corridor outside the bridge, that he said the thing he'd apparently been holding since the strut had rocked them both against the same bulkhead.

"I lost a partner to a corridor I trusted because it had been safe a hundred times before," Mirek said. "I told you that in my interview. I didn't tell you the rest of it, which is that afterward, for a long time, I convinced myself I'd have caught it if I'd been paying closer attention, run one more check, trusted my instruments one degree less. It took me years to understand that wasn't true, that some things simply arrive faster than anyone could have caught them. I watched you go down against that strut today and get back up faster than anyone should have, and I don't know what that means yet. I just wanted you to know I noticed, and that I'm not going to stop noticing, whatever you decide to tell me."

Tavik held his gaze for a long moment, weighing how much of the truth a single sentence could safely carry without collapsing the careful architecture he'd built around it. "I hear you," he said finally. "I need you flying this ship, Mirek, more than I need you worrying about me. I'm asking you to trust that if it becomes something you need to worry about, I'll tell you myself, the same way I've tried to tell all of you everything else plainly since we left."

"That's not the same as telling me you're fine," Mirek said.

"No," Tavik agreed. "It isn't. I noticed that too."

Mirek didn't push further, and Tavik understood, watching him turn back toward the bridge with the unhurried care of a man choosing not to spend a friendship's worth of trust on a single conversation, that the not-pushing was its own kind of mercy, one Tavik hadn't earned and intended to repay by surviving long enough to make the lie stop mattering. He stood alone in the corridor a moment longer, one hand pressed flat against the dressing beneath his shirt, and thought of Ilse on the observation platform months ago, telling him she trusted him to come back whole because she couldn't survive the alternative story, and understood that whatever whole meant by the time the Constantine finally turned home, it was not going to mean what either of them had pictured that night.

IX

Ilse stood on the observation platform the night the preliminary expedition report arrived, confirming an engagement, confirming damage, confirming three minor injuries and no fatalities, and read the word minor three times before she let herself feel the complicated relief of a report that told her the people she'd sent out there had come through something violent and were, by every account she had access to, still whole.

She did not yet know what minor was quietly covering for, three weeks and a trajectory's worth of transit still standing between her and the version of this story Tavik would eventually have to tell her in person. She only knew, standing on the platform with Ingrid Bakke's name still sitting fresh and unbearable in her chest, that the danger she had spent so many months of this project's life discussing in the abstract, on this exact platform, with Corin, with Sefu, with Hassoun's own warnings ringing in her ears, had finally, all at once, arrived with a face attached to it on one side of this project and a name she didn't yet know the full truth of on the other.

Corin found her there, later than usual, carrying nothing this time, no tablet, no report, just the exhausted weight of a man who had spent the day trying to comfort an engineer he couldn't actually absolve.

"Boro asked me today whether the lattice itself was ever going to be trustworthy," Corin said. "Not the geometry. Not the growth process in the abstract. Whether the actual physical thing his hands have spent two years growing, will ever stop being capable of doing what it just did. I didn't have an honest yes to give him."

"What did you tell him," Ilse asked.

"I told him the truth," Corin said. "That I don't know yet. That every dead end we've closed on this project closed because we found the actual limit of what a mechanism could do, and that I don't yet know whether this is the limit of the lattice or a flaw in one growth cycle we can identify and correct. I told him I owed him an answer, and that I didn't have one tonight, and that I wasn't going to manufacture one just because he needed comfort more than he needed accuracy."

"That's the same thing I told him," Ilse said. "Word for word, almost."

"I know," Corin said. "I think that's the only thing either of us actually knows how to offer anyone anymore. I used to think that was a strength. Standing here tonight, I'm not entirely sure it still is."

"It's still a strength," Ilse said. "I don't think it stops being one just because tonight is the night it costs something instead of merely risking it. I think the alternative, the version of this project where we started softening things the moment softening them got easier than the truth, would have cost us something worse eventually, even if it never felt as sharp as tonight does. I'm not saying that to make either of us feel better. I don't think there's a version of tonight that feels better. I'm saying it because I still believe it, and I think believing it is the only thing keeping me upright right now."

"I went to see Boro's crew after I left him," Corin said. "The three who cleared the chamber. One of them, a young man I don't think either of us has met properly, asked me whether the project was going to shut the training bay down. Not out of anger. He wanted to know if he'd have a place to go back to tomorrow. I told him yes, because I believe it's true, and because Sefu's already drafting the return schedule, and because I think the worst thing any of us could do right now is let the machinery of this settlement stop moving out of grief, when grief is exactly the thing eleven thousand other people are also going to need somewhere to put tomorrow morning."

"You think reopening it that fast is right," Ilse said.

"I think reopening it at the pace the crew themselves can bear is right," Corin said. "Not faster than that. Not slower either, out of some performance of respect that actually just leaves people standing in a settlement with nothing to do but relive this morning on a loop. I asked Boro directly whether he could stand to walk back into that bay this week. He said he didn't know yet. I told him nobody's going to make him, and that the schedule would wait for him specifically, even if it doesn't wait for anyone else."

Ilse thought of the anonymous message she had carried, unanswered, since the night of the ratification vote, you are going to build something and then discover you cannot control what it becomes, and understood, for the first time with the weight of proof rather than warning, exactly how much of that sentence had already come true, not in the shape of the machine itself, which still didn't exist, but in the shape of everything this project had already cost to get there, measured now in a name she would carry for the rest of her life alongside her sister's.

"I keep thinking about the message," she told Corin, without explaining which one, because she had told him about it once, years ago, and he had never forgotten it. "The one that said I'd build something and lose control of it and do it anyway, because people like me always do. I used to think it was warning me about the machine. I think tonight it was warning me about exactly this instead. Not the thing we're building. Everything it costs, in people, before it's ever finished being built at all."

"You didn't lose control of anything tonight," Corin said. "A material failed. That's not the same as losing control of the project. I need you to hear the difference, because I think you're standing on the edge of blaming yourself for something that was never within anyone's control to prevent, the same way Boro's doing right now, and I don't think this project can afford to lose both of you to that arithmetic in the same week."

"I hear the difference," Ilse said. "I don't know that hearing it and believing it are the same thing tonight. I think I need a little more time before they are."

They stood together a long while, the gas giant fixed in its permanent place above them, the Anvil's lights spread below, one of them dimmer now by exactly one life that had believed, until this morning, that honesty alone might be enough to make a wait like this worth staying for.

"I keep coming back to something Perrin said to me tonight," Ilse said eventually. "That the truth and the project's survival happen to want the same thing right now. I believe her. I just keep waiting to feel less like believing her is a convenience I'm allowing myself because it's easier than the alternative."

"Maybe it is a convenience," Corin said. "I don't think that makes it false. I think most of the true things I've believed on this project have also happened to be the things that let me keep working the next morning. I used to think that made them suspect. I've stopped thinking that. Some days the truth and the reason to keep going are just the same fact, looked at from two directions."

"I hope you're right," Ilse said. "I don't have the certainty left tonight to argue you out of it even if I thought you were wrong."

Below them, the training bay's lights stayed on far longer than the shift schedule called for, a small, stubborn brightness neither of them commented on, and somewhere past every chart this project had ever drawn, a wounded man kept a silence of his own, carrying it the same way Ilse and Corin were carrying theirs, each of them certain, in their own separate distance from one another, that the truth they weren't yet telling was the kindest thing left available to give.
