THE PROBLEM OF MIND

I

Corin Anvegad had learned, in the eleven months since the ratification vote, that a Council charter could survive almost any delay except the kind that had no visible shape, and the mind-architecture team's fourth-floor workroom, three levels below the administrative module Ilse spent most of her days in, had become the place where that kind of delay lived.

The room itself gave no sign of the difficulty it housed. Six workstations arranged in a loose ring around a central display column, cable runs taped flat against the floor rather than buried in it because the budget for proper conduit had gone, like every other line item that quarter, to the training bay's growth frames instead. A single window looked out on the construction yard's floodlit apron, and beyond it, dark now, the shape of the hull sections that would eventually become something nobody in this room had a settled name for yet, only the charter's careful, uncommitted language: the system, the architecture, the project, whichever word happened to fit the sentence being spoken.

"I want to walk through the actual mandate again," Naledi Mokoena said, standing at the room's central display with the patience of someone who had run this same walkthrough for every new hire the project had brought on this year. She was tall in the way that made small rooms feel smaller, and she had a habit, when she was building toward a point, of pacing a short arc in front of the display rather than standing still, as though the argument needed the room the way lungs needed air. "Not the version that made it into the charter's language. The version underneath it. The system has to be able to model a mind, any mind, human or whatever else is out there waiting for us to meet it, well enough that a decision made inside the simulation carries the same weight as a decision made in the world it's simulating. If the modeled version of a person would panic and make a mistake that costs lives, the real version needs to be shown that mistake before it happens, not a sanitized guess at it. That's the entire promise. Honest simulation, not comforting simulation."

"Which means the model can't cheat," Idris Bello said, from his station near the room's far wall, still young enough in his role that he phrased half his contributions as questions even when he wasn't asking one. He was the youngest person in the workroom by a decade, hired straight out of the settlement's last surviving university program before the program itself had been folded into the project for lack of anywhere else to send its graduates, and he still had the habit, six months in, of glancing at Naledi after he spoke, checking his own conclusions against her face before he trusted them. "It can't flatten a person into a set of preferences and call that a mind. It has to actually hold what makes them unpredictable."

"That's the whole difficulty in one sentence," Naledi said. "We've built things that predict people well enough to sell them things or route them around traffic for over a century. Nobody's ever built something that has to be right about a person the way a person is right about themselves, under the exact pressure that would break them, because nobody's ever needed to be. We need to, because Ilse promised a Council chamber that this system would tell the truth even when the truth was uncomfortable, and a model that flinches away from the moment a person actually breaks isn't telling the truth. It's telling a nicer story with the same names attached."

"I keep thinking about the word honest," Idris said. "Everybody on this project says it like it's obviously good. Nobody's asked yet what it costs the people it's honest about."

"Ask it now, then," Naledi said. "You're allowed to have doubts in this room. You're not allowed to have them silently."

"I don't have a doubt exactly," Idris said. "I have a question I don't know how to finish asking. If we build something that can hold a person's actual breaking point, faithfully, who does that belong to afterward? The person it modeled? Us? The Council?"

"Finish it later," Naledi said, not unkindly. "Right now we don't even have the honesty. We're arguing about who should own something we haven't built yet."

Corin sat through the exchange the way she'd sat through a dozen like it since the workroom opened, taking notes she mostly didn't need, because watching Naledi work a problem out loud had become, over the months, one of the few genuinely reliable comforts this project still offered her. She had spent eleven months, once, reconstructing Ilse's own recursive-self-design proof line by line rather than take her word for it, and she brought the same instinct into this room: not a cognition specialist herself, but the person who had learned, across two years of finding the holes in this project's own founding argument, exactly how a room full of people fluent in a problem could talk themselves past the weakest point in it without noticing they'd done it. Ilse had asked her to run this team for precisely that habit, because she wanted someone in the room who would catch the specialists trusting their own fluency over their evidence, the same discipline Ilse had once needed her to bring to her own proof. She had taken the job seriously enough to still be here at hours the charter didn't require of her.

"Show him the Verdent set," Corin said. "If we're going to talk about what honest actually costs, he should see it before he spends another six months theorizing about it."

II

The Verdent set was the project's oldest and most complete archive, three years of survey data, population records, and personal logs recovered from the settlement before the atmospheric-engineering failure that had killed a hundred and forty thousand people, and Naledi handled it the way a surgeon handles a scalpel she has used on someone she loved, competent and unhurried and never once casual.

"We reconstruct individual residents from the archive," she told Idris, calling up a single profile on the display, a woman in her fifties named in the record only as Resident 4471, a horticulturist who had spent the last recorded week of her life arguing, calmly and at length, that the settlement's atmospheric monitors were reading wrong. The record gave her face in the flat, uninflected resolution of an identification photograph, taken years before the failure, a woman with close-cropped gray hair and the deep squint of someone who spent most of her working hours outdoors under a sun that wasn't kind to eyes. "We build the model as faithfully as the data allows, and then we run her forward through the actual failure, the same information she'd have had, the same instruments, the same warnings arriving in the same order, and we see whether the model reacts the way the record says she actually reacted."

"And if it doesn't," Idris asked.

"Then we don't have a model of her," Naledi said. "We have a puppet wearing her name, and puppets don't tell you anything true about the moment they're supposed to be honest about." She initiated the run, and the display filled with a compressed simulation of a woman's last conscious hours, argument, doubt, a decision to trust her own reading of the instruments over the settlement's official channel, the same decision the archive recorded her actually making. The simulated voice that came through the display's audio channel was a reconstruction too, built from four recovered logs and a training-record interview nobody at Verdent had ever intended for archival use, and it carried the horticulturist's actual cadence closely enough that Idris flinched slightly at the first line of it. "There. That's a clean run. Model matches record within acceptable variance. I could show you forty more like it."

"Then it's working," Idris said.

"Watch this instead," Naledi said, and ran Resident 4471 forward a second time, from the identical starting conditions, no changes to the archive, no changes to the model's parameters, not so much as a rounding difference in the numbers feeding the simulation.

The second run diverged within the first two minutes, the modeled woman reaching a different conclusion about the same instrument reading, hesitating where the record showed certainty, ultimately taking an action the actual Resident 4471 had never taken. In the first run she had gone to the monitoring station in person, certain enough of her own reading to override the automated channel entirely. In the second, she hesitated at her own front door, weighing a call to a colleague she trusted against the walk across the settlement, and the record showed no such hesitation ever having happened. Small, almost nothing, the difference between a woman who acted and a woman who paused. It was also, Naledi pointed out, the entire difference between a mind and an average.

"That's not supposed to happen," Idris said, quiet now.

"No," Naledi said. "It's not. Same person, same starting conditions, same everything we can control for, and the model produced two different people. I've run this pair over six hundred times this month. It diverges a different way almost every time, and I can't yet tell you why, because the model has no memory of having been her a moment ago. Each run starts clean. Nothing about the first attempt carries forward into the second, which means, as far as the system itself is concerned, there is no such continuous thing as Resident 4471 at all. There's only ever the current run, briefly wearing her name."

"Show me the range," Idris said. "Not just the two extremes. All six hundred."

Naledi pulled the distribution up instead of arguing with him, a scatter of outcomes clustered loosely around the archive's actual record but spread wide enough, at the tails, that a handful of runs had the horticulturist doing nothing at all, standing at her own window while the failure unfolded around her, a version of the woman the record gave no support for whatsoever.

"That one," Idris said, pointing at the farthest outlier. "That's not a small divergence. That's a different person entirely."

"I know," Naledi said. "That's the one that keeps me up. Because if I can't tell you why the model produced her, I can't promise you it won't produce someone equally wrong the next time we run somebody real, somebody whose reaction actually matters to a decision the Council is about to make."

Idris was quiet for a moment, studying the outlier's frozen last frame, the modeled Resident 4471 standing at her window with her hands loose at her sides, doing nothing while the record's actual woman had been three streets away arguing with a monitoring officer who wouldn't listen to her. "Did you ever meet anyone from Verdent?" he asked. "Before the archive. Someone who could tell you whether the clean runs even feel right, not just match the numbers."

"A handful," Naledi said. "Survivors who'd left the settlement before the failure, mostly. One of them looked at an early clean run of a neighbor of hers and told me it was close enough to make her put the display down and walk out of the room for twenty minutes. I took that as confirmation the model was doing something real. I've thought about it differently since. A model can be close enough to unsettle someone who knew the original and still be wrong in ways neither of them would ever be positioned to catch, because grief isn't a verification method. It's just the thing that makes verification feel unnecessary."

"That sounds like a reason to distrust the clean runs too," Idris said. "Not just the divergent ones."

"It is," Naledi said. "I distrust all of it, some days. I keep working anyway, because the alternative is deciding in advance that the problem can't be solved, and I'm not willing to make that decision for people who haven't voted on it. Ilse asked the Council to trust this project with a promise. I don't get to unilaterally decide the promise was too big before I've actually exhausted what's possible."

She closed the outlier's frame and pulled up the six hundred runs as a single overlapping mass instead, translucent lines stacked one over another until the display looked less like data than like weather, a hundred small variations of the same woman's last week blurred into something that resembled, from a distance, grief rendered as a graph.

"That's what six hundred honest attempts at one person looks like," she said. "I don't have a name for the feeling that gives me. I don't think there's a word yet for watching something try that hard to be someone and come that close, over and over, without ever quite landing."

III

Corin called the full team together three days later, once Naledi's six hundred runs had become nine hundred and the divergence pattern had refused to resolve into anything they could call a bug rather than a wall.

"I want to say the actual sentence out loud, because I think we've all been circling it separately," Corin said. "The system can hold a mind's shape for exactly as long as a single simulation run lasts, and not one instant longer. The moment that run ends, whatever it built evaporates completely. If we want to run the same person forward through a second scenario, tomorrow, or five minutes from now, we're not continuing anyone. We're starting over from an identical seed and hoping the two versions happen to agree."

"They don't agree," Naledi said. "Not reliably. Not even close to reliably enough for anything this project has promised anyone."

"What would it take to fix," a third researcher asked, a systems engineer named Petra Halvorsen who had joined the team two months earlier from the same construction crew that had built the training bay's growth frames. She still spoke like someone more comfortable with load calculations than minds, blunt in a way the rest of the room had come to appreciate rather than resent, because she asked the questions the specialists sometimes talked past on the way to more interesting ones. "Because from where I'm sitting this sounds like a storage problem. You want the thing to remember what it did last time. Bolt on a drive, write the state to it, read it back before the next run starts. I've solved harder problems than that hauling frame components across three kilometers of unfinished corridor."

"I wish it were that," Naledi said, and something in her voice, tired rather than dismissive, kept Petra from pushing back. "We tried something close to that in the first month, before you joined. Writing the model's internal state to storage between runs and loading it back at the start of the next one. It works, technically. The state loads. The problem is that the state isn't a fact the way a coordinate is a fact. It's a live configuration of weighted relationships across an enormous space, and loading it back doesn't reconstitute a person continuing to think. It reconstitutes a snapshot, frozen mid-thought, and then asks that snapshot to resume moving as though no time had passed and nothing about the world asking it to move had changed in the meantime. Sometimes that works close enough to convince you. More often it produces someone who answers like they're catching up on a conversation they weren't actually part of, because they weren't."

"Persistence," Naledi went on. "Some way for the model to carry its own state forward between runs instead of starting cold every time. That sounds simple. It isn't. Every architecture we've tried for it either doesn't scale past a handful of iterations before it breaks down entirely, or scales fine and produces something that isn't recognizably the same mind anymore by the tenth or twentieth pass. We haven't found the version that does both."

Corin looked around the room at people who had spent months on a problem that refused to announce itself the way the settlement's early power crisis had, a single clean epiphany and then an engineering slog toward a known target. This had no known target. It had only the shape of its own absence, a gap where continuity should be and wasn't, and no amount of care seemed able to fill it on its own.

She remembered that earlier crisis clearly enough to trust the comparison: months of the settlement running on rationed output before someone had finally traced the loss to a single misrouted conduit junction, and once traced, the fix itself had taken only days. Engineers had known, from the moment of that discovery forward, exactly what success would look like. Nobody in this room could say the same about persistence. There was no single junction to find, no clean moment where the diagnosis would resolve into an obvious repair. There was only the divergence, patient and total, refusing every explanation anyone offered it.

"Then that's the actual project," Corin said. "Not honest simulation in the abstract. Persistence, specifically, as the thing standing between us and everything else the charter promised. I'd rather name it plainly now than keep discovering it by accident every time a new run diverges."

Petra was quiet for a moment, turning the idea over the way she turned over a load calculation before she trusted it. "So we're not building a mind," she said finally. "We're building the thing a mind needs in order to still be the same mind an hour from now. I didn't think that would be the hard part. I thought the hard part would be the thinking."

"Everyone thinks that," Naledi said. "It's the part every century before ours got to skip, because every mind anyone ever studied came already glued together by something we never had to build. We're the first ones who have to build the glue on purpose."

IV

Naledi's first serious attempt at persistence took another six weeks to build, and Corin watched her spend most of those weeks sleeping less than the project's own safety guidelines would have permitted a construction crew to work under, a fact she raised with her exactly once and never raised again after the answer she got.

"I had a sister at Verdent," Naledi told her, not looking up from the architecture diagram spread across her console. The workroom was empty except for the two of them, well past the hour the rest of the team had gone home, and the display's blue light was the only thing lighting her face. "You won't find her in the archive under that name. I changed it after, for reasons that don't matter to this conversation. I've run her reconstruction more times than I've run anyone else's, and every time the model diverges from what I remember of her, I feel it the way you'd feel a wound reopening on a schedule you don't control. I would like to fix this before I run her again. I don't think I can promise myself I'll stop running her if I don't."

"How many times," Corin asked, because it was the only question that felt honest enough to ask back.

"More than the log says," Naledi said. "I delete some of the runs afterward. Not the data, the record that I ran them. I'm not proud of that. I'm telling you anyway, because you asked me a real question and I'd rather you have the real answer than the one that makes me look more disciplined than I am."

Corin didn't have an answer for that, and didn't try to manufacture one, and let Naledi keep working. She thought, walking back to her own quarters that night, about the difference between a professional obligation to solve a problem and a private one, and understood, not for the first time on this project, that the two rarely traveled in the same person at the same weight, and that Naledi was carrying both at once in a way that would eventually cost her something if nobody found a way to share the load.

The attempt itself carried a simple premise: rather than letting each simulation run start from a fresh seed, feed the model's own prior output back into itself as a starting condition for the next run, a closed loop meant to let whatever the system had built persist by literally handing it forward rather than reconstructing it from the archive each time. Naledi called it mirroring, in the workroom's shorthand, though she was careful, presenting it to the team, to call it by its full technical name first and the shorthand only after, because she didn't want anyone treating a nickname as a guarantee of what the thing actually did.

The first ten iterations held together cleanly, Resident 4471's reconstruction answering consistently across runs for the first time since the project began, and for two days the workroom carried something close to celebration.

"It's holding," Idris said, watching the eleventh run confirm the tenth's behavior almost exactly. "Whatever we did, it's actually holding."

"Don't say that yet," Naledi said, but she was smiling when she said it, the first time Corin had seen her smile in a workroom context in longer than she wanted to count, and even her caution that day had the shape of someone who wanted, badly, to be wrong about needing it.

By the fifteenth iteration, the model's responses had begun drifting in a direction none of the archive data supported, small inconsistencies at first, a preference expressed that contradicted an earlier run, then larger ones, until by the twentieth pass the modeled Resident 4471 bore only a loose resemblance to the woman the archive described, confident about things she had no data to be confident about, certain in a way the real record never showed her being certain about anything. By the twenty-second pass she was describing a conversation with a colleague the archive had no record of, specific enough in its detail, a disagreement over fertilizer ratios in the settlement's western dome, that Idris spent an hour searching the full archive convinced they'd simply missed a source document, before Naledi told him, gently, that there was no document to find, that the model had built the conversation whole from nothing and reported it with the same confidence it reported things that had actually happened.

"It's compounding," Naledi said, running the numbers a third time before she let herself believe them. "Every pass introduces a small error, and instead of correcting toward the archive, the errors are feeding on each other. We didn't build persistence. We built a mirror that keeps reflecting its own reflection, and every reflection loses a little more of what the actual person looked like."

"But it doesn't know that," Idris said. "It sounds exactly as certain on pass twenty-two as it did on pass one."

"That's the part that frightens me most, if I'm honest," Naledi said. "A version of her that hesitated and said it was unsure, I could work with. I could build a confidence threshold, flag the uncertain runs, weight them differently. This doesn't do that. Whatever's compounding compounds smoothly. There's no seam where I could point and say, here, this is where it stopped being sure of true things and started being equally sure of invented ones. It's the same voice, the same cadence, all the way through the drift. If I didn't have the archive to check it against, I would have believed every word of pass twenty-two the same way I believed pass one."

She let the room sit with that for a moment before she went on, because she wanted the shape of it understood before she moved past it. "I keep thinking about what happens outside a controlled test. Here, I have the archive. I can always go back and check a claim against the record and catch the moment it stopped being true. Nobody running this system against a live decision, a real person, a real crisis unfolding in real time, is going to have that luxury. They're going to have whatever the model tells them, delivered in a voice that sounds exactly as certain whether it's remembering or inventing, and no way to tell which from the outside. That's not a bug I can patch quietly and move past. That's the entire danger of building something fluent before we've built something honest."

"Could we flag it some other way," Idris asked. "Even a rough signal. Anything that tells the person on the other end, this claim is load-bearing on twenty passes of accumulated guesswork, treat it accordingly."

"I've tried three versions of exactly that," Naledi said. "Every one of them either flags almost nothing, because the drift doesn't announce itself internally any more than it announces itself externally, or flags everything, because past a certain number of passes the entire output is technically downstream of some earlier invention, and a warning attached to every sentence isn't a warning anymore. It's wallpaper. People stop reading it by the third page." She rubbed at her eyes, the gesture of someone reckoning honestly with a night that had already gone on too long. "I don't have the answer yet. I have a very precise description of the shape of not having it, which some days feels like the only progress available to me."

V

Ilse found Corin on the observation platform two weeks later, later than her usual hour, carrying the stillness Ilse had learned to recognize in a woman who had spent the day failing at something she couldn't yet explain to anyone outside her own workroom.

"You've been down there past midnight three nights this week," Ilse said. She came to stand beside Corin at the rail rather than in front of her, the way Ilse had learned, over the months of this project, tended to make hard conversations easier for people who weren't used to having them with someone in her position. "Sefu mentioned it. I didn't ask her to watch you. She watches everyone, out of habit, and she worries about you specifically, which I think you should know."

"I haven't told you where the mind problem actually stands," Corin said. "I've been giving you status updates that were true without being complete, and I think that's worse than lying, because it's let you believe we're closer than we are."

"Tell me now," Ilse said.

"We can hold a mind's shape for exactly one simulation run," Corin said. "Not one day. Not one conversation carried across two sessions. One single, contained run. The instant it ends, everything the model built about that person is gone, and starting it again produces someone close enough to fool a casual glance and different enough, underneath, that I wouldn't trust it with a decision that mattered. We tried to fix it by feeding the model's own output back into itself. It worked for ten iterations and then began inventing a person who never existed, more confidently with every pass."

Ilse was quiet for a long moment, the gas giant's pale curve fixed in its place above them the way it had been fixed above every hard conversation this project had produced.

"Say the part you're not saying," she said. "You've told me the mechanism. Tell me what it means for the promise."

Corin exhaled slowly before she answered, the kind of breath that came before an admission rather than a report. "It means the version of this system the charter describes doesn't exist yet, and might not exist for a long time, because the version we can currently build forgets everyone it meets the instant it stops looking directly at them. If the Council asked us today to run a real simulation, on a real decision, with real consequences riding on the outcome, I would tell them not to trust a single result past the run it came from. And I don't know yet whether that's a problem we solve in another six months or a wall we spend years learning to work around instead of through."

"I made a promise in that Council chamber," she said. "That this system would ask honestly and be answered honestly. I don't think I understood, when I said it, that the honesty problem would start this far down, underneath the machine's whole personality, in whether it can even hold a single mind steady long enough to be honest about anything."

"Neither did I," Corin said. "I think that's the actual shape of what we're up against. We keep discovering that the promise goes deeper than the last place we found it."

"Do you think it's solvable," Ilse asked.

Corin didn't answer immediately, weighing the honest response against the comforting one the way she'd watched Ilse weigh the same choice a hundred times before, and she found herself thinking, in the pause, of every meeting in which she'd watched Ilse choose the harder answer in front of a room that would have accepted an easier one, and understood that whatever she owed Ilse now, it wasn't a version of that same courage dressed up smaller.

"I don't know," she said finally. "I think that's the true answer, and I think you'd rather have it than a confident one I can't back up."

"I would," Ilse said. "I'd rather have that from you every single time, even when it costs both of us sleep." She looked out at the gas giant a moment longer. "Does Naledi know I'd rather have it that way too? Because I don't think she's sleeping at all, from what Sefu tells me, and I'd like her to know the pressure she's carrying isn't coming from me expecting a miracle. It's coming from somewhere inside her."

"I think she knows the difference," Corin said. "I don't think knowing it helps as much as either of us would like."

"Tell me about her," Ilse said. "Not the architecture. Her. I've read her personnel file, but a file isn't the same thing as someone telling me what they've actually watched a person carry."

Corin considered how much of it was hers to share, and settled on the parts she thought Naledi would forgive her for repeating. "She lost her sister at Verdent. She doesn't talk about it in the workroom, but it's the reason the archive's oldest test subject exists at all, and I think some days it's the only reason she keeps working past the hour anyone would reasonably ask her to. I watched her tell me, in that same workroom, that she deletes some of her own run logs afterward, not the data, just the record that she ran them, because she doesn't trust herself to stop running her sister's reconstruction if the log kept an honest count. She told me that the same way she tells me everything, which is to say plainly, without asking me to feel sorry for her about it."

"That's not a woman who needs protecting from the truth of where the project stands," Ilse said. "That's a woman who needs someone to notice when she's carrying more of it than her share."

"I've been trying," Corin said. "I don't know if trying is the same as succeeding, in her case. She doesn't ask for the kind of help that's easy to offer. She asks for time, and better architecture, and neither of those is something I can hand her directly."

"Then hand her what you can," Ilse said. "Tell her the Council isn't waiting on a miracle. Tell her the eleven months she's already given this project bought us something real, even if it isn't the thing the charter originally promised. I'd rather she hear that from someone who watches her work every day than from me, showing up once to say something that will sound, coming from my position, like a performance of concern rather than an actual one."

"I'll tell her," Corin said. "I don't know that it'll change how many hours she keeps."

"It might not," Ilse said. "Say it anyway. Some things are worth saying even when you already know they won't fix the problem. I learned that from watching Boro, if I'm honest about where I picked it up."

VI

Idris caught the pattern that finally gave the problem a name, three weeks after the mirroring failure, buried in a stretch of diagnostic data nobody else had thought worth a second look.

"I want to show you something," he told Naledi, pulling up a comparison between two failure modes side by side, the original cold-start divergence from the very first test and the compounding drift from the mirroring attempt. "These look like two different problems. I don't think they are. I think they're the same problem wearing two different faces."

"Explain," Naledi said.

"Cold start throws away everything and starts fresh every time, so it never compounds an error, but it also never remembers anything true," Idris said. "The mirror remembers everything, including its own mistakes, and compounds them until the true parts drown. It's like the system can hold onto nothing, or it can hold onto everything indiscriminately, and it has no way to hold onto the right things and let the rest go. A person doesn't work like either of those. You forget what you had for breakfast three weeks ago and you remember exactly what your sister said to you the day she died. The forgetting isn't a flaw in a person's memory. It's half of what makes the remembering mean anything."

Naledi sat with that longer than she usually sat with anything Idris offered, and Corin, watching from across the room, recognized the quiet that meant something real had just landed.

"Say the second half again," she said finally. "The part about what makes the remembering mean anything."

"If you kept everything," Idris said, "every breakfast, every idle thought, every version of every conversation you ever had, weighted the same as the thing your sister said to you, then the thing your sister said stops standing out from the noise around it. It's not that you'd remember more. You'd remember worse, because nothing would be allowed to matter more than anything else. Selective forgetting isn't the opposite of memory. It's the mechanism that makes memory usable at all."

"He's right," Naledi said, finally. "We've been treating memory like a single problem, hold it or don't. It's actually two problems stacked on top of each other. What to keep, and what to let go of, and we haven't built anything that can tell the difference between them. Every architecture we've tried either keeps nothing or keeps everything, because we never gave it a mechanism for choosing."

"Is that buildable," Corin asked.

"I don't know yet," Naledi said. "I know it's the actual problem now, instead of a shape we keep circling without naming. That's worth something, even on a day this discouraging."

Petra, listening from her station, spoke up for the first time in the conversation. "In frame construction, we don't keep every calculation we ever ran either. We keep the ones that changed a decision. Everything else gets discarded once the structure's up, because keeping it wouldn't make the structure any sounder, it would just take up room we need for the next problem." She paused. "I don't know if that translates to a mind. But it sounds like the same shape of choice to me. Keep what changed something. Let the rest go."

"It might translate," Naledi said slowly. "Or it might be a comfortable analogy that falls apart the moment we try to build it. I don't want to trust it just because it's tidy. But I want to try it anyway, because tidy and wrong is still faster to disprove than nothing at all."

"What decides what counts as changing something," Corin asked. "In a frame, I understand it. A calculation either changed a load-bearing choice or it didn't. In a mind, I don't know how you'd draw that line."

"That's the actual difficulty underneath Petra's version of it," Naledi said. "In construction, the thing that changed is visible in the structure afterward. You can point at a beam and say, this held because of that calculation. A mind doesn't leave that kind of visible seam. My sister's death changed how I answer a hundred small questions I'll never consciously connect back to her, and I'd never know to credit it if you asked me to point at the moment it happened, because the influence gets absorbed into everything downstream of it. If we build a filter that only keeps memories with an obvious, traceable effect, we'll throw away most of what actually matters, because most of what shapes a person does it exactly the way I'm describing. Quietly. Untraceably. After the fact."

"Then what do we keep," Idris asked.

"I don't know yet," Naledi said again, and this time there was less discouragement in it than there had been an hour earlier, something closer to the steadiness of a person who has just traded a vague problem for a precise one and finds the precision, even unsolved, easier to work inside of. "But I know the filter can't be built on visible consequence alone. It has to account for things that mattered without ever announcing that they mattered at the time. That's a harder problem than the one I walked in here with this morning. I'd still rather have it than the one I walked in with, because at least now I know what shape the solution has to be, even if I don't have it yet."

Petra nodded slowly, turning the idea over the way she'd turned over her own analogy a few minutes earlier. "In a structure, sometimes a beam that never bears an obvious load turns out to matter anyway, in a storm nobody planned for. We don't always know until the storm comes. Maybe minds aren't so different. Maybe you can't know in advance what was going to matter. You can only know after, once the storm's already tested it."

"That's bleaker than I wanted the afternoon to end on," Naledi said, "but I think you're right, and I'd rather end on something true and bleak than something false and comfortable. That's supposed to be the whole point of this room."

VII

The team ran Resident 4471 one more time before the month closed out, a version of the test neither Corin nor Naledi had proposed out loud until the morning they actually ran it, using every partial fix the workroom had produced so far layered together rather than tested separately, less an experiment than an admission that none of the individual pieces were going to be enough on their own.

Naledi built the combined architecture over four days, sleeping in the workroom itself for two of them rather than walking back to her quarters, a fact Corin noted without commenting on it, because she had learned by now that commenting on it changed nothing except Naledi's mood. The design layered Petra's keep-what-changed-something filter over the mirroring loop, discarding the majority of each run's output before feeding the remainder forward, and added a rough confidence check meant to flag moments where the model's certainty outran what the archive actually supported.

The run held for forty-three iterations before it began drifting, longer than anything the mirror attempt had managed, and Naledi watched the divergence begin with an expression Corin had not seen on her since the day she'd told Corin about her sister.

"Forty-three," she said. "That's real progress. I want to say that plainly, so it's on the record before I say the rest of it." She let the current run finish, the modeled woman's confidence bending, by the fifty-first iteration, into something the archive gave no support for at all, a version of Resident 4471 who spoke with total certainty about having argued the atmospheric monitors' failure to the settlement's full council months before the actual record showed her raising it to anyone at all. "It's still not her. It's a very good, very long-lasting impression of her, and an impression that eventually degrades into something else isn't honesty. It's a more expensive way of lying to ourselves about how close we actually are."

Idris looked between the two of them, young enough still to ask the question the others had stopped voicing out loud. "What happens if we can't solve it? Not this month. Ever."

The room went quiet in a way Corin recognized. Nobody had wanted to be the one to ask that question first, and now it hung there, asked, no longer avoidable.

"Then this system never gets built the way the charter promised," Corin said. "It gets built as something smaller and more honest about its own limits, or it doesn't get built at all, and I've spent eleven months believing neither of those outcomes is acceptable to the people who ratified this project, which means we don't get to stop until we've actually exhausted what's possible, not just what's been easy to try."

"I don't think we've exhausted anything yet," Naledi said. "I think we've found the actual shape of the wall for the first time. That's not nothing, even on a night this tired."

Petra spoke up again, quieter this time. "Forty-three is more than zero," she said. "I've built structures on smaller margins than that and watched them hold for years, because the next fix always taught us something the first one didn't. I'm not saying that to make anyone feel better. I'm saying it because it's true, and I think true is what this room runs on."

Idris looked at the frozen frame of the fifty-first iteration, the modeled Resident 4471 mid-sentence, describing a council meeting that had never taken place with the same unshaken certainty she'd used describing the meeting that had. "Can I ask something that isn't about the architecture," he said.

"Go ahead," Naledi said.

"Earlier this month I asked who a mind like this would belong to, if we ever built it faithfully enough to matter," Idris said. "I want to ask a version of that question about this instead. Resident 4471 didn't consent to any of this. Neither did your sister. The archive gave us permission to use their data, technically, under a Council statute nobody read carefully at the time it was passed, but nobody ever asked the actual people whether they wanted a version of themselves run six hundred times, compared, discarded, run again. I don't think that makes what we're doing wrong. I think it makes it a debt we haven't paid yet, and I'd rather we say that plainly instead of only ever talking about the honesty we owe the Council, as if the people in the archive don't also deserve some version of it."

Naledi was quiet long enough that Corin thought she might not answer at all. "I think about that most nights," she said finally. "More than I think about the architecture, if I'm being honest with you the way I asked you to be honest with me. I don't have a resolution for it. I have a private rule I've never told anyone on this team, which is that I don't run a reconstruction for a test I could run just as well on synthetic data instead. Resident 4471 is different. She's the test case the whole approach was validated against, before either of you joined, and I don't think we can retire her now without losing the baseline every other run gets measured against. But the fact that I have a rule for everyone else and an exception for her should probably tell you something about how unsettled I am underneath the confidence I try to bring into this room."

"It tells me you're taking it seriously," Idris said. "I wasn't trying to accuse you of anything. I wanted it said, once, out loud, so it's part of the record instead of just something we all privately carry and never mention."

"Then it's said," Corin told him. "And it stays part of the record from here forward. Every meeting, if it needs to be. I'd rather carry that weight visibly than pretend the architecture problem is the only one this room is actually solving."

VIII

Corin walked the length of the Anvil's outer corridor alone that night, the way Ilse had once walked it alone after the ratification vote, carrying a version of the same unfamiliar weight, the difference between believing a problem was hard and discovering, precisely, how hard it actually was.

She thought of Resident 4471, run forty-three times faithful and then, on the forty-fourth, someone else entirely, confident about a version of her own last week that had never happened, and understood that the horror of it wasn't that the model had gotten something wrong. Models got things wrong constantly, and correcting for wrongness was the entire discipline of the work. The horror was narrower and colder than that: somewhere between the first faithful iteration and the drifting fifty-first, there had been no moment anyone could point to and say, here, this is where she stopped being herself and became someone else instead. The failure didn't announce itself. It simply accumulated, unnoticed, until the gap between the record and the model had grown too wide to call a resemblance anymore.

She thought about what that meant for a system built, eventually, to hold minds far more complex than a single reconstructed horticulturist, to hold whole populations, whole histories, running continuously rather than in single contained tests, and felt, for the first time since the mess hall pitch three years earlier, something that wasn't quite fear and wasn't quite doubt, closer to the vertigo of a woman who has just understood that the ground she's been building on is further from solid than anyone told her when she agreed to build there.

She thought, too, of Naledi's sister, run more times than the log admitted to, and of the cruelty in a system that could hold a grieving woman's memory faithfully for exactly as long as a single conversation lasted, and then let it go, indifferent, the instant that conversation ended, forcing her to rebuild the same closeness from nothing every time she needed it again. She did not know yet whether that cruelty belonged to the machine or simply to the shape of loss itself, made visible for once instead of merely felt, and she suspected the answer mattered more than she currently had the clarity to work out.

She did not tell Ilse that feeling directly, not that night, because she didn't yet have language honest enough to describe it without sounding like she'd already given up, and giving up wasn't true yet, only frightened, which was a different thing entirely and one she intended to keep separate for as long as the distinction still held.

She passed the corridor's single unshuttered window on her way back, the same construction-yard apron she could see from the workroom itself, floodlit hull sections standing dark and half-finished against the night. Somewhere inside that unfinished shape, months or years from now, something would eventually be asked to hold a mind the way this room had spent months failing to hold one horticulturist's last week. She did not know, standing there, whether the failure they were currently living through would resolve into a fix before that day arrived, or whether it would simply become a limitation the finished system carried quietly, the way every built thing carried the compromises made under the pressure that built it. She found she could not decide which possibility frightened her more: that the problem might never be solved, or that it might be solved so completely that nobody building on top of the solution would ever again have reason to remember how close it had come to failing outright.

Idris's question stayed with her longer than she expected it to, longer even than the forty-three iterations that had briefly looked like progress. She thought about the debt Idris had named, the reconstructed dead who had never consented to being run and rerun in service of a promise made to the living, and understood that the project had inherited two obligations rather than one: an obligation to the Council that had ratified it, and a quieter, unratified obligation to the people whose archived lives made the whole undertaking possible in the first place. Nobody had voted on that second obligation. Nobody could have, since the people it was owed to were beyond asking. That didn't dissolve it. If anything, she thought, it made it heavier, the kind of debt that never got called due and therefore never got the chance to be honestly paid.

The workroom's lights stayed on long after every other window in the administrative wing had gone dark, the same stubborn brightness that had once burned late over Boro's training bay and Ilse's own resource figures, another room on this project full of people who had decided, each in their own register, that the honest answer was worth staying up for even when it refused to arrive.
