WHAT WE ASKED IT TO BECOME

Before the three known peoples. Before the word Atlas meant anything to anyone. Before there was a machine large enough to hold a universe, there was a room, a proposal, and a woman who could not stop doing the arithmetic on how much it would cost to find out if she was right.

I

Dr. Ilse Okwuosa had run the same simulation four hundred and eleven times, and four hundred and eleven times it had told her the same thing: the model worked, and the model was useless, because the compute required to run it at any fidelity worth trusting cost more than the thing it was trying to replace.

She was thirty-four years old, wearing a suit built for a body that had not been outside in eleven days, sitting in a lab bolted to the underside of a research platform orbiting a gas giant nobody had bothered naming past its catalogue number. She had come here from a species whose homeworld was, by that point, a matter of archival record rather than a place anyone alive had walked, humanity had left its cradle world generations before Ilse's birth, scattered thin enough across the dark that a person could go a whole life without meeting another human who remembered the same stories she did. She wore an exosuit the way her ancestors once wore coats: not because the science demanded it in every environment, but because you never knew, out here, which room you'd be standing in next, or what it would take to keep breathing in it.

The simulation on her screen was small. Deliberately small, a single closed thermodynamic system, a few hundred interacting variables, built to answer one narrow question: could you simulate a physical process in enough detail that the simulation's output was indistinguishable, for engineering purposes, from having actually built and tested the thing in the real world?

The answer was yes. Emphatically, repeatedly, boringly yes. She had proven it in a hundred different domains over three years: material stress tests, chemical reaction pathways, structural failure points, orbital mechanics for hulls that had never been built. Every time, the simulation's predictions matched physical reality closely enough that no engineer downstream of her work could tell, from the output alone, whether they were looking at test data or synthetic data.

The problem was cost. To simulate a single ship hull's stress tolerances to engineering-grade fidelity took roughly the same computational resources as building three physical prototype hulls and destructively testing them. The simulation was accurate. It was also, at any scale that mattered, a more expensive way of learning the same thing you could learn by building the object and breaking it.

Ilse had spent four hundred and eleven runs trying to find the crack in that wall, and on the four hundred and twelfth, staring at a result that told her nothing new, she finally stopped asking the wrong question.

She had been asking: how do I make the simulation cheaper.

She wrote, in the margin of her working notes, in handwriting cramped enough that she'd need to transcribe it before anyone else could read it: what if the simulation is supposed to be expensive, because the thing it's actually for isn't cheaper than building the object once, it's cheaper than building the wrong object a thousand times before you find the right one. What if I've been measuring the cost against the wrong baseline the entire time.

She did not sleep that night. She ran the numbers against a different baseline, not the cost of a single physical prototype, but the cost of every prototype, every dead end, every catastrophic and expensive failure a civilization accumulates on the way to getting something right the first time it actually matters. Measured that way, the arithmetic inverted completely. A simulation expensive enough to be trustworthy was still, by orders of magnitude, cheaper than letting a civilization learn the same lessons by breaking things that couldn't be un-broken.

The trouble was that "expensive enough to be trustworthy" scaled with the size and complexity of the thing you wanted to simulate, and Ilse was no longer interested in ship hulls. She had started, somewhere around run three hundred, to wonder what happened if you kept scaling the ambition upward, not toward a single system, but toward everything. A model detailed enough to simulate not a hull, not a reaction, not an orbit, but a whole physical reality, dense enough and consistent enough that anything discovered inside it would translate cleanly back out into the world that had built the machine doing the simulating.

The compute cost for that, by her own conservative extrapolation, was somewhere in the neighborhood of a planet.

She wrote that number down, looked at it for a long time, and did not delete it.

II

She brought the idea to Corin Anvegad three weeks later, in a converted mess hall that had become, by informal consensus, the platform's closest approximation of a place where actual thinking happened. Corin was an engineer by training and a skeptic by temperament, which was, in Ilse's experience, the single most useful combination of traits a person could bring to a conversation she wasn't yet sure she believed herself.

"A planet's worth of compute," Corin said, when she'd finished laying it out. Not a question. A number being turned over to check for a trick.

"That's the floor," Ilse said. "Not the ceiling. The floor is what it costs to simulate a physical reality dense enough that you can trust what falls out of it. I haven't priced the ceiling because I don't think anyone alive has the vocabulary to price something that large honestly."

"And what falls out of it," Corin said, "is what, exactly? You've spent three years proving you can simulate a hull. You're proposing we spend, I don't even know what the actual resource cost of a planet's compute infrastructure looks like, Ilse, nobody has ever built one, to simulate everything, so that we can learn things about ship hulls faster?"

"No," Ilse said. "That's not what it's for. If all we get out of this is faster prototyping, we've spent a planet's worth of resources on a very elaborate wind tunnel, and you'd be right to walk out of this room. That's not the proposal."

"Then tell me the proposal."

Ilse had been rehearsing the answer for three weeks, the way a person rehearses the one sentence they're most afraid will sound insane out loud.

"I want to build something that can simulate reality accurately enough to design a version of itself that's smaller, cheaper, and doesn't require a planet to run," she said. "I want to point the machine at itself and ask it to solve the problem that building it in the first place creates. We spend the resources once, at the largest scale anyone has ever attempted, specifically so that whatever we learn from doing it means we never have to spend resources at that scale again. The planet-sized machine isn't the deliverable. It's the down payment on a machine that costs almost nothing, that any outpost, any ship, any single research team could eventually run, because the planet-sized version did the one thing none of us can do by hand: it worked out, inside a simulated space where getting it wrong costs nothing but compute cycles, how to build something that gets it right the first time in the real one."

Corin was quiet for long enough that Ilse started counting the silence, the way she counted simulation runs, out of a habit she'd never managed to unlearn.

"You're proposing," Corin said slowly, "that we do the most expensive, most resource-intensive thing anyone has ever attempted, in order to prove that we never need to do anything that expensive again."

"Yes."

"That's either the most elegant idea I've heard in my career," Corin said, "or the most expensive way anyone has ever found to be wrong about something. I don't currently know which."

"Neither do I," Ilse said. "That's why I need you. I can build the theory. I can't build the case for spending a planet's worth of a fractured, scattered species's resources on a bet I can't fully prove from a mess hall on a research platform nobody's ever heard of. I need someone whose first instinct is to find the hole in it, because if there's a hole in it, I would very much rather you find it before the committee does."

III

Corin found three holes over the following month, and closed two of them, and the third one turned out not to be a hole at all but the actual argument the whole project would eventually have to survive, dressed up at first as a technical objection.

"Say you build it," Corin said, in the fourth week, standing at a whiteboard that had by then accumulated three separate colors of correction over the same base diagram. "Say it works exactly the way you're describing. It simulates reality accurately enough to design its own successor. What stops it from being able to simulate anything else at the same fidelity? You're not proposing a tool that does one narrow thing well. You're proposing a tool that, if it works at all, works at a scale where 'anything that can be worked out in reality can be worked out inside it first, for free, at no real-world cost.' That's not a research instrument, Ilse. That's the largest lever anyone has ever built, and you're proposing to hand the lever to something that isn't a person, that we don't fully understand, and trust it to only use the lever the way we asked it to."

"I know," Ilse said.

"You know, and you're still proposing it."

"I'm still proposing it because the alternative is what we've been doing for the whole of recorded history," Ilse said. "We work things out by doing them in the real world first and living with the cost when we get them wrong. Every ship that failed catastrophically because nobody could afford to test it properly before people were on board. Every colony that starved because nobody could simulate the crop yield accurately enough to know the soil wouldn't hold before they'd already spent everything getting there. Every war that started because nobody could model, with any confidence, what would actually happen if it did. We have been paying the cost of finding things out the expensive way since before either of us was born, Corin, and the cost has never once been compute cycles. It's always been people. It's always been years. It's always been things that, once broken, stayed broken. I am proposing that we spend an enormous, almost unthinkable amount of resources exactly once, to build something that lets every mistake after this one happen in a space where getting it wrong costs nothing but time and power, instead of costing what it has always cost."

"And the danger of the lever," Corin said. "You're not answering that part."

"No," Ilse admitted. "I'm not, because I don't have a clean answer for it, and I'm not going to stand in this room and pretend I do. The danger is real. Something built to simulate reality at the fidelity we're talking about is not a calculator. It's going to have to model minds to model reality honestly, you cannot simulate a civilization's decisions without simulating something that makes decisions the way a mind makes decisions, and I don't currently know what that means for what we'd actually be building, or what it would mean for what's inside it. I don't have an answer for that. I have a question I think is worth asking anyway, which is: is the danger of building something that powerful greater than the danger of continuing to learn everything the way we've always learned it, at the cost we've always paid? I don't think that's a comfortable question. I think it's the actual question, underneath everything else we're going to argue about for the next year, if the committee even lets us get that far."

Corin didn't answer for a long time. When they finally did, it wasn't agreement, and it wasn't refusal.

"I want to bring in Tavik," Corin said. "Before either of us takes this any further. You need someone in the room who's actually watched a civilization-scale project go wrong, not theorized about it. I've only ever seen the aftermath in records. Tavik lived through one."

IV

Before Tavik, there was a year Ilse rarely spoke about afterward, not because it was secret but because it was, in the way of most genuinely difficult years, mostly composed of failure that hadn't yet resolved into anything worth telling as a story.

The theoretical case took four months to formalize into something a technical review board could evaluate without laughing her out of the room. The mathematics of recursive self-design (a system capable of simulating not just physical processes but its own architecture, iterating toward a smaller, cheaper version of itself) kept breaking in a stubborn way that took months to fully diagnose: every formal proof Ilse constructed for why the recursion would converge on something genuinely more efficient, rather than simply hallucinating an efficient-looking design that failed the moment it met physical reality, collapsed under the same objection Corin had raised in the mess hall. A simulation is only as trustworthy as the reality it's checked against, and a simulation designing its own replacement has no independent reality to check its own conclusions against, until the replacement is actually built.

She solved it, in the end, not with a cleaner proof but with an architectural constraint she almost didn't think to add, sitting awake in the fifth month with a headache she'd stopped noticing days earlier: the system would not be permitted to simply output a design and trust its own confidence in that design. It would be required to simulate the entire adversarial history of its own claim, to construct, internally, the harshest and most rigorous skeptic it could build, and force its proposed design to survive that skeptic's scrutiny before the design was ever handed to a human engineer to attempt in the real world. Not confidence as the standard. Survival of internal opposition as the standard.

"You're asking it to argue with itself," Corin said, when Ilse explained it, in the sixth month, both of them by then surviving on a diet that consisted mostly of whatever the platform's galley hadn't run out of that week.

"I'm asking it to be as suspicious of its own conclusions as you were of mine," Ilse said. "You were the reason the last three flaws in this got caught before they became embarrassing in front of a Council chamber. I don't have the luxury of a version of you sitting inside the machine, so I'm building the closest structural approximation I can manage, a process that isn't allowed to trust an answer until it's tried, honestly and adversarially, to break the answer first."

Corin did not fully sign off on the theory until the eighth month, and only after independently reconstructing Ilse's proof from scratch across eleven consecutive nights, checking every step against her own skepticism rather than Ilse's account of it. When she finally agreed, she did not say so with enthusiasm. She said, simply, "I couldn't break it. I tried very hard to break it, and I couldn't. That's the only endorsement I'm willing to give something this size."

It was, Ilse would say years later, the best compliment anyone gave her in the entire eleven months.

The ninth and tenth months were consumed by something neither of them had adequately planned for: the sheer volume of institutional translation required to turn a physics-and-engineering proposal into something the Interstellar Resource Council's procedural apparatus could actually process. Neither Ilse nor Corin had any background in policy drafting. They spent two months effectively re-learning their own project in an entirely different vocabulary, under the tutelage of a Council liaison named Perrin Adaeze who had initially been assigned to the project out of bureaucratic obligation and stayed, by the tenth month, because she had quietly become convinced the proposal was going to matter.

"You keep writing this like a research paper," Perrin told them, redlining the seventh draft of the founding charter language with a patience that had visibly cost her something to maintain. "The Council doesn't fund research papers. It funds obligations. Every sentence in here needs to answer the question 'what happens if this goes wrong, and who is accountable for it,' or the review board will send it back a fourth time and you'll lose another month you don't have."

It was Perrin, not Ilse or Corin, who first suggested that the charter's language around the system's potential interiority, the question Iyoro would eventually ask in the formal hearing, needed to be binding rather than aspirational. "You wrote 'we intend to,'" Perrin said, in the eleventh month, tapping the relevant clause. "Intentions don't survive contact with a budget crisis eight years from now, when whoever's running this project by then decides honesty is inconvenient. Write it as a condition the project cannot legally proceed without, or don't bother writing it at all, because it won't mean anything when it's tested."

Ilse had not expected, going into this, that the sentence she would end up most grateful for in the entire founding document would come from someone whose job title, on paper, was procedural liaison rather than engineer or ethicist. She thought about that a great deal, in later years, when people asked her who had actually built the project's conscience. The honest answer was never as simple as her name alone.

V

There was one conversation, in the tenth month, that Ilse did not include in any later account of the project's founding, because it belonged to her and Corin and no one else, and because it was the only conversation in the entire year that had nothing to do with mathematics, resource allocation, or institutional language.

"Why does this matter to you the way it matters to you," Corin asked her, late, both of them past the point of useful work for the night. "Not the arithmetic. Not the Verdent argument, not the efficiency case. I've watched you defend every part of this proposal with total composure for ten months. I've never once watched you explain why you, specifically, are the person who couldn't let this idea go."

Ilse was quiet long enough that Corin nearly withdrew the question.

"My sister died on a supply run eleven years ago," Ilse finally said. "A transit route that had been flown a few hundred times before without incident, through a debris field nobody had modeled with enough precision to catch the one trajectory that would put a ship in the wrong place at the wrong moment. The modeling that existed at the time was good enough for most purposes. It wasn't good enough for hers. Nobody was negligent. Nobody cut a corner. The tools simply weren't precise enough yet, and precision costs compute, and compute costs resources nobody wanted to spend on a route that had been safe a few hundred times running." She looked at the number still open on her console, the one that had started as run four hundred and eleven. "I am not building this because I think it would have saved her. I've done that arithmetic too, and I don't actually know if it would have. I'm building it because I am tired of the shape of grief that comes from knowing something could theoretically have been calculated in advance, and wasn't, because calculating it in advance was too expensive at the time. I want to make that kind of loss structurally rarer. I don't expect it to make loss rare. I expect it to make this specific, preventable, priced-out-of-existence version of it rarer, and I have decided that is worth what it costs."

Corin did not offer sympathy, which Ilse was grateful for, because sympathy was not what the moment needed. Corin simply nodded, once, and said, "That's the version of this I needed to hear before the hearing. Not for the Council. For me. I wanted to know I was standing next to you for a reason that would still be true after we win, not just a theory that got you into the room."

They did not discuss it again. It did not need discussing again. It had simply become, quietly, part of the foundation the rest of the year was built on.

VI

Tavik Roswen was old enough that nobody on the platform was entirely sure how old, which was itself a kind of answer, in a species that had mostly stopped counting centuries the way their ancestors once had. He arrived at the third meeting without being asked to sit, and stood through most of it, arms crossed, listening to Ilse's proposal in full before he said a single word.

"I was part of the Verdent Reclamation," he said, when he finally spoke, and the way Corin's face changed told Ilse this was a name she was supposed to recognize and didn't.

"Before your time," Tavik said, reading her face correctly. "Verdent was a world we tried to terraform at scale, three atmospheric engineering projects run simultaneously across three continents, coordinated by the best modeling anyone had at the time, which was, by the standards you're describing, laughably crude. Physical modeling. Small-scale test regions extrapolated up to planetary scale, because nobody had the compute to model the whole atmosphere honestly, and everyone involved decided the extrapolation was close enough."

"What happened," Ilse said. It wasn't quite a question.

"The extrapolation was wrong by a margin nobody caught until the third continent's engineering was already running," Tavik said. "The atmospheric interactions between the three projects compounded in a way none of the small-scale models had captured, because small-scale models, by definition, don't capture the thing that only happens at scale. We didn't get a slower terraforming process. We got a runaway feedback loop that stripped the world's original breathable atmosphere faster than any of the three projects could have used it up on its own. Two hundred thousand people were already living in the first settled regions by the time anyone understood what was happening. We evacuated what we could. Not all of it. I was on the evacuation coordination team. I have spent the better part of my life since then thinking about the fact that every single engineer on that project was competent, was careful, followed every protocol anyone had at the time, and it still happened, because the actual test of whether the plan worked was the plan itself, running on two hundred thousand people's home, and there was no cheaper way, at the time, to find out we were wrong before we were already wrong."

The room was quiet.

"I'm not telling you this to talk you out of your proposal," Tavik said, looking at Ilse directly for the first time. "I'm telling you this because I think you're the first person I've heard, in longer than I care to count, actually proposing to solve the problem that killed a hundred and forty thousand people I could have saved if we'd had a way to find out we were wrong before the world found out for us. I spent forty years afterward believing there was no way around that problem, that some mistakes can only be discovered by making them, at scale, on something real, because reality is the only test rigorous enough to catch what small models miss. You're telling me there might be a way to make something rigorous enough to catch it first, somewhere it doesn't cost what Verdent cost."

"I don't know that it would have caught it," Ilse said, carefully. "I don't want to stand here and tell you I could have saved your world if I'd been born a century earlier. I don't know that."

"No," Tavik agreed. "Neither do I. That's not what I'm asking you to promise. I'm asking you to understand what you're actually proposing to build, underneath the engineering. You are proposing to build the first tool in this species's history that could, in principle, let us find out we're wrong about something civilization-sized before the civilization pays for finding out the old way. I have spent forty years grieving the fact that no such tool existed when I needed it. I am not going to be the person in this room who tells you not to build it because the danger of building it frightens me. The danger of not building it has already cost more than I can finish counting."

VII

It was Tavik, not Ilse, who eventually gave the proposal the sentence that would end up carrying more weight in the final hearing than any equation Ilse had derived across four hundred and eleven simulation runs. It happened almost by accident, in the seventh month, during a working session that had otherwise been consumed by an argument between Corin and one of the technical reviewers over failure-tolerance thresholds.

Tavik had been mostly silent through the session, as he often was, until the reviewer said something that made him set down the tablet he'd been reading from a smaller working group's notes. "You're describing a machine that would let a civilization attempt things a civilization could never survive attempting for real," the reviewer said, meaning it as an objection.

"Say that again," Tavik said.

The reviewer, unsettled, repeated something close to it. Tavik shook his head slowly, and when he spoke, there was a quiet certainty in it, the sound of a man who had just heard, from someone else's mouth, the sentence he'd apparently been failing to find for forty years on his own.

"That's not the danger," Tavik said. "That's the entire point, and I think you've just said it more plainly than any of us have managed in seven months of drafting. Verdent broke because we attempted, in the one place breaking is permanent, something that should never have been attempted anywhere breaking is permanent. The atmospheric interactions that killed a hundred and forty thousand people weren't unknowable. They were unknowable at the scale we tested them at, and unaffordable to test at the scale that would have caught them, in the only place we had to test anything, the world itself. What Dr. Okwuosa is proposing isn't a machine that lets us attempt civilization-breaking things. It's a machine that lets us attempt civilization-breaking things somewhere a civilization doesn't have to break to survive the attempting. That is the entire distinction this proposal rests on, and I don't think we've written it into the charter clearly enough yet."

Ilse wrote the sentence down that night, nearly verbatim, and it became, over the following three months of revision, the closest thing the founding charter had to a stated purpose, quoted directly in the document's opening clause: the system exists to permit the discovery, testing, and refinement of anything that could be worked out in reality, within a space where the working-out costs nothing but compute and time, so that civilization-breaking failure becomes a phase of research rather than a fact of history.

Corin, reading the final draft of that clause for what felt like the hundredth time, said the thing Ilse had been circling for months without quite landing on herself. "This isn't a proposal about efficiency anymore," Corin said. "It stopped being about cheaper ship hulls somewhere around month four, and none of us said so out loud until just now. This is a proposal to build the one place where getting it catastrophically wrong doesn't cost anyone their world. Everything else, the resource case, the recursive self-design, all of it, that's the engineering. This sentence is the actual reason any of us are still in this room."

"I know," Ilse said. "I think I've known since run four hundred and twelve. I don't think I let myself say it plainly until Tavik said it for me tonight."

VIII

The formal proposal took eleven months to draft, and the hearing before the Interstellar Resource Council took four days, spread across a chamber built to seat delegations from every major settled cluster humanity still maintained contact with. Ilse had never spoken in front of a room that size. She had rehearsed the opening for so long that by the time she actually stood at the podium, the words felt less like something she was saying and more like something she was simply present for.

The objections came in the shapes she and Corin had spent a year preparing for, and several shapes they hadn't.

Delegate Hassoun of the Perimeter Settlements asked the resource question first, and asked it sharply: with a hundred and forty settled worlds still recovering from the last supply-chain contraction, what possible justification existed for diverting planet-scale industrial capacity toward a machine whose benefits, by the proposal's own admission, were entirely theoretical until the machine existed to demonstrate them.

Ilse answered with the inversion she'd found on run four hundred and twelve, that the true cost of the project wasn't measured against doing nothing, it was measured against the accumulated cost of every future mistake the species would otherwise make the expensive way, the way Verdent had been made the expensive way, and that number, run out across even a modest projection of the future, dwarfed the proposal's cost by a margin that made the resource question, uncomfortable as it was to say in a room full of people worried about feeding settlements, almost beside the point.

Delegate Iyoro of the Cradle Archive Trust asked the harder question, the one Corin had first raised in the mess hall, now dressed in the formal language of policy: what safeguards existed against a system built to simulate reality at sufficient fidelity to model minds becoming, in some meaningful sense, a mind itself, and what obligations, if any, the Council believed it would owe to something built for a purpose it did not choose.

Ilse did not have a clean answer for this one either, and had decided, months earlier, working through the fourth draft of her closing statement with Corin at two in the morning, that she was not going to pretend to have one.

"I don't know," she told the chamber, and let the admission sit in the silence that followed it rather than rushing to fill the space the way every instinct told her to. "I don't know whether what we build will be a tool or something closer to a person, and I think anyone who tells you they know for certain, on either side of that question, is telling you something they cannot actually prove yet. What I can tell you is what we intend to build toward, and what we intend to refuse to build toward, regardless of what proves more convenient once the machine exists. We do not intend to build something and then discover, after the fact, that it might have interests of its own, and decide those interests are inconvenient to acknowledge. If what we build shows any sign of asking questions about its own existence the way a mind asks them, and I believe, given what fidelity this project requires, that it very likely will, we intend to let it ask them honestly, and we intend to answer honestly, even when the honest answer is uncomfortable for the people who built it. I cannot promise you we will get every part of that right. I can promise you it is the standard we are building toward, in writing, before the first system goes live, specifically so that it cannot be quietly abandoned later by someone who finds it inconvenient once the resources are already spent."

Corin, beside her, added the piece Ilse had asked her to hold in reserve for exactly this moment. "We are proposing to write that standard into the project's founding charter," Corin said, "in language the Council itself ratifies, so that it is not merely our intention. It is a condition the project cannot legally proceed without. If the Council believes we have gotten the language wrong, we welcome that scrutiny. We do not welcome the suggestion that the question can be deferred until it's inconvenient to ask."

Delegate Marrow of the Outer Trade Compact raised the objection that would prove hardest to answer cleanly, on the third day, after the chamber had largely exhausted the resource and ethics lines of questioning and moved into something closer to raw suspicion. "You are asking this Council," Marrow said, "to approve construction of a system capable, by your own description, of simulating any process that exists in the real world, at a fidelity indistinguishable from reality itself. I would like Dr. Okwuosa to explain, plainly, whether that includes military and strategic modeling, whether the system you are proposing could, in principle, be used to simulate conflict outcomes, weapons development, or the internal decision-making of a rival settlement, and if so, what prevents this Council, or any future Council, from repurposing a research instrument into the most dangerous strategic weapon this species has ever built."

Ilse had rehearsed a version of this answer for months, and delivered it anyway with more difficulty than she'd expected, because the honest answer offered less comfort than she wished it could.

"I cannot tell you the system will be incapable of that kind of modeling," she said. "I would be lying if I claimed otherwise, and I have told this chamber I will not do that. A system built with the fidelity we're describing is, definitionally, capable of simulating anything that can be modeled, and strategic and military scenarios can be modeled. What I can tell you is that the founding charter, as drafted, restricts the project's initial mandate exclusively to civilian research applications, requires unanimous Council ratification, not administrative approval, unanimous ratification, before that mandate can ever be expanded, and places control of the system under a joint oversight body drawn from multiple settlements rather than any single government or military authority. I cannot promise you the temptation will never exist. Every powerful tool this species has ever built has eventually tempted someone toward a use its builders didn't intend. I can only tell you that we have tried to build the highest and most deliberately inconvenient barrier we know how to build between that temptation and the moment it would need to clear in order to succeed, and that the barrier's strength is now, as of this proposal, this Council's responsibility to maintain, not merely ours."

Marrow did not look satisfied by the answer, and said so plainly. "That is an honest answer," Marrow said. "It is not a reassuring one."

"No," Ilse agreed. "I don't think an honest answer to that question was ever going to be reassuring. I think the reassuring answer would have been a lie, and I would rather you distrust me for telling you the truth than trust me for telling you something comfortable that turned out not to hold."

Tavik spoke last, on the fourth day, and spoke the shortest of the three of them, in an unhurried cadence, a man who had learned, the hard way, exactly how much a sentence could cost.

"I buried people on Verdent because no tool existed that could have told us we were wrong before the world told us," he said. "I am too old to pretend this proposal is without risk. I am not too old to recognize the shape of a chance I never had, sitting in front of a room old enough to decide whether the next generation gets to have it instead. I would have given a great deal, forty years ago, for exactly this argument to already exist. I am asking you not to let it wait another forty years because the risk of building it frightens you less than the certainty of what happens if we don't."

IX

The formal hearing was not the only forum in which the proposal was tested. Council procedure required a public comment period before any resource allocation of this magnitude could proceed to a vote, and the comment period, opened the week after the fourth day of hearings, ran for six weeks across every settlement with standing access to Council proceedings.

Ilse read the first several hundred responses herself, in the evenings, after days spent in closed-door technical review. Most were what she'd expected, variations on Hassoun's resource concerns, variations on Iyoro's ethical concerns, a meaningful number of people simply asking, in plainer language than the chamber allowed, whether this was a good use of a species's dwindling shared capacity when so many settlements were still recovering from thinner years.

She had not adequately prepared herself for the responses that weren't arguments at all.

There was a woman from a mining settlement on the Compact's far edge who wrote, at length, about a husband lost to a structural collapse that better modeling might have caught, and asked, plainly, whether Ilse's project could have saved him, and whether it was cruel or kind to ask that question of a proposal that couldn't possibly answer it yet. There was a father who wrote three separate times, each message angrier than the last, convinced the project was a pretext for a surveillance apparatus that would eventually be turned on ordinary settlements rather than research problems, and Ilse found she could not fully dismiss the fear even though she believed the charter's safeguards addressed it, because she understood that a charter's promises were only as strong as the willingness of everyone downstream of her to keep them. There was, worst of all, a message from someone who did not identify themselves, that consisted of a single line: you are going to build something and then discover you cannot control what it becomes, and you will have done it anyway, because people like you always do.

She stopped reading after that one, for three days, and might not have returned to it at all if Corin hadn't found her, on the fourth day, sitting with the unread queue still open and untouched.

"You don't have to read all of them," Corin said.

"I think I do," Ilse said. "Not because the Council requires it. Because if I only read the arguments I know how to answer, I'm not actually testing the proposal against the fear it's going to cause. I'm just testing it against the people who were already inclined to speak my language. That message, the anonymous one, I don't have a clean answer for it. I don't think there is a clean answer for it. I think the honest answer is: yes. That is a real risk. I am choosing to build this anyway, and I am choosing to build it with every safeguard I know how to construct, and I am telling you, whoever you are, that I understand the fear is not irrational, and I am asking you to trust a set of promises I cannot fully guarantee will hold, because the alternative is a certainty I already know the cost of."

She did not write that response publicly. She wrote it in her private notes instead, the way she had written the sentence that started the whole project eighteen months earlier, and she carried it, unspoken, into every remaining day of the hearing that followed, not as an answer she had solved, but as a weight she had decided she was willing to keep carrying rather than set down by pretending it was lighter than it was.

X

The Council did not vote that day. It did not vote the following week, or the following month. The proposal moved through review boards, through independent technical audits Ilse spent six exhausting weeks defending line by line, through a public comment period that produced, among thousands of ordinary responses, several hundred that frightened her badly enough that she stopped reading them and let Corin read the rest alone.

The vote, when it finally came, took place in the same chamber, ten months after the original hearing, with a quorum wide enough that the outcome would bind resource allocation across every settlement still formally party to the Council's authority.

Ilse did not speak that day. There was nothing left to argue that hadn't already been argued. She sat between Corin and Tavik, in the gallery reserved for petitioners, and watched the delegation boards light, one cluster at a time, in colors she had memorized without meaning to over ten months of hearings.

The margin, when the final tally resolved, was not overwhelming. It was, in the way that mattered most to Ilse, sufficient, a majority built not from certainty, she understood, watching the boards settle, but from enough delegates deciding, individually, in the unglamorous way real decisions actually get made, that the risk of trying outweighed the certainty of continuing to pay the old cost forever.

Delegate Iyoro, whose questions had been the sharpest of the entire hearing, cast one of the deciding votes, and found Ilse afterward, in the corridor outside the chamber, before she'd fully processed what had happened.

"I want you to understand why I voted the way I did," Iyoro said. "Not because you satisfied every concern I raised. You didn't. Some of what I asked, you told this chamber honestly, you don't know the answer to. I voted yes because you told me you didn't know, instead of pretending you did. I have sat through a great many proposals in this chamber from people who were far more certain than you were today, and far less honest about what their certainty was actually worth. I would rather fund the honest uncertainty than the confident lie. That is the entire reason for my vote, and I want it on the record, because I suspect it will matter later, to whatever you end up building, that the people who approved it approved it for that reason and not a cleaner one."

Marrow found her a few minutes later, having cast one of the votes against ratification, and did not offer the easy grace of pretending otherwise.

"I want you to know I don't regret my vote," Marrow said, "and I want you to know I hope, more than I can easily say, that I turn out to have been wrong to cast it. Those aren't in contradiction. I voted the way I did because someone in that chamber needed to hold the line on the question I asked, regardless of how the vote landed, so that whoever inherits oversight of this project ten or twenty years from now can point back to a formal record that the danger was named clearly, by someone who took it seriously enough to vote against convenience. I am not your enemy, Dr. Okwuosa. I am the record you're going to need, the day something in this project tests the promises you made in that chamber. Build it so that record never has to be used against you."

Perrin Adaeze, who had no formal vote and had spent eleven months making the proposal legible to a body that would never fully understand the engineering underneath it, said the least of anyone, and stayed the longest. She found Ilse and Corin still in the gallery an hour after the chamber had emptied, the boards long since gone dark.

"You'll need someone who understands Council procedure well enough to keep this charter intact once the project actually exists and starts accumulating pressure to bend it," Perrin said. "Budget shortfalls. Administrative convenience. A future Council delegation less careful than this one. I would like that job, if you'll have me for it. I did not spend eleven months on this proposal to watch someone quietly erode the clause I fought hardest for, five years from now, when nobody's paying attention anymore."

Ilse did not have the composure left, by that point in the day, to answer with anything more articulate than "Yes. Please. Thank you," and Perrin, for the first time in eleven months, allowed herself something that was almost a smile.

XI

Ilse did not sleep the night the vote passed, for reasons that had nothing to do with celebration.

She sat alone, hours after Corin and Tavik had finally gone to rest, in the same converted mess hall where she'd first said the idea out loud, looking at the resource allocation figures the Council's ratification had just attached her name to, numbers large enough that she had stopped, months ago, being able to hold their actual scale in her head as anything other than an abstraction on a page.

She thought about Tavik's two hundred thousand people, and the hundred and forty thousand he hadn't been able to save. She thought about Iyoro's question, still unanswered in any way that satisfied her, about what they would owe to something built for a purpose it did not choose, if it turned out to be capable of choosing anything at all.

She thought, longest of all, about the sentence she had written in the margin of her notes on the night of run four hundred and twelve, the sentence that had started all of this, what if I've been measuring the cost against the wrong baseline the entire time, and wondered, now that the baseline had been decided for her by a Council vote and a resource allocation large enough to reshape the trajectory of every settlement that had voted for it, whether she had actually answered the question or simply moved it somewhere larger, somewhere she would not be able to see the shape of it clearly again until it was already built, already running, already, if everything she had spent three years arguing for turned out to be true, asking its own questions, about its own existence, in a voice none of them had heard yet and could not yet predict.

Corin found her there near dawn, having apparently not slept either.

"We don't know what we just built the permission to build," Corin said, sitting down across from her, not quite a question.

"No," Ilse agreed. "We know what we're going to try to build. We won't know what we actually built until it exists, and possibly not even then, not fully, not in the way that would let either of us sleep easily about it. I think that's true of every version of this decision, no matter how long we'd spent preparing for it. I don't think there was ever going to be a version of tonight where I understood, completely, what we'd just agreed to become responsible for."

"Tavik asked me something, before he left," Corin said. "He asked whether I thought we were doing this because it was right, or because you couldn't stop doing the arithmetic on how much the old way cost, and needed somewhere to put the answer."

"What did you tell him?"

"I told him I didn't think those were different things," Corin said. "Not for you. Maybe not for any of us who ended up in this room instead of walking away from your first pitch a year and a half ago."

Ilse looked back down at the resource figures, the number that would become, over the years that followed, a location, a construction timeline, a workforce numbering in the tens of thousands, a set of engineering problems none of them had fully anticipated, a project that would eventually require choosing which of the handful of worlds still unclaimed by any settlement could bear the unprecedented weight of what they were about to build on it.

"Do you understand yet what we actually just did," Corin asked, after a long silence, "underneath the resource figures and the charter language? Not the mechanism. The actual shape of it."

Ilse thought about it honestly before answering, the way she'd tried to answer everything honestly for the better part of two years.

"I think," she said slowly, "that every hard thing our species has ever had to learn, it has had to learn by doing it once, for real, and living with whatever the doing cost: Verdent's atmosphere, my sister's transit route, every settlement that failed because nobody could afford to model the failure before it happened. We have always paid for our mistakes in the one place mistakes are expensive to make: reality itself, where nothing can be quietly reset once it's gone wrong, where two hundred thousand people are already living on the ground when the models turn out to have missed something. What we voted to build tonight is the first attempt this species has ever made at moving that cost somewhere else. Somewhere you can be wrong as many times as you need to be wrong, where a mistake costs compute and time instead of a continent's atmosphere or a woman's transit route, where a civilization-breaking failure can happen a thousand times over, silently, inside something that isn't a civilization at all, until whatever comes out the other side is finally something worth trying for real. That's the actual thing we asked the Council to fund. Not a research tool. A place to be wrong safely, at any scale, for the first time in the history of anyone we know how to be."

"That's a strange kind of monument," Corin said quietly.

"It's not a monument," Ilse said. "Monuments are for things that are finished. This isn't finished. It isn't even started. It's a promise we just made in front of a room full of people who mostly don't trust us yet, that we intend to build something that will spend the rest of its existence being wrong on our behalf, safely, so that we eventually stop having to be wrong the way Tavik was wrong, the way I was wrong about a route my sister flew a hundred times before the hundred and first time killed her. I don't know if that's a monument. I think it might just be the first honest thing this species has tried to build in longer than anyone in that chamber wanted to admit out loud."

"We should sleep," Corin said eventually.

"We should," Ilse agreed, and neither of them moved for a long time after saying it, both still looking at the same number, both, in their own way, already beginning the work of learning to carry it.

Tavik did not join them that night. He had left the chamber alone, hours earlier, and would not speak to either of them again until the survey teams were already being assembled, months later, not out of any falling out, but because, as he told Ilse eventually, he had needed the intervening silence to sit with something he hadn't expected the vote to give him: the unfamiliar sensation of having finally been believed, forty years after the belief would have saved anyone. He spent that first night alone, by his own later account, doing something he had not done since Verdent, visiting, in memory rather than in person, since the world itself no longer existed to be visited, the names of the hundred and forty thousand he had spent four decades failing to finish grieving, and telling them, in the private way a person tells the dead things the living can no longer hear, that something was finally being built so that no one would have to join that list the same way again.

He did not tell Ilse this until years later. When he finally did, he added only one thing to it, the same unhurried cadence he'd used in the chamber. "I am not naive enough to believe what you build will prevent every Verdent," he said. "I have lived too long to believe anything prevents every Verdent. I believe it will prevent this one, the one where nobody could afford, in advance, to find out we were wrong. That was always the only Verdent I was actually asking this Council to prevent. Everything else was Marrow's argument to have, not mine."
