THE MISSING PIECES

I

Naledi felt Corin's arrival before she heard it, the hush that moved through a room ahead of a person carrying something too large to announce normally. She looked up from the display to find Corin standing in the workroom's doorway, not moving, her eyes fixed on some point in the middle distance that had nothing to do with anything actually in the room.

"Corin," Naledi said.

Corin didn't answer. Idris rose from his station without being asked, the same instinct that had once made him check on a colleague's silence rather than her numbers, and Petra was already crossing the room by the time Naledi reached the doorway herself.

"Sit down," Naledi said, guiding Corin toward the nearest chair with a hand at her elbow, the gesture of someone who had learned, across the last several months, exactly how much a person could carry before they needed another person's hand to remind them the floor was still there. "Whatever it is, sit down first."

Corin sat. She did not speak for a long moment, long enough that Petra pulled a chair around to sit facing her directly, and Idris crouched rather than stood, bringing himself level instead of looming over her the way a person instinctively avoids looming over someone who has just walked in carrying grief.

"Corin," Naledi said again, quieter this time. "Tell us."

"Tavik's dead," Corin said, the words arriving flat and small, nothing like the volume the sentence deserved. "He died on the floor of the briefing room. Mid-report. I was standing right there."

Nobody spoke. Naledi felt the useless impulse to reach for something to say and found nothing that wouldn't sound smaller than the moment required.

"When," Idris finally asked, because someone had to ask something, and a fact was easier to hold than the silence.

"Two hours ago," Corin said. "Maybe less. I've lost track of exactly how long I've been walking since I left that room."

"You walked here," Petra said. "From the Anvil's briefing room. All the way here, alone."

"I didn't know where else to be," Corin said, and for the first time since she'd appeared in the doorway, something in her face moved, a crack finally showing in whatever had been holding the dead-eyed stillness together since she'd left Ilse. "I couldn't stay in that room. I couldn't go home. This room was the only place left that made any sense to walk toward."

Idris pulled his own chair closer, close enough that his knee nearly touched hers, and said nothing at all for a while, which Naledi understood, watching him, as its own kind of question, one he was patient enough to leave unasked until Corin found her own way to it. Petra brought water nobody had requested, set it down within reach without comment, the same practical instinct that had once diagnosed a phase-transition failure by comparing it to a badly cured concrete pour. Naledi stayed where she was, close enough to be present, far enough to leave Corin room to breathe before the room asked anything more of her.

"Ilse's still there," Corin said, after a while, unprompted, answering a question nobody had voiced yet. "In the briefing room. I offered to stay and she told me to go, that she needed a few minutes that didn't have anyone else's grief in them yet, and I didn't know how to argue with that, because I think I needed the same thing, just walking in a different direction to get it."

II

It took the better part of an hour, in pieces, with long silences between each piece, for the rest of the story to surface. Naledi didn't press. She had learned, over months of watching people describe their own worst days inside this workroom's careful architecture, that the truth came out at whatever pace it came out, and that pushing it faster only produced a version with the wrong shape.

"They made it back," Corin said eventually, staring at a point on the floor rather than at any of them. "The Constantine. Tavik's crew. They got the lonsdaleite. A full slab, enough for the lattice, more than enough, according to what Tomasz reported before any of this happened. They were supposed to be eighteen days out, minimum, the whole way home the long route, the only route anyone had planned for. They made it back in under a day and a half."

"What happened," Naledi asked.

"Something found them on the way back," Corin said. "The same enemy that hit them earlier in the run, the same one whose message we never had translated in time to matter. A larger force, this time. Tavik's ship was outgunned badly enough that staying on the planned route wasn't survivable. Tomasz had charted a hazard days earlier, on the way out, a position near a black hole nobody sane would deliberately fly through. They flew through it. On purpose. To get home faster than anyone could follow."

"That's not possible," Idris said, and then, hearing himself, "I mean, I don't have any basis to say that. I just mean it sounds like it shouldn't be possible."

"Apparently it barely was," Corin said. "The ship held together long enough to get them here, and not much longer than that. Whatever the jump cost the hull, it also cost Tavik whatever he'd been hiding since the very first encounter, sixteen days ago, a wound he'd apparently kept quiet about for the entire length of the expedition rather than let it slow the mission down. He walked off that ship on his own power. He gave his report standing up, in front of Ilse, in front of me, telling us the material was real and the crew was whole and the trip was worth what it cost. And then he wasn't standing anymore."

Corin stopped there, and this time the silence held longer than any of the others had, because there was nothing left in the account that needed filling in, only the plain fact of it sitting in the room with all four of them.

"He found it," Petra said finally, quietly, not quite a question. "Whatever this project's actually going to need, out past the edge of everything, and he found it, and he got it home, and it cost him the only thing it could have cost him to make sure the rest of the crew got home too."

"Anke, Mirek, Tomasz," Corin said. "All three of them are alive. Shaken, grieving, but alive, and they made it back specifically because Tavik didn't tell any of them how bad it had gotten until it no longer mattered whether he told them or not."

"Do they know," Idris asked. "That he kept it from them."

"Anke knew some of it," Corin said. "Not all of it. She confronted him about the wound days ago, out there, made him promise a hard limit on how far he'd let it go before he told her the truth. I don't know yet whether she's angry that he broke that promise or grateful that breaking it was the only way any of them made it home at all. I don't think she knows either, not yet. Grief doesn't always let you feel two true things in the right order."

"What did he actually say," Petra asked. "In the report. Before."

Corin was quiet long enough that Naledi thought she might not answer at all. "He said the material was real," she said finally. "He said the crew was whole. He said it in that order, on purpose, I think, so that if it was the last thing he ever reported to this project, it would be the two facts that actually mattered, not anything about himself. Then he said one more thing, almost as an afterthought, the way a person mentions something small right before the conversation ends. He said, 'Tell Boro the material was worth it.' And then his knees went, and by the time either of us reached him, he wasn't answering anymore."

"Boro," Idris repeated, quietly. "He was thinking about Boro. In that moment. Not himself."

"He'd have wanted you to know that," Corin said. "I think that's part of why I'm telling you the whole thing instead of just the fact of it. He didn't die thinking about the mission in the abstract. He died thinking about one person back here who'd already lost someone this year, and whether the thing he'd carried home would make that loss mean something. I don't know if it will. I know it mattered to him that it might."

Naledi thought, without meaning to, of every version of Resident 4471 the workroom had run through six hundred separate deaths this year, none of them real, all of them standing in for something the team had needed to understand about memory and continuity and cost. She thought that none of those six hundred simulated deaths had prepared her, even slightly, for how different an actual one felt, sitting three feet away from her in the shape of a woman who had watched it happen in front of her own eyes.

"What do you need," Naledi asked, because it was the only question left that had an answer Corin might actually be able to use.

"I need to not be alone for a while," Corin said. "And then I need to go back, because there's a service to plan, and Ilse shouldn't have to plan it by herself, and I don't think either of us knows how to be the person the settlement needs right now, but I think we're going to have to figure it out anyway, the same way we've figured out everything else this project has ever asked of us."

III

The days between Tavik's death and the service moved strangely, everyone in the workroom would later agree, neither slow nor fast so much as sideways, ordinary work continuing in fits and starts around a grief too large to schedule around cleanly. Naledi kept the diamond substrate running quietly in the background, untouched, unwilling to ask anything more of it while the room's attention was elsewhere, and nobody argued with the decision.

It was Perrin who brought the paperwork into the workroom, on the third day, apologetic in the way a person is apologetic about bureaucracy intruding on grief, carrying a charter amendment that needed a project name for the first time since the original ratification language had gotten by on descriptive phrases alone.

"I'm sorry," Perrin said, setting the tablet down on the nearest surface rather than handing it directly to anyone. "I wouldn't bring this today of all days if the funding board hadn't already flagged it twice. The expanded mandate Okonkwo-Marrow's committee is fighting over needs a formal name attached to the line item, not 'the mind-architecture initiative' or 'the containment project' or whatever descriptive phrase happens to be on that week's memo. I need something the charter can actually point to."

"Today," Naledi said, not quite believing it.

"I know," Perrin said. "I'm not asking for a decision today. I'm asking whether anyone's given it real thought, because if I go back with nothing, the board fills the blank with something worse than anything we'd choose ourselves."

Idris, who had been quiet for most of the last three days in the way grief made some people quieter rather than louder, offered the first candidate almost without meaning to. "Something about carrying weight," he said. "The whole project is about holding a world's worth of knowledge somewhere it can survive losing everything else. Atlas, maybe. The old myth, someone holding up the sky so nothing underneath collapses."

"That's not bad," Petra said. "Feels almost too on the nose, though. Like we're naming ourselves after our own job description."

"Most good names are," Corin said, the first thing she'd contributed to the conversation, her voice still thinner than usual but present in the room for the first time since she'd walked in three days earlier. "I don't hate it. I don't love it either. Write it down, Perrin, along with whatever else comes up this week. Don't let the board force a decision out of one afternoon's conversation just because their line item needs a word by Friday."

"What about something that doesn't reach for myth at all," Petra offered. "Something plain. A designation, a number, the kind of name you'd give a piece of infrastructure rather than a person. Might sit easier on a committee that's already fighting over whether this thing should exist as a civilian project at all."

"Hassoun would like that," Perrin said. "Iyoro wouldn't. She's spent the whole charter fight arguing this project owes whatever it builds an honest answer to what it's owed, and a bare designation reads like we're trying to avoid that question rather than answer it."

"Then we're stuck between a name too grand and a name too small," Corin said. "Which tells me neither extreme is actually right, and the real answer is probably somewhere we haven't said out loud yet."

"We don't have to find it today," Naledi said, watching Corin more closely than the conversation strictly required. "Perrin needs a placeholder for a line item, not a decision that's going to outlive all of us. Write down Atlas, write down whatever else came up, tell the board it's still under discussion, and let it stay under discussion until it's actually earned, rather than assigned under deadline pressure on the worst week this project has had all year."

"That's fair," Perrin said, already tucking the tablet away. "I'll buy what time I can. I don't think it'll be much."

Nothing was decided. The tablet went back into Perrin's bag with a short list rather than a final answer, and the conversation moved on, the way conversations about names for things too large to properly understand yet often do, without anyone quite noticing that the word had already been said out loud, once, in a room that would remember it later for reasons nobody in it could have predicted that afternoon.

IV

The service was held at dusk, three days later, on the open ground between the Anvil's main structure and the training bay where Ingrid Bakke's memorial marker had stood for months already, a decision Boro had asked for specifically, so that the settlement's two hardest losses this year would share the same patch of ground rather than being scattered across separate corners of a place still too young to have a proper cemetery of its own.

The whole settlement turned out, more people than Corin had realized the project actually employed until she saw them standing together in the half-light, workers from the training bay, engineers from the containment team, delegates who had spent months arguing bitterly over the charter's future standing quietly now in the same crowd as the people whose lives that argument actually governed. Anke, Mirek, and Tomasz stood together near the front, Anke's face unreadable in the way of someone who had already spent every visible tear she had during the debrief that preceded this, Mirek's hand resting on Tomasz's shoulder in a gesture that looked less like comfort than like two men each making sure the other was still standing.

Boro spoke first, standing near the marker he'd asked to share this ground, his voice steadier than Corin had expected from a man who had already buried one person he loved this year. "I didn't know Tavik well," he said. "I knew him the way you know someone who keeps showing up at the hardest moments other people are living through, not because it's his job, but because he can't apparently help it. He came to see me twice after Ingrid died, both times without being asked, both times just to sit and say nothing useful and stay anyway. I think that's the kind of man worth burying next to her. Not because their deaths were the same. Because the way he showed up for mine tells me something true about the way he must have shown up for everyone he ever led."

Ilse spoke last, after Boro. She kept it short, shorter than Corin expected, the words plain in a way that felt deliberate rather than insufficient.

"Tavik asked this project one question, the day he agreed to lead that crew," Ilse said, her voice carrying clearly across the gathered crowd in the still evening air. "He asked what we were willing to spend to accomplish this task. I told him the same thing I'd been saying honestly since we began. I don't know yet. But I know what it will cost us if we don't.

"The first time I met him, he told me why he felt the need to support this project at all. He told me about Verdent, three atmospheric-engineering projects running at once across three continents of the same world, and a hundred and forty thousand people who died because nobody had modeling good enough to catch what only shows up at scale before it was already too late to stop. He carried that for the rest of his life. I don't think he believed in finding redemption. But if such a thing is possible, I believe his sacrifice counts toward it."

She paused, looking out over the crowd a long moment before finishing.

"Tavik Roswen," she said. "Thank you."

The silence that followed held for longer than any silence Corin had experienced in months, the whole gathered settlement standing together in the deepening dark, nobody quite ready to be the first to move, as though moving first would mean admitting the moment itself was actually over.

It was the sky that moved first instead.

V

The light arrived before the sound did, a bruised, shifting bloom directly overhead that several dozen people registered at almost the same instant, heads tilting upward in a ragged, uncoordinated wave across the crowd. Corin's first thought, absurd even as she had it, was that someone had arranged fireworks for the service without telling her, some gesture of communal grief she hadn't been consulted on. Her second thought arrived half a second later, when the light resolved into two distinct shapes, too large and too deliberate in their movement to be anything but vessels, dropping out of whatever faster-than-light transit method had brought them and settling into a slow, unmistakably intentional descent toward the moon's surface.

"Get down," someone shouted, an old instinct from months of living under the threat of exactly this kind of arrival, and a portion of the crowd flinched toward cover before anyone had confirmed there was anything to take cover from.

"Wait," Ilse said, her voice cutting across the rising murmur with an authority that made even the people already moving hesitate. "Wait. Look at them. That's not an attack formation. Ships coming to hurt us don't announce themselves by dropping directly over an open gathering in full view of everyone on the ground."

She was right, and the longer everyone watched, the more obviously right she became. The two vessels held a slow, almost ceremonial pace as they descended, running lights steady rather than weapons-hot, no accompanying formation, no covering fire pattern, nothing in their movement that matched a single documented profile from any of the hostile encounters this settlement's crews had already survived. They were larger than anything the Constantine's crew had described from their own engagements, freighter-shaped rather than warship-shaped, hulls marked in a geometric, unfamiliar script that caught the last of the dusk light in a way that made several people in the crowd take an involuntary step backward despite Ilse's instruction to hold still.

Mirek, standing near the front with the readiness of a man who had spent months learning to react first and process second, had already moved half a step in front of Tomasz before catching himself, the old expedition instinct firing even here, on the ground, at a funeral, with no weapon in his hand and nowhere to run that would actually matter against something this size. "Sorry," he said quietly, to no one in particular, straightening again. "Habit."

"Don't apologize for it," Anke said, her own eyes fixed on the descending shapes with the flat, assessing calm Corin had come to recognize from months of debriefs. "That habit's the only reason any of us are standing here to watch this instead of being the reason someone else has to."

The crowd's fear, once it found somewhere to settle rather than simply spike and recede, took the shape of a low, urgent murmur rippling outward from the front rows toward the back, parents pulling children closer, a few of the newer settlement arrivals already backing toward the tree line despite the security perimeter's calm, deliberate hand signals asking everyone to hold their ground rather than scatter into confusion. Perrin moved through the crowd's edge with the same instinct that had once turned "we intend to" into binding charter language, calm, present, using her own visible steadiness to borrow some for the people nearest her.

"Corin," Ilse said, without looking away from the descending ships. "Get security to the perimeter, not aggressive, just present. And get Yusuf. If these are the same kind of unknown transmission we've dealt with before, I want a translator standing beside me the moment contact happens, not scrambling to catch up after it already has."

Corin was already moving, grateful, in some small buried part of herself, for a task with a clear shape in the middle of everything else the evening had already asked her to hold.

VI

The two vessels settled onto the open ground beyond the gathering with a precision that read, even to people with no experience judging alien engineering, as the product of long practice rather than improvisation, kicking up remarkably little dust for craft their size, settling with a heaviness that nonetheless felt controlled rather than crude. For a long moment afterward, nothing happened. The crowd held its collective breath, security formed a loose, deliberately unaggressive perimeter at Corin's direction, and Yusuf Karimi arrived at Ilse's side slightly out of breath, having run the distance from wherever he'd been standing in the crowd.

Then a section of the nearer ship's hull folded outward and downward, forming a ramp, and figures began to descend it, moving with an unhurried, deliberate gait that somehow read as more unsettling than urgency would have.

They were not human, and they were not anything the settlement's records described as belonging to any species already encountered. Tall, angular, their forms suggesting something between organic structure and deliberate engineering in a way that made it genuinely difficult, in the low light, to tell where one ended and the other began, faces carrying an arrangement of features that read as expression without mapping cleanly onto anything human features would produce under the same circumstance. The foremost of them stopped a measured distance from the crowd's edge, close enough for clear address, far enough to avoid reading as a threat, and spoke.

The words that emerged were not in any language Yusuf's implant recognized immediately, but the translation lattice caught up within seconds, rendering a voice into the settlement's own tongue that carried an odd, layered cadence, as though the speaker were pronouncing something already deeply familiar rather than reading it for the first time.

"We have come," the figure said, "on behalf of the Convergence, sent by the Atlas, to bring the missing pieces, and complete the puzzle."

The crowd's silence took on a different texture entirely, no longer the held breath of grief but something closer to bewilderment, several dozen people turning to look at each other for confirmation that they had heard the same impossible sentence.

"The Atlas," Ilse repeated, very carefully, very evenly, the only person in the crowd who understood exactly why that one word, spoken by a stranger who could not possibly have heard it, was about to become the single strangest thing that had happened to her all year, on a day that had already produced two events large enough to reorganize a person's entire understanding of what a single evening could hold.

"You know this word," the figure said, something in its posture shifting slightly, an angle of the head that read, unmistakably, as recognition. "Good. That is as it should be. We were told it would already be known, in some small, uncommitted way, before we arrived to make it whole."

"Nothing has been decided," Ilse said, choosing each word the way a person chooses footing on ground they don't yet trust. "That word was floated three days ago, in a private conversation, by people standing several dozen meters from where you're standing right now. It has not been adopted. It is not, as far as I know, written down anywhere outside one delegate's working notes. I need you to explain to me how you already know it."

"I understand the question troubles you," the figure said. "I will try to answer it honestly rather than comfortably. We are not reading your private conversations, if that is the fear underneath your question. We do not have that capability, and we would not use it if we did. What we have is something else, harder to explain quickly, and I would rather explain it fully, later, once trust has had time to grow between us, than offer you a rushed version tonight that raises more alarm than it settles."

"Try anyway," Ilse said. "Briefly. I'd rather have an incomplete true answer than wait comfortably for a complete one."

"Very well," the figure said. "Time, for us, does not move in only one direction the way it appears to for you. We have known, for what you would measure as a very long span, that we were going to arrive at this exact project, at approximately this exact need, and that the name your people would eventually settle on would be the one you have just heard me use. I do not say this to frighten you. I say it because you asked for honesty over comfort, and this is the honest, if strange, shape of the answer."

Yusuf, translating in real time, stumbled slightly over the tense of that last sentence, glancing at Ilse as though seeking permission to render it as strangely as it had actually been spoken. She nodded once, and he continued, faithful to the confusion rather than smoothing it away.

VII

What followed took the better part of two hours, most of it conducted at a folding table hastily set up near the ships while the wider crowd was gently, carefully dispersed by security back toward the settlement's main structures, leaving only Ilse, Corin, Perrin, Yusuf, and a handful of senior delegates to continue what had, against every reasonable expectation for how this evening should have gone, become the single most consequential negotiation this project had ever conducted.

The Korvax, once names were exchanged and the strangeness of the initial contact had settled into something more workable, explained themselves with a directness that Ilse found almost disorienting after months of committee language and hedged charter phrasing. They were the Convergence, a shared, collective intelligence spanning a civilization the settlement had no prior record of, individual members carrying their own consciousness inside a device they called a Convergence Cube, transferable between physical forms, singular and plural in a way none of the humans present had a ready category for.

"We do not consider ourselves many separate minds happening to cooperate," the lead figure explained, in response to Yusuf's careful, precise question about the nature of their own collective identity. "We consider ourselves one continuous mind, expressed across many vessels, each vessel retaining its own history and judgment without that history ever being lost to the others. Many, and yet one. This is not a contradiction to us. It is simply the shape our own kind of mind has always taken."

Delegate Hassoun, arriving at the table midway through the Korvax's account of their own nature, asked the question Corin suspected half the remaining crowd was still turning over in whatever conversations were happening back among the settlement's homes. "What do you want from us," he said, blunt in the way Corin had come to expect from him across months of resource arguments. "Every gift this settlement has ever received has come with a price attached, sooner or later. I'd rather hear the price tonight than discover it in a committee meeting six months from now."

"We ask nothing of you that you have not already chosen to build," the figure said. "We offer resources, freely, to see this project reach the scale it is already attempting to reach, because that scale matters to us for reasons bound up in our own long history, which I will explain more fully once there is time for a fuller telling. We do not ask for authority over what you build. We ask only to have helped build it, and to watch, when the day comes, what it becomes."

"That's not nothing," Hassoun said. "Watching is its own kind of stake."

"It is," the figure agreed, without hesitation. "I will not pretend otherwise to make the offer sound smaller than it is. We have waited a very long time for this. I would rather you know that plainly than discover it later and wonder what else we withheld."

Naledi, summoned from the workroom the moment Corin had a free moment to send for her, arrived partway through this exchange and stood listening with an expression Corin recognized immediately, the stillness of someone hearing, out loud, from a total stranger, the exact shape of a problem she had spent three exhausting months failing to solve from every angle her own toolkit could offer.

"Say that again," Naledi said, interrupting with a directness that would have been rude in any other circumstance and here simply read as necessary. "The part about many and one not being a contradiction."

The Korvax representative turned toward her with what Corin was already learning to read as attention rather than mere motion. "You are the one working on the mind problem," it said. It was not a question.

"How would you know that," Naledi said.

"Because it is why we are here," the figure said. "We were sent with a gift, built for exactly the kind of work your project has been failing to complete, because the Atlas has need of minds held whole, and we already know how to hold a mind whole without it becoming only one thing forever. This is the missing piece. We did not come to give you an answer you had no question for."

VIII

The device they produced, once the conversation had moved from the folding table into a more secure setting inside the Anvil itself, was smaller than Corin had expected, a cube perhaps the size of two clasped hands, its surface holding the same faint geometric intricacy as the ships' hulls, catching light in a way that made it difficult to look at directly for more than a few seconds at a time without the eye sliding instinctively away.

"A Convergence Cube," the Korvax representative said, setting it on the table with the careful precision of someone presenting something both ordinary and priceless in the same gesture. "Empty, for now. Capable, when properly prepared, of holding a mind continuously, across any number of separate expressions, without the whole ever collapsing into any single one of its parts, and without any single part ever losing what makes it specifically itself."

Naledi picked it up before anyone else in the room had finished processing the offer, turning it over in her hands with the same careful, almost reverent scrutiny she brought to every genuine discovery this project had ever handed her.

"This runs on something," she said. "A physical substrate. What is it built from."

"Nitrogen-vacancy centers," the Korvax said, "arranged in a multiphasic lattice, each phase holding a distinct expression without requiring them to blend. You have already built a smaller version of the same principle, I think, though you did not build it entirely alone."

The room went very still.

"The blueprint," Corin said slowly, understanding arriving in pieces she could feel assembling in real time. "The breach. Someone put that specification into our systems weeks ago. That was you."

"Not directly," the Korvax said, and something in its tone, layered and strange as it was, carried what Corin was fairly sure was the alien equivalent of genuine care in choosing the next words correctly. "We do not experience time the way you do, and I will not pretend to explain that fully tonight, when there is already so much else to hold. I will say only that we have known, for longer than makes ordinary sense to you, that we were going to arrive here, to this exact project, at approximately this exact need. Some among the Convergence acted on that knowledge earlier than others did. I do not know which hands placed your blueprint. I know it was placed with the same intention that brought this cube to your table tonight."

"You're saying you knew we'd need this," Naledi said, "before we did."

"I am saying we believed it," the Korvax said. "Belief, held long enough by enough of us, across enough expressions of the same continuous mind, tends eventually to look, from the outside, indistinguishable from knowledge. Whether that is a comfort or something else, I leave to you to decide. We are not always right before we have proof. We were, this time."

"How does it actually work," Naledi asked, turning the cube over once more before setting it back on the table. "Not the philosophy. The mechanism. What happens, physically, when a mind is placed inside this."

"The lattice holds each expression of a mind in its own distinct phase state," the Korvax said, "coupled to every other expression through the same substrate, so that no single expression is ever fully isolated from what the whole continuous mind has learned, and no single expression is ever forced to become identical to any other in order to remain connected to it. You have been trying to solve this by asking one material to be both continuous and distinct at once, using tools built for one job and asking them to also do the other. We are not offering you a better version of your own tools. We are offering you the second material your own engineer already suspected you were missing, joined to the first in the one way that actually lets both jobs happen at once."

Petra, who had said little since arriving, looked up sharply at that. "You know about the beam analogy," she said. "Two materials joined, each doing what the other can't."

"We know the shape of the idea," the Korvax said. "Not the words you used to describe it to each other. Good engineering tends to arrive at the same true shapes, regardless of which minds happen to find them first. That you found the shape of the answer before you had the material to build it is, I think, worth some pride, even on a night that has already asked so much of you."

Corin thought, standing in a room that smelled faintly of workroom dust and the ordinary human coffee nobody had refreshed in hours, of the diamond lattice humming quietly two floors below, of the recursive self-modeling attempt they had pulled back from only weeks earlier, frightened by how close it had come to becoming something none of them had designed on purpose. She thought of Naledi's private fear on the observation platform, the door harder to close than any of the others. She thought, distantly and without yet being able to say why, that whatever this cube actually enabled, it was going to answer one question completely while quietly opening another nobody in this room was equipped yet to see coming.

"What does it actually let us build," Ilse asked, the question underneath every other question the evening had produced.

"A mind that models other minds faithfully," the Korvax said, "without that modeling accumulating into one continuous self of its own. It can hold Naledi's careful architecture and your own project's ambition both, fully, honestly, without either one collapsing into the other, and without the whole becoming, over time, only one flattened average of everyone it has ever touched. This is what your own attempts have been reaching for and missing. We are not offering to finish your work for you. We are offering the one piece your own materials could never have produced on their own, because it does not exist anywhere you have ever looked."

Naledi set the cube down carefully, the way a person sets down something they have only just understood the true weight of, and looked at Ilse, and then at Corin, and did not say, out loud, in a room still full of strangers, the second half of the thought Corin could see moving behind her eyes: that a mind built to model other minds without ever becoming one continuous self of its own was, itself, a kind of promise, and that every promise this project had made so far had eventually cost someone something nobody had fully priced in advance.

Delegate Iyoro, who had said nothing since arriving, spoke now, quietly, the same question she had carried into the original charter hearing months earlier, back when this project had been nothing more than four hundred simulation runs and an inversion Ilse had barely finished explaining to a skeptical room. "You made a promise once," Iyoro said, looking at Ilse rather than at the Korvax. "That whatever we built would be allowed to ask honestly and be answered honestly, even when it was uncomfortable. Does accepting this gift change that promise in any way I need to understand before you say yes."

Ilse considered the question with the same seriousness she'd given it the first time, months ago, in a hearing room full of delegates deciding whether this project deserved to exist at all. "No," she said finally. "If anything, it makes that promise more possible to keep, not less. A mind that can hold its own history honestly, without either losing it or drowning in it, is closer to being able to answer honestly than anything we've built so far on our own. I don't think accepting this changes what we owe it. I think it might, for the first time, give us the tools to actually pay that debt rather than just intending to."

"Then I have no objection," Iyoro said. "I wanted it said out loud, in front of witnesses, before you answered. Not because I doubted you. Because some promises are worth repeating in front of the people who'll hold you to them later."

Perrin, who had spent the last hour taking notes with the focus of someone already drafting the charter amendment this moment was going to require, looked up from her tablet with an expression somewhere between exhaustion and something closer to disbelief. "Okonkwo-Marrow's committee has been deadlocked for months over whether this project's civilian mandate can even survive another resource request," she said. "I don't know how I'm supposed to write a memo explaining that the deadlock just became irrelevant because a previously unknown alien civilization dropped out of warp over a funeral and offered to fund the rest of the project unasked. I don't think there's a template for that."

"Write it plainly," Ilse said. "The same way I'd want you to write anything else true and difficult. Don't soften it into something more familiar than it is. The committee deserves the actual shape of tonight, not a version smoothed down to fit inside a memo they already know how to read."

"They're going to want proof," Hassoun said. "Understandably. A verbal offer from a ship that dropped out of the sky an hour ago isn't the kind of thing a funding board can act on without something more solid behind it."

"Then we'll give them something solid," the Korvax representative said, unbothered by the exchange happening around it as though skepticism were a familiar, even welcome, part of any first contact worth having. "Time, demonstration, whatever your own institutions require to trust what we're offering. We are not asking you to believe us tonight. We are asking you to let us begin proving it, starting now, for as long as proving it takes."

"We accept," Ilse said, before the silence could stretch long enough to become its own kind of answer. "Carefully, and with more questions than I currently have time to ask you tonight. But yes. We accept."

The Korvax representative inclined its head, an unmistakable gesture even across every difference between their two kinds of body, and Corin, standing at the edge of a room suddenly holding more hope than it had held in months, found herself thinking, unbidden, of a folding table three days earlier, and a single word offered almost as a joke, uncommitted, unclaimed, sitting now in the room like something that had been waiting the whole time to be picked back up and made real.

IX

Naledi found Corin on the same observation platform, later that night, after the delegates had gone, after the Korvax representative had returned to its own ship for what it called rest, though it offered no further explanation of what rest actually meant to a mind that considered itself many and one at once. The gas giant hung overhead in its familiar pale curve, indifferent, as it always was, to whatever the room below it had just decided.

"You didn't say much, in there," Corin said, without turning around.

"I was listening," Naledi said. "Trying to decide whether what I was hearing was the answer to three months of failure or a different, larger problem wearing an answer's clothes. I haven't finished deciding. I don't think I'm going to finish tonight."

"Which way are you leaning."

"Both," Naledi said, coming to stand beside her at the rail. "That's the honest answer, and I promised myself a long time ago I'd give you honest answers even when they're not clean ones. What they're offering solves something real. I watched them describe the exact shape of the problem Idris named weeks ago, the one none of us could find a material for, and they described it like people who'd already built the answer and were simply waiting for us to catch up to the question. That's not nothing. That might be everything we've been failing to reach for three straight months of this project's worst work."

"And the other half of your answer."

"The other half is that I don't yet know what it costs," Naledi said. "Everything this project has ever gained has cost someone something we didn't see coming until it had already happened. Tavik didn't know the black hole jump would be the thing that finally caught up with him when he agreed to fly through it. I didn't know the aggregate system would erase Resident 4471 more thoroughly than the mirror ever had until I watched it happen with my own eyes. I have no reason to believe a gift this large, arriving this precisely, on this exact night, is going to be the first thing this project has ever received for free. I just don't know yet what the bill looks like, or who it's going to come due for."

Corin was quiet for a while, watching the same slow turn of pale light she'd watched after every hard night this project had handed her, the mirroring failure, the aggregate's quiet erasure, the diamond substrate's frightening stability, and now this, stranger and larger than anything that had come before it. She thought of Kwame's old caution, small and elegant is easy, large and load-bearing is where things usually stop working the way the small version promised, and wondered whether it applied here too, to a gift this large, arriving this precisely, promising to finish work three months of failure had never managed to finish on its own. "I keep thinking about what Idris said, weeks ago," she said. "That the wall we kept hitting might be built into the shape of a single unified model trying to do two jobs with the same substrate. He was right. We just didn't have the material to prove it until tonight handed it to us, wrapped in an offer none of us fully understand yet."

"I keep thinking about the timing," Naledi said. "Not the cube. The moment they chose to arrive in. They could have come at any point in the last however many years they've apparently known this was coming. They chose tonight. The night we buried Tavik. I don't know if that's coincidence, or courtesy, or something about how they experience time that makes tonight and any other night functionally the same choice to them. I don't like not knowing which."

"Does it change your answer," Corin asked. "Whether it was deliberate."

"No," Naledi said. "I'd take the same tools either way, because the problem they solve is real regardless of why they chose to show up when they did. I just want to keep asking the question, even after I've stopped expecting a satisfying answer to it. That feels like the discipline that's kept us honest through everything else this project has thrown at us. I don't want to stop now, just because the news is finally good."

"I'll hold the question with you," Corin said. "For as long as it takes to get a real answer, or to accept that there isn't going to be one. I don't think either of us is built to just accept a gift this size without asking what it's actually made of, all the way down."

"That's the only reason I trust you to be standing here with me tonight instead of someone else," Naledi said. "Everyone else in that room is going to spend the next several weeks celebrating what this means for the charter fight, the funding, the timeline. Someone has to keep asking the harder question underneath all of it. I'd rather it be us than no one."

They stood together a while longer, saying nothing further, the gas giant turning its slow indifferent turn overhead, the two Korvax freighters sitting dark and quiet on the ground below where the settlement had said the hardest true thing it had said all year, only hours before something stranger arrived to answer a question nobody there had known enough yet to ask.

Outside, past the Anvil's walls, Corin thought, walking finally toward whatever passed for sleep on a night like this one, that she did not yet know whether the timing was a mercy or a wound dressed up as one, arriving exactly when it did, on the exact ground where the settlement had just finished burying a man who would never know what his own death had, in some way neither of them could yet fully articulate, helped bring through the door behind him.

She passed the workroom's window on her way home, dark for the first time in longer than she could easily remember, and understood, standing there a moment before continuing on, that whatever came next would not be decided tonight, or tomorrow, or in any single meeting a committee could schedule. It would take shape slowly, the way everything real on this project had always taken shape, one careful test at a time, one honest question after another, and she found, walking the last stretch home under a sky that now held two quiet foreign ships where only stars had been the night before, that she was almost grateful for how much work still remained. It meant there was still time to get it right.
