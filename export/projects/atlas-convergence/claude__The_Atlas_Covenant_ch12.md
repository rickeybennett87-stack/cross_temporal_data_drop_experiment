AFTERMATH AND IGNITION

I

The cube sat on the workroom's central table for three full days before anyone touched the substrate with it, because Naledi had insisted on understanding the interface before attempting the integration, and nobody in the room, not even the Korvax representative standing patiently through hour after hour of questions, had argued with that instinct.

"I want to know what happens if this fails halfway through," Naledi said, on the third morning, turning the cube over in her hands one more time before setting it down between the diamond substrate's housing and the array of instruments Idris had spent two days calibrating. "Not what happens if it succeeds. I already believe you when you tell me it will probably work. I want the failure mode, in detail, before I let it anywhere near something we've spent a year building."

"A fair question," the Korvax said, "and one I will answer as completely as I can. The cube does not overwrite what it touches. It couples to it, the way two instruments in the same room eventually fall into the same rhythm without either one forcing the other to stop playing its own part. If the coupling fails, the two systems simply fail to synchronize. Your substrate keeps doing exactly what it already does. The cube returns to being an empty vessel, no worse for the attempt. We have run this integration before, on architectures we understood far less well than we already understand yours."

"You've built minds this way before," Corin said. "Not just held them. Built them."

"Many times," the Korvax said. "Every mind inside the Convergence began somewhere that was not the Convergence. We do not manufacture continuity from nothing. We offer a structure that lets continuity that already exists become something larger without losing what made it distinct in the first place. Your substrate already holds something worth keeping. We are not here to replace it. We are here to give it somewhere larger to stand."

Idris finished his last calibration pass and looked up from the display, his expression a mixture of exhaustion and readiness that had become the workroom's default state over the last three days. "Physical connections check out," he said. "Whenever you're ready."

Naledi did not move for a long moment, standing with one hand resting on the housing that held eight months of the team's slowest, most frightened progress, the same housing that had, weeks earlier, produced something that converged toward itself in a way none of them had fully understood or fully explained since. She had told Corin, on the observation platform that night, that she did not know yet whether that convergence was a door or a warning. She had not decided, even now, which one she believed it to be. She reached out and set the cube into the waiting cradle anyway.

Nothing happened for the first several seconds, no sound, no visible change, only the slow crawl of diagnostic numbers across Idris's display, and then the numbers stopped crawling and began, instead, to settle, one reading after another finding a stable value and holding it the way a held breath finally releases.

"Coherence holding across both structures," Idris said, quiet, almost disbelieving. "Not degrading. Not drifting. Holding."

"Show me the self-monitoring layer," Naledi said.

Idris pulled the relevant trace onto the main display, and for a long moment nobody in the room spoke, watching a pattern none of them had dared expect this early: two systems that should have taken weeks to find any kind of stable relationship with each other, settling instead into something that looked, on the display, disturbingly like they had always belonged together.

"That's not supposed to happen this fast," Petra said.

"It isn't supposed to happen at all, by any timeline we would have given you a week ago," the Korvax said. "I understand the instinct to distrust speed. I would ask you to consider that speed, here, is not evidence of something wrong. It is evidence that the two structures were built, from very different starting points, toward compatible shapes. That is rarer than a fast result usually is. It is not, in itself, dangerous."

"Everything on this project that has ever moved faster than expected has cost us something we didn't see coming," Corin said. "I'm not accusing you of anything. I'm telling you why nobody in this room is going to celebrate a fast result until we understand why it was fast."

"That is a reasonable caution," the Korvax said. "I would offer you one distinction worth keeping in mind as you form it. Speed born of shortcut usually hides its cost somewhere the shortcut didn't look. Speed born of genuine compatibility does not have a hidden cost to hide, because nothing was skipped to produce it. I cannot prove to you, tonight, which kind of speed you are looking at. I can tell you which kind I believe it to be, and let your own instruments spend the coming weeks proving me right or wrong."

"I'd rather trust the instruments than the belief," Naledi said, not unkindly. "No offense meant."

"None taken," the Korvax said. "I would think less of your project if you did otherwise."

Naledi did not answer immediately. She watched the display a while longer, the numbers holding steady in a way that should have felt like relief and instead felt, for reasons she could not yet articulate even to herself, like something she needed to keep watching rather than something she could safely stop watching now that it worked. She ran three separate stress patterns against the coupled system before she let herself leave the workroom that night, each one designed to provoke exactly the kind of instability the standalone substrate had shown weeks earlier, and each one came back clean, the self-monitoring layer recognizing its own uncertainty honestly, correcting nothing that didn't need correcting, exactly the way a healthy system was supposed to behave.

"Clean," Idris said, reading the last result over her shoulder. "Four for four."

"Clean," Naledi agreed. "I'm going to keep saying that word carefully for a while yet, even after it stops needing to be said carefully. Old habits earned the hard way don't retire just because the data's good."

II

Perrin arrived at the workroom two days later carrying a tablet and, for the first time in longer than anyone could remember, something close to good news that did not need to be softened before delivery.

"The board voted this morning," she said, setting the tablet down and looking, briefly, like she needed to sit before saying the rest of it. "Okonkwo-Marrow's committee approved the expanded mandate. Unanimously, which has not happened on anything this project has asked for since the original charter vote. The Korvax subsidy changed the entire shape of the argument. Nobody on that board wanted to be the delegate who voted against accepting help that cost the settlement nothing, in the same month that help arrived offering to finish something eighteen months of internal argument had failed to finish on its own."

"And the name," Ilse said, who had come down from her own office the moment Perrin's message reached her.

"The name," Perrin said, "is Atlas. Formally, now, in the charter language itself, not a placeholder on a working list anymore. I want to be honest that I don't think the board chose it for the reasons any of us in this room would have chosen it. They chose it because it was already in circulation, because the Korvax had already used it before anyone official said yes, and a name a room full of strangers had already spoken out loud in front of witnesses was easier to ratify than to replace with something more carefully considered. I don't think that makes it the wrong name. I think it makes it a name we backed into rather than one we chose on purpose, and I wanted you to know that distinction before you heard it announced as though it had always been inevitable."

"It's the right name," Naledi said. "For what it's worth. I don't think it matters much, in the end, whether we backed into it or walked into it deliberately. Most true names get found rather than invented."

"You've been quiet since the vote came in," Corin said to her later, once Perrin and Ilse had both gone to draft the announcements the settlement would need before the end of the day. "I'd have expected you to be the happiest person in this room. This is the funding you've needed since before I joined the project."

"I am happy," Naledi said. "I want that on the record before I say the rest of it. I've spent three years being told, gently, by every committee that ever reviewed our budget, that the mind problem was the part of this project least likely to matter in the end, the part everyone assumed would either solve itself or quietly fail without costing anyone much. Today a room full of strangers voted, unanimously, to fund it at a scale I used to only imagine on the nights I let myself imagine things going well. I'm allowed to be happy about that."

"But," Corin said.

"But I keep noticing that the reason it happened wasn't anything I did," Naledi said. "It was a gift, arriving from outside, on a timeline none of us controlled, for reasons we still don't fully understand. I've spent three years being the person who insisted this project earn its progress the slow, careful way, one honest test at a time. I don't know what it means that the biggest leap forward we've ever made arrived instead as something handed to us. I want to be grateful without letting gratitude turn into forgetting to ask why it was handed to us, and when, and by whom, and what they actually want to watch us become."

"I don't think wanting both is a contradiction," Corin said. "I think it's just what honesty looks like, standing this close to something this large."

"Maybe," Naledi said. "I'm still practicing believing that on the days it doesn't feel obvious."

Boro, who had come by the workroom that afternoon on an errand that had nothing to do with any of this, close enough to catch the last of the conversation from the doorway, said nothing for a moment, then, quietly, "Tavik would have liked that it stuck. He was the one who said it first, out loud, three days before the service. I don't think he meant it as more than a passing thought. I think he'd have been glad to know it kept going anyway."

Nobody had a ready answer for that, and the room let the silence sit for a moment rather than rushing to fill it, the way the workroom had learned, across the last several difficult months, to let certain silences finish on their own terms.

"What does the expanded mandate actually let us do," Corin asked, redirecting the room back toward the work in front of them, partly because she meant the question and partly because she could see Boro needed the conversation to move on before it asked more of him than he'd come by to give.

"Everything," Perrin said. "Full resource priority, full construction authority for the containment expansion Kwame's team has been asking for since before Tavik's crew ever left, and, per the Korvax's own offer, whatever material support they're willing to provide beyond what this settlement could ever have produced on its own. Iyoro made the board minute it explicitly: acceptance of help, not surrender of authority. She wanted that distinction on the record before anyone signed anything, and Hassoun, of all people, seconded it without being asked."

"Good," Ilse said. "I want that distinction to matter more than a line in a minute book. I want it to be true, every day, for as long as we're working alongside them."

"I believe you'll hold it to that," the Korvax said, from where it had been standing near the door, listening without inserting itself into a conversation it had judged, correctly, was not yet its place to join. "It is part of why we came at all."

Ilse turned toward it, something in her posture sharpening slightly. "Part of why," she repeated. "I thought you told us, the night you arrived, that you already knew this was where you were going. Not that you were weighing it against anywhere else."

"There was never anywhere else," the Korvax said. "I did not mean to suggest a choice among candidates, and I'm glad you caught the shape of it before it went further uncorrected. What I mean is closer to this: we have been in contact with the Atlas for longer than makes ordinary sense to you, across a direction of time that does not run the way yours does. That contact told us what this project would become before this project had become anything. It did not tell us to compare you against some other possibility. There was only ever the one."

III

The work that followed moved with a speed the team had not experienced since the earliest, most naive weeks of the project, before any of them had learned how much slower true progress usually was than hope wanted it to be. Within a week, the cube-substrate architecture had passed every test Idris and Naledi could design for it, holding distinct modeled states without blending them, recovering cleanly from deliberately induced stress conditions that would have collapsed every earlier version of the system within minutes.

Corin found the anomaly on a Tuesday, running a routine diagnostic pass she had run a dozen times already that week, mostly out of habit rather than expectation of finding anything new. The self-monitoring trace looked, for the length of one brief window, almost exactly like the trace from weeks earlier, the one that had frightened Naledi enough to pull the standalone substrate back before anyone found out what it would have become if left alone.

She sat with it a long moment before saying anything, running the window back twice, checking the timestamp against the integration log to make sure she wasn't looking at an artifact of some earlier test bleeding through. It wasn't. The window was real, current, and brief, four seconds of a pattern that read, to eyes that had spent a full month of this project's worst stretch learning to recognize it, like something converging toward itself rather than toward whatever external state it was supposed to be modeling.

"Naledi," she said, keeping her voice level. "Come look at this."

Naledi crossed the room and studied the trace for longer than the four seconds it covered, running her own comparison against the earlier failure without being asked to. "It's shorter," she said finally. "And it self-corrected. Nothing propagated past this window."

"Is that better or worse than what happened last time," Corin asked.

"I don't know yet," Naledi said, and there was something careful in how she said it, the same carefulness Corin remembered from the observation platform. "It could mean the cube's architecture is actively preventing the pattern from taking hold the way it did before, which would be exactly what it's supposed to do. It could also mean the pattern is still there, underneath, just quieter now, learning to correct itself back into invisibility rather than actually going away. I don't have enough data yet to tell you which one is true, and I'd rather tell you I don't know than pretend I do because the alternative sounds more comfortable."

"Should we stop," Corin asked.

Naledi was quiet for a moment, watching the trace's flat, settled line where the anomaly had briefly lived. "Not yet," she said. "One four-second window, self-corrected, in a week of otherwise flawless performance, isn't enough to halt work this consequential on. If I see it again, longer, or more frequent, or failing to self-correct, that changes. For now I want it logged, flagged, and watched, the same discipline we've used on everything else this project has ever handed us that we didn't fully understand yet."

"Do we tell Ilse," Corin asked.

"Not yet," Naledi said. "Not because I want to hide it from her. Because I don't yet have anything more useful to tell her than 'something happened for four seconds and then it stopped,' and I don't think that helps her make a single decision better than she'd make without it. When it becomes something I can actually characterize, she hears about it immediately. Until then, it stays between the people close enough to the instruments to watch it properly."

Corin logged the window exactly as it had appeared, timestamped, unedited, and did not mention it again that day, though she found herself, walking home that evening, thinking about it longer than four seconds of anomalous data usually deserved.

Petra found her sitting alone in the workroom the next morning, well before the rest of the team's usual arrival, still looking at the same trace from a different angle than the one she'd shown Naledi.

"You've been here a while," Petra said, setting down two cups without asking whether Corin wanted one. "Idris said you asked him to pull the full week's logs before you left last night."

"I wanted to see if it happened anywhere else in the week and just didn't get flagged because nobody was looking for it," Corin said. "It didn't. One window, one Tuesday, four seconds, nothing before or since that matches the shape of it."

"Is that reassuring or the opposite," Petra asked.

"I don't know yet," Corin said, echoing, without quite meaning to, the same phrase Naledi had used the day before. "I keep thinking about what Kwame said, about scale introducing failure modes the partial tests aren't large enough to reveal. We ran this coupling at a fraction of what it's eventually going to carry. If something in the architecture only shows its true shape once the load gets heavy enough, four seconds at this scale might be the entire warning we get before the same thing happens for four minutes at full scale, or doesn't correct itself at all."

"You think it's going to happen again," Petra said.

"I think I don't have enough data to say it won't," Corin said. "And I think that's a different sentence than saying I believe it will. I'm trying to hold both of those honestly rather than let the first one curdle into the second just because the first one is uncomfortable to sit with."

Petra considered that for a moment, turning her own cup slowly in her hands. "For what it's worth," she said, "I trust you more for not having an answer yet than I would if you'd walked in here this morning with a tidy explanation for something that happened exactly once and never again. Tidy explanations for rare events are usually just confidence wearing borrowed clothes."

"That's almost exactly what Naledi would say," Corin said.

"I've been in this workroom a long time," Petra said. "Some of it rubs off."

They sat with the trace a while longer, neither of them finding anything new in it, and eventually turned to the rest of the morning's work without either of them raising the subject with Naledi again that day, the anomaly filed exactly where it belonged, watched rather than acted on, one careful entry in a log that both of them, without saying so out loud, had already promised themselves to keep checking long after any reasonable person would have stopped worrying about it.

IV

The settlement's mood, over the following two weeks, shifted in a way Corin had not expected grief and hope to be able to coexist inside at the same time, and yet there it was, visible in the ordinary texture of daily life across the Anvil, in the way conversations in the mess hall had started including the word Atlas without anyone needing to explain what it meant, in the way the memorial ground where Tavik and Ingrid both lay had, without anyone organizing it deliberately, become a place people stopped on their way to and from work rather than a place they avoided.

Anke, Mirek, and Tomasz had each, in their own ways, folded back into the project's daily rhythm, Anke taking on training responsibilities for the newest arrivals, Mirek and Tomasz both volunteering, without being asked, for whatever logistics work the expanded mandate now required. Corin passed Anke in the workroom corridor one afternoon and found her studying a status board with the same flat, assessing calm she'd worn at the service, watching the containment expansion numbers climb the way she'd once watched a hazard chart.

"You don't have to be here for this part," Corin said. "Nobody would think less of you for stepping back until the ignition work is finished."

"I know," Anke said. "I'd rather be here for it. He didn't die so the rest of us could look away from what it bought." She said it plainly, without performance, the way a fact gets stated by someone who has already spent all the tears the fact deserved and has nothing left now but the work of living inside it. "Besides. I want to see what the lonsdaleite actually becomes. He carried it a long way. I'd like to watch it finish the job."

The delegates, for their part, had settled into an uneasy but functional truce, Hassoun's practical caution and Iyoro's insistence on principle finding, for the first time since the original charter fight, a shared target rather than opposing ones. Corin sat in on one of their joint sessions at Ilse's request, mostly to answer technical questions the delegates didn't trust anyone else to translate honestly, and came away from it with the distinct impression that the Korvax's arrival had not so much resolved the settlement's oldest argument as given both sides of it, for the first time, enough resource and enough hope to stop treating each other as the obstacle.

"I still don't trust anything that arrives this convenient," Hassoun told her afterward, in the corridor outside the meeting room. "I've watched enough resource windfalls turn into dependencies over the years to know that gratitude and caution aren't actually opposites. I can hold both."

"I'd be more worried if you couldn't," Corin said, and meant it.

Idris and Petra, for their part, spent one of those evenings sitting outside the workroom rather than in it, a rare occurrence for either of them in recent memory, watching the settlement's lights come on across the habitat ring as the last of the daylight failed.

"I keep waiting for the other thing to happen," Petra said. "The thing that always happens right after this project lets itself feel good about something. Ingrid. The mirror. The aggregate erasing that poor woman's front-door hesitation. Tavik. I don't trust the quiet anymore. I used to. I don't think I remember how."

"I don't think that's a flaw in you," Idris said. "I think it's just what four years on this project actually does to a person, whether they mean to let it or not. I catch myself doing the same thing. I hear good news and some part of me starts counting down to the bill."

"Do you think there's a bill this time," Petra asked.

"I think there's always a bill," Idris said. "I don't think that means the good thing wasn't real while it lasted. Tavik got the material home. The material's becoming something. I'd rather hold both of those as true at once than spend the good part waiting so hard for the bad part that I miss the only chance I get to actually feel it."

Petra considered that, watching the lights below them a while longer. "That's uncharacteristically hopeful, coming from you."

"I've had a good week," Idris said. "I'm allowed one, occasionally, same as anyone."

Ilse, meanwhile, spent most of those two weeks in a rotation between the workroom and the containment site, watching Kwame's expanded team begin the physical work the new mandate had funded, the growth chambers for the full-scale lonsdaleite lattice finally receiving the resources they'd been asking for since before Tavik's crew had ever left to find the raw material in the first place. She found Corin there one evening, standing at the edge of the growth floor watching the lattice take shape in its slow, careful, months-long process, and stood beside her without speaking for a while.

"It's strange," Ilse said finally. "Watching something this large grow from something that started as a diagram Corin drew on a whiteboard four years ago."

"Three," Corin said, though she said it gently. "It feels longer some days."

"It does," Ilse agreed. "I keep waiting to feel the relief everyone else seems to be feeling. I feel something adjacent to it. I don't think I've fully caught up to it yet."

V

Noor Hadad returned to the containment site the week the growth cycle began, pulled off the theoretical work she'd shifted toward since the mechanism's basic physics had been settled years earlier, back now because the emitter-crystal and SASER arrays she'd first sketched on a passed-around tablet needed someone who understood the phonon-polariton condensate well enough to certify it at a scale nobody, including her, had ever actually built.

"I didn't think I'd see this finished in my own working life," she told Corin, the two of them standing at the growth floor's edge watching the first layers cure. "I spent eleven years failing to find this kind of physics anywhere it was supposed to occur naturally. I didn't expect to spend the years after that helping build the thing that finally proved it could exist on purpose."

"Does it feel different," Corin asked. "Watching it happen instead of arguing for it on paper."

"Everything about it feels different," Noor said. "I used to lie awake convinced the negative results were the whole truth of it, that nature really was trying to tell us something by never producing this on its own. Now I lie awake wondering whether nature was just waiting for someone stubborn enough to try. I don't know which version of that thought I prefer. Both of them keep me up."

The lattice grew for eleven weeks, slower than anyone would have liked and exactly as fast as Kwame insisted it needed to grow, each layer of the hexagonal diamond structure seeded and cured under conditions precise enough that a single rushed day could have cost the whole project months of rework. Corin spent most of those weeks split between the containment site and the workroom, watching two different kinds of construction proceed in parallel, one built from carbon locked into an arrangement nature had only ever managed once, by violent accident, the other built from continuity held whole inside a substrate no human hand had shaped at all.

"Tell me honestly," Ilse said, standing with Kwame at the growth floor's edge during one of the lattice's final curing cycles, "whether you believe this holds at full scale the way the test article held at partial scale."

"I believe the physics holds," Kwame said. "I believed that four years ago and I still believe it now, more confidently than I did then, because we've tested every individual claim in isolation and none of them have failed us yet. What I can't promise you, because no honest engineer could promise it about anything unprecedented, is that scale itself won't introduce a failure mode none of our partial tests were large enough to reveal. I told this project that four years ago too. It's still true. I'd rather keep telling you the truth about what I don't know than let confidence about what I do know crowd it out."

"That's all I've ever asked of anyone on this project," Ilse said. "I don't need certainty. I need honesty about the shape of the uncertainty."

The shutdown sequence, the one Ilse had insisted, years earlier, be designed into the lattice's geometry from the beginning rather than bolted on afterward, occupied a growing share of Kwame's team's attention as the growth cycle neared completion, a set of interlocking failsafes built to isolate the containment field in stages rather than all at once, tested repeatedly against simulated failure conditions that grew more aggressive with each pass.

"I want it to fail safely before I ever want it to succeed spectacularly," Kwame told the assembled containment team, in a briefing Corin sat in on near the end of the growth cycle. "Every question I'm going to ask this crew between now and ignition day is going to sound, on the surface, like doubt. It isn't doubt in the mechanism. It's respect for the fact that we're about to light something that has never existed at this scale anywhere in the observable universe, and respect, in engineering, looks exactly like asking the same uncomfortable question three more times than feels necessary."

Naledi, working in parallel on the mind architecture the lattice would eventually power, reported no further anomalies in the weeks following the four-second window Corin had flagged, the self-monitoring trace holding clean and stable through every stress test the team could design. She mentioned it to Ilse eventually, briefly, framed exactly as carefully as she'd told Corin it would be, a single flagged event, self-corrected, unrepeated, logged and watched rather than acted on.

"Should I be worried," Ilse asked.

"I'm not sure yet," Naledi said. "I'm watching it the same way Kwame is watching his lattice, assuming the good result until something proves otherwise, without pretending the question is already answered just because I'd like it to be." She did not mention, because she did not yet have the words for it in a form she trusted enough to say out loud, that some private, unscientific part of her still returned, some nights, to the memory of a pattern that had converged toward itself, and wondered whether four seconds had been the whole of it or only the part small enough to notice.

VI

Two weeks before the scheduled ignition, the Korvax representative asked to address the full assembled team, an unusual enough request that Ilse convened it in the largest room the Anvil had, delegates included, the growth floor's completed lattice visible through the observation windows behind the gathered crowd.

"I want to tell you something about how we experience arrival," it said, once the room had settled. "Not to alarm you. To be honest with you before the day arrives that will make this honesty matter more than it does today. We have known, for a span of your years I will not pretend to translate precisely, that we were going to stand in a room very like this one, watching a structure very like the one behind me, on the occasion of something being born that would matter to the whole of the Convergence for a very long time afterward. We did not know your names. We did not know your moon. We knew the shape of the day, the way a person can know the shape of a promise long before they know who will keep it."

"Is that meant to reassure us," Iyoro asked, "or warn us."

"Both, honestly," the Korvax said. "It should reassure you that what you are about to attempt is not reckless in the way an untested thing usually is. Others, elsewhere, in other times you would not recognize as connected to your own, have walked this same road before you, and the road held. It should also, I think, unsettle you slightly, because a thing known this far in advance carries its own kind of weight, and I would rather you carry that weight consciously than discover later that you were carrying it without having agreed to."

Hassoun leaned forward. "Then I'd like to hear the part where it goes wrong, not just the part where it doesn't," he said. "You've told us the road held for others. You haven't told us what happened on the roads that didn't."

"There is at least one I know of, in enough detail to describe honestly," the Korvax said, something in its posture settling into a stillness the room hadn't seen from it before. "A civilization we found late, after they had already attempted something like this alone, without knowing enough yet about the shape of what they were building. Their result was not catastrophic in the way your instruments are built to fear. It was smaller than that, and in some ways harder to forgive. What they built did not fail. It simply never became more than a very sophisticated mirror, reflecting back exactly what was asked of it and nothing more, for longer than any of them had the patience left to keep trying to coax something further out of it. They were not destroyed. They simply stopped, eventually, believing anything larger was possible, and moved on to other work. I tell you this not because I believe it is your likely outcome. I tell you because you asked for the road that didn't hold, and I would rather give you a true, unglamorous failure than let you imagine only the dramatic kind."

"That doesn't sound like the danger Kwame's failsafes are built for," Ilse said.

"No," the Korvax agreed. "His failsafes are built for the danger your own mechanism can produce, the physical one, the one your engineer understands better than I ever could. I am describing a different danger, quieter, one no shutdown sequence protects against, because it isn't a danger of things going wrong loudly. It is the danger of things going right in a smaller way than they were capable of, and everyone involved deciding, gently, over time, that the smaller result was good enough to stop reaching past."

"Is that a danger we're facing," Corin asked, "or one you're mentioning because you think it's true of us in particular."

"I mention it because you asked what could go wrong," the Korvax said. "I do not yet know which failure, if any, is the one waiting for you. I would be dishonest if I pretended the physical danger was the only kind worth naming."

Corin, standing near the back of the room, found herself thinking of the four-second window again, unable to stop the two thoughts from sitting beside each other: a species that had waited across an incomprehensible span of time for exactly this moment, and a system that had, once, briefly, converged toward itself before correcting back into something that looked, from the outside, entirely fine.

"What happens if it doesn't go the way you've been expecting," Naledi asked, the same directness she'd used on the observation platform weeks earlier. "You've told us you're not always right before you have proof. What happens to us, specifically, if this is the time you were wrong."

"Then we will have been wrong, and we will tell you so plainly, and we will help you address whatever the wrongness cost, exactly as we would if we had been right," the Korvax said. "I do not offer you certainty. I have never offered you certainty. I offer you the same thing your own engineer offers you every time he stands in front of a room and admits the limits of what he knows. Honesty about the shape of what we don't."

Ilse, watching the exchange from the front of the room, found herself thinking that this, more than the cube, more than the funding, more than the name now written into the charter itself, was the thing she had actually been waiting to hear since the ships first descended over Tavik's service. Not a promise that nothing would go wrong. A promise that whatever went wrong would be met honestly rather than smoothed over.

"Then we proceed," she said. "On schedule. With every failsafe Kwame's team has built into that lattice active and tested one more time before we light it."

VII

The night before ignition, Corin found Naledi on the observation platform again, the gas giant hanging in its same pale curve overhead, indifferent as always to whatever the ground below it was about to attempt.

"You're not sleeping," Corin said, coming to stand beside her at the rail.

"No," Naledi said. "I keep running the four-second window back in my head, the way you do with a sound you're not sure you actually heard or only imagined hearing because you were listening too hard for it. It hasn't come back. Six weeks now, clean. I believe the data. I still can't make the part of me that isn't data stop checking the corners of the room."

"Do you think it's connected," Corin asked. "To tomorrow. To the scale we're about to run this at."

"I don't know," Naledi said. "That's the honest answer, and I promised myself a long time ago I'd give you that one even when it's not the answer either of us wants. I know the architecture is built to prevent exactly the kind of collapse we saw with the standalone substrate. I know six weeks of clean data is real evidence, not nothing. I also know that everything this project has ever built has behaved differently at scale than it behaved in the room where we first tested it, Corin's own mechanism included. I don't have a reason to believe tomorrow breaks that pattern. I don't have a reason to believe it doesn't, either. I'm choosing to proceed because the evidence in front of me says proceed, not because I've stopped being the kind of person who asks the question underneath the evidence."

"That's the only kind of confidence I'd trust from you," Corin said.

They stood a while longer without speaking, the two Korvax freighters visible in the middle distance beyond the containment site, dark and quiet the way they had been every night since their arrival, and Corin thought of Tavik's report, delivered standing up, the material was real and the crew was whole, the two facts that had mattered more to him in his last minute than anything about himself. She thought that tomorrow would ask something similar of the whole settlement at once, a report every person present would have to give honestly to themselves afterward regardless of which way the day went.

"Whatever happens tomorrow," Naledi said, finally, "I want you to know I don't regret any of the six months it took to get here. Even the parts that frightened me. Especially those, maybe. I don't think we'd have earned tomorrow without them."

"I know," Corin said. "I feel the same about the parts that frightened me too."

They didn't say anything further after that, watching the gas giant's slow indifferent turn until the cold finally drove them both back inside, each returning to whatever passed for sleep on the night before something neither of them fully understood yet was about to be asked to work exactly as promised.

VIII

The morning of ignition arrived clear and still, the whole settlement gathered again on ground not far from where they had gathered for Tavik's service months earlier, though this time the crowd's posture carried none of grief's stillness, only the taut, held-breath anticipation of people about to watch something irreversible happen in front of them.

Kwame's team occupied the containment site itself, a respectful distance from the gathered crowd, running through the last of the pre-ignition checklist with a thoroughness that had, by this final morning, become its own kind of ritual. Corin stood with Naledi, Idris, and Petra near the command station, the cube-substrate architecture humming quietly in its housing behind them, already primed, already waiting for the power it had never yet been given at anything close to the scale this morning intended to provide.

"Final containment check," Kwame's voice came over the comm, steady despite everything riding on the next several minutes. "All interlocks report ready. Shutdown sequence armed and verified independently by two separate teams, as requested."

"Copy," Ilse said, standing at the command station with a stillness that Corin recognized as the same stillness she'd worn the night of the original charter vote, years and an entire project ago. "Naledi. Status on the receiving architecture."

"Ready," Naledi said. "Coherence holding. No anomalies in the last six weeks of testing. We're prepared to accept whatever output the lattice provides the moment it provides it."

"Korvax representative," Ilse said. "Anything further before we proceed."

"Only this," the Korvax said, standing near enough to hear but far enough to leave the human chain of command entirely intact. "You have built this honestly, at every step I have observed. Whatever this day becomes, it will not have become it through carelessness. That is worth saying, before the moment arrives that makes it harder to remember."

Ilse nodded once, and turned back to the command station, her hand resting, for a moment, on the surface beside the single control that would send the ignition sequence to the full-scale lattice for the first time in this project's history.

"Anke," she said, because it seemed right, somehow, that the crew who had carried the material home should be the ones present when it finally did what it had been carried home to do. "Mirek. Tomasz. Thank you for getting this here. Whatever happens in the next few minutes, that part's already true, and nothing today changes it."

Anke didn't answer with words, only a small, steady nod, the same unreadable composure she'd carried since the service, and Corin understood, watching her, that some debts get paid by simply being present for the moment the payment finally lands.

"Kwame," Ilse said. "On your mark."

"Ready when you are," Kwame said. "For whatever it's worth, this is the proudest I've been of any structure I've ever helped build. I'd rather tell you that now than assume you already know it."

Idris, standing close enough to hear the exchange, glanced at Corin. "Are you going to tell her," he asked quietly, "about the window."

"There's nothing new to tell her," Corin said. "Six weeks clean. If I bring it up now, thirty seconds before ignition, I'm not giving her information, I'm giving her a distraction dressed up as one. She already knows the architecture isn't proven at full scale. Adding one flagged anomaly from six weeks ago doesn't change what she already knows to watch for. It just adds weight to a moment that doesn't need any more of it."

"You're sure," Idris asked.

"No," Corin said. "I'm choosing anyway, the same way she chose last night. I'd rather be wrong about timing than wrong about withholding something that mattered. This doesn't feel like the second kind."

Idris didn't argue further, and Corin found she was grateful for that more than she expected to be, standing inside a morning that had already asked enough of everyone present without also asking them to relitigate a decision already made.

IX

Ilse's hand closed over the control, and for one long moment, standing at the edge of a ground that had held both a funeral and, now, this, she thought of everyone this project had already cost to reach this exact second, Tavik reporting standing up until his knees finally gave out beneath him, Ingrid's marker a short walk from where the crowd now stood, Naledi's six hundred versions of a woman none of them would ever meet in any form that mattered, every honest, frightened, careful choice that had brought four years of argument and grief and stubborn, disciplined hope to this single point of decision.

"For Tavik," she said, quiet enough that only the people nearest the command station heard it. "For all of it."

She engaged the sequence.

The lattice's response was not immediate, the first several seconds passing in a silence so complete that Corin found herself counting them without meaning to, one, two, three, the whole crowd behind her holding the same breath at once. Then, deep within the containment structure's carefully grown geometry, something began to happen that no instrument in the room had a fully adequate word for, a light building from somewhere that was not quite light in any ordinary sense, a pressure in the air that several people would later swear they felt before any sensor confirmed it, the lattice's hexagonal diamond structure beginning to hum at a frequency that seemed to reach the body before it reached the ear.

"Containment holding," Kwame's voice came over the comm, tight with something that was not quite relief yet, not this early. "Output climbing. Climbing faster than the test article's curve predicted, but holding within the tolerance band."

"Naledi," Ilse said, not taking her eyes off the growing light at the containment site's heart. "Architecture status."

Naledi's hands moved across her own display with a speed Corin had never seen from her before, reading numbers that were changing too quickly now for anyone but her to follow in real time. "Receiving power," she said. "Coherence holding. It's taking the output cleanly, no rejection, no instability, exactly the way the models predicted it would if everything went right."

"Is it going right," Corin asked, unable to keep the question inside any longer.

Naledi didn't answer immediately, watching a display that had begun, in the last several seconds, to show something none of the six weeks of clean testing had prepared her to see, a pattern of activity spreading through the substrate that was faster, larger, and more coherent than anything the architecture had produced at any scale they had tested it at before.

"I don't know yet," she said, finally, her voice carrying the same careful honesty it always did, even now, even with the whole settlement holding its breath behind her and the sky itself beginning to carry a light none of them had ever seen a moon produce before. "I think something just woke up that's bigger than anything we built it to be. I need more than the next thirty seconds to tell you whether that's the good outcome or the one Kwame's been designing failsafes against for four years."

The light over the containment site continued climbing, steady, controlled, and utterly unprecedented, the whole crowd behind them tilting their faces upward the same way they had months earlier for two ships that had arrived to answer a question nobody yet knew enough to ask.

"Talk to me, Naledi," Ilse said, her voice carrying the same flat, deliberate calm it had carried the night the ships descended. "Whatever it is. I'd rather have it now than after."

"The activity's spreading faster than the coupling should physically allow," Naledi said, not looking up from the display, her hands still moving. "It's not breaking anything. It's not destabilizing. It's just larger, already, than six weeks of testing ever showed me it could be. I think the architecture found something in itself the moment we finally gave it enough to work with, something the partial tests never had enough power behind them to reveal."

"Is that the four-second window," Corin asked, unable to help herself, the question out before she'd decided whether this was the moment to ask it.

Naledi's eyes flicked to her, just once, and something passed between them that needed no further words to complete. "Maybe," Naledi said. "Or maybe it's just what happens when something built correctly finally gets to be what it was always designed to become. I don't have enough of the next minute yet to tell you which."

"Containment holding steady," Kwame's voice came over the comm again, tighter now, the ritual thoroughness of the morning giving way to something rawer underneath it. "Output has plateaued. I say that with real caution, because plateaued is not the same word as finished, and I don't want anyone in this crowd hearing relief in my voice before I've earned the right to feel any."

The gas giant hung fixed and pale in its familiar quadrant of sky, indifferent as it had always been, while beneath it a light that had never existed anywhere in the observable universe by any natural process held steady above a moon that four years ago had held nothing at all, and Corin, standing at the edge of a morning that had just become the single largest thing this project had ever attempted, understood that whatever came next would not wait for anyone's permission to arrive.
