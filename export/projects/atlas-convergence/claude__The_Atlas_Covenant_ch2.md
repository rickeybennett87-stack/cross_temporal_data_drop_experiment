BREAKING GROUND

I

Ilse and Corin were still on the observation gallery when the first work order arrived, neither of them having managed to leave the chamber in any real sense, though the boards had gone dark hours earlier and the corridor outside had long since emptied of everyone but the two of them and an unhurried exhaustion, the kind that comes from having just been handed something too large to carry to bed. Ilse had not answered the anonymous message yet. She would not answer it for a long time, would in fact never answer it publicly at all, but she found herself, in that gray hour before the station's cycle called it morning, reading it again on a private channel, you are going to build something and then discover you cannot control what it becomes, and understanding that the sentence had stopped being a warning about some distant future machine and had quietly become, sometime in the last several hours, a description of the morning she was already living in.

Nobody had thought to ask who was allowed to spend the money.

Ilse noticed it on the second morning, not the first, because the first morning had been consumed entirely by the disorienting exhaustion of having won. She and Corin had slept four hours in a supply berth neither of them had used in a year, woken because a steward knocked on the door to ask whether the resource allocation office needed her signature on something, and Ilse had said yes before she'd finished reading what the something was, because saying yes to paperwork felt, that morning, like the easiest kind of yes she would ever be asked for again.

It was Perrin who found the actual problem, on the second morning, standing in the doorway of the mess hall with a tablet held slightly away from her body the way a person holds something they already know is going to ruin someone's day.

"The Council ratified a mandate," Perrin said. "It did not ratify an org chart. There is no line in the charter that says who is legally the executive of this project. There's a line that says who oversees it, plural, a joint body, five settlements deep, and there's a line that says what it can and can't be used for. There is no line that says whose signature actually authorizes a procurement order over a certain threshold, and the threshold on a project this size is going to come up in about eleven days, when the first industrial contractor asks who they're supposed to bill."

Ilse looked up from the tablet she'd signed without reading. "I thought that was implied."

"Implied by what?"

"By the fact that I proposed it."

"You proposed a research theory," Perrin said, not unkindly, but flatly, in the voice of someone who had spent eleven months learning exactly how little kindness the Council's procedural apparatus extended to good intentions. "A theory doesn't sign contracts. A person signs contracts, or an incorporated entity does, and right now this project is neither. It's a ratified resolution and a resource figure and forty pages of oversight language, and not one sentence of it says that you, specifically, are the person a contractor calls when a shipment of prefab housing doesn't show up on schedule."

Corin, three seats down, had stopped eating. "So who is."

"That's the question I'm bringing you," Perrin said. "Nobody, currently. The Council assumed, because every prior resolution of this scale had assumed it, that the proposing party would already have some kind of standing institutional structure behind them, a university, a settlement government, a corporate research division, something with an accountable legal officer already in place before the vote ever happened. You didn't have one of those. You had a mess hall and a whiteboard and eleven months of drafting, and it worked, because the theory was strong enough to carry itself through the hearing on its own. It is not going to carry itself through a procurement dispute. Procurement disputes don't care how elegant your recursion proof is."

Ilse set down the tablet. She had spent three years learning to feel the vertigo of a simulation returning a result she hadn't expected, and this had the same shape, a floor she'd assumed was load-bearing turning out to be assumed rather than verified.

"Then we build the structure," she said.

"You build it in eleven days," Perrin said, "or the first contractor walks, and every contractor after them hears why, and you spend the next six months rebuilding a reputation you never got to finish earning in the first place."

II

The eleven days that followed were, Ilse would say later, harder in an unglamorous way than the eleven months that had preceded them, because the eleven months had at least been a fight she understood the shape of. This was a fight against a category of problem she hadn't known existed until Perrin named it: the problem of how a thing that had just been granted permission to exist actually began existing, procedurally, in a system that had never had to accommodate anything quite this shape before.

They incorporated something. Perrin insisted on the word something, in the drafting sessions, because none of the existing legal categories fit cleanly. Not a corporation, because a corporation implied shareholders and a profit motive the charter explicitly forbade. Not a settlement authority, because it governed no settlement and no population. Not a research institute, in the traditional sense, because no existing research institute had ever been granted resource allocation at planetary scale. Perrin spent four of the eleven days in closed correspondence with the Council's registrar, arguing for a new category, provisional charter trust, that had existed on paper for eight years without ever being used, created for exactly this kind of edge case and never once tested because nothing had ever actually needed it.

"You're the first thing that's ever needed this category," Perrin told them, on the fifth day, with something that might have been satisfaction and might have been exhaustion. "The registrar had to look it up. I don't think they believed it was real until I sent them the statute number."

Ilse became, on paper, the trust's accountable executive, a title that meant, in practice, that her name went on every document large enough to require one, and that if something went catastrophically wrong with a shipment or a contract or a workforce dispute, the legal exposure ran through her specifically rather than diffusing into an institution large enough to absorb it. Corin became technical director, a title that carried less legal weight and more actual authority over anything that touched engineering, an asymmetry neither of them had planned but that felt, once it existed, like the only honest arrangement available: Ilse's name on the risk, Corin's judgment on the work.

"You realize what this makes you," Corin said, reading the final incorporation documents on the eighth day, in the same converted mess hall that had by now hosted every important conversation of the project's short existence.

"What."

"The single most legally exposed person in this quadrant, over a project that doesn't have a building yet."

"I've been the most exposed person in this quadrant since run four hundred and twelve," Ilse said. "This just made it official."

Tavik, reading over both their shoulders, said nothing for a while, and then said the thing Ilse would remember longest out of the entire eleven days. "Verdent had an accountable executive," he said. "Took the Council four years to determine who, exactly, that had been, because the actual authority had been so diffused across three separate atmospheric engineering firms that nobody could be made to answer for the outcome. I watched the hearings. Nobody went to prison. Nobody lost a license. A hundred and forty thousand people died and the closest anyone came to accountability was a joint statement of regret issued by a consortium that dissolved itself eighteen months later specifically so it couldn't be sued." He looked at Ilse for a long moment. "I would rather you carry this than watch it disappear into the same kind of fog. I mean that as something closer to a gift than a warning, though I understand it won't feel like one most days."

The first test of what the incorporation actually meant came on the ninth day, before the paperwork had even finished its final registrar review, when a prefab-housing contractor out of the Compact's shipyards missed a delivery window by six days and then, when Sefu's predecessor in logistics called to ask why, simply stopped answering the channel.

"They think there's nobody to sue," Perrin said, reading the contractor's silence correctly within an hour of hearing about it. "They've done this before, to smaller outfits, projects without a clear legal spine. Miss a delivery, absorb the penalty as a cost of doing business, and by the time anyone's assembled the standing to actually pursue it, they've moved on to the next contract. It works, against most buyers this size, because most buyers this size don't have anyone whose name is personally on the exposure."

"Does it work against us," Ilse asked.

"Not if I file today," Perrin said, "under your name, citing the trust's registered standing, which as of yesterday afternoon is now a matter of Council record rather than a pending application. I want you to understand what that means before I send it. Your name goes on a formal contractual dispute, in a public registry, on the project's ninth day of legal existence. If we lose, it costs the project standing with every contractor watching how this plays out. If we win, and I believe we will, because the delivery terms were unambiguous and their silence is itself close to an admission, it tells every contractor watching that this project has teeth it didn't have a week ago."

"File it," Ilse said, and did not sleep well that night, not from doubt about the decision but from the still-unfamiliar weight of watching her own name become an instrument other people would use to measure whether this project was real.

They won the dispute in eleven days, a decision that arrived, fittingly, on the same day the trust's own incorporation finished its last formal review, and Perrin brought her the ruling in person rather than through a channel.

"Word travels fast in that industry," Perrin said. "By next week, every contractor bidding on this project's remaining work will know exactly what happens if they treat this the way they've treated smaller outfits before. I wish I could tell you that's the last time you'll need me to weaponize your own name on your behalf. It won't be. It's just the first time, and I wanted you to see one go well before the harder ones start arriving."

It did not feel like a gift, most days, over the eleven days that followed. It felt like the weight of being the only name a lawsuit could actually reach, and Ilse carried it the way she'd carried the resource figure the night of the vote, not lightly, not with any pretense of lightness, but with the settled understanding that she had asked for this, specifically, on the night she'd written a number down and refused to delete it.

III

The site search had technically begun months earlier, buried in a subclause of the charter's ninth draft that specified the project required an unclaimed world or moon of sufficient scale and isolation to host construction at the scope the mandate described, and Ilse had not thought about that subclause seriously until the incorporation was finished and someone finally had the legal standing to actually purchase a survey.

There were, according to the Council's unclaimed-registry, eleven candidates within a range the project's initial logistics could support without requiring a supply chain longer than anyone was prepared to maintain for a decade. Ilse reviewed all eleven with a survey team assembled hastily out of engineers who had, until eleven days earlier, been employed by three different settlements and were now, awkwardly, employed by a legal category that had not existed the week before.

Nine of the eleven were rejected within the first pass, for reasons that had nothing to do with the project's actual requirements and everything to do with the fact that unclaimed didn't mean unwanted. Two were marginal cases, worlds nobody had settled yet but that multiple settlements had filed preliminary interest in, worlds with breathable atmospheres or workable soil or mineral deposits someone was already quietly planning to claim once their own resource situation stabilized. Taking either of those, Corin pointed out in the third review session, would mean building the most dangerous unproven technology anyone had ever attempted directly on top of a world someone else was going to want to live on eventually.

"We're not doing that," Ilse said, before anyone else had finished the thought out loud. "Whatever this project costs, it isn't going to cost someone a world they were going to live on."

Ilse walked the surface of one of the two marginal candidates herself, in the second week of the search, a small world with thin but breathable air and soil a preliminary agricultural survey had rated promising, because she did not trust herself to reject it on paper without first standing on it. A settlement liaison from the Compact met her there, a man who had clearly been told very little about what the visit was actually for, and who spent most of the walk describing, with the pride of someone who had spent years angling for exactly this outcome, the families his settlement hoped to relocate here within the decade, once the resource situation allowed it.

She did not tell him what she was actually there to evaluate. She thanked him for the tour, filed the world as unsuitable that evening, and did not need Corin's argument about proximity to a living population to explain to herself why. She had stood on ground a family somewhere was already imagining a house on, and she had known, standing there, that she could not authorize building the most dangerous unproven mechanism her species had ever attempted anywhere near a floor someone else had already started laying plans across, whatever the survey's mineral report said about its convenience.

That left the dead moon.

It orbited a gas giant in a system with no habitable worlds at all, no atmosphere of its own, gravity thin enough that construction would require modified equipment but not so thin that it created engineering problems nobody could solve, and a surface geology stable enough, according to the survey team's preliminary read, to support a structure of significant mass without the kind of seismic risk that would have ruled it out immediately. Nobody had ever filed interest in it. Nobody, the survey lead said flatly, in the tone of someone delivering a fact rather than an opinion, had ever wanted it, because there was nothing on it worth wanting: no atmosphere to terraform, no minerals worth the extraction cost at this distance from any settled market, no reason for anyone to have looked at it twice in the two centuries since the survey drones first catalogued it and moved on.

"It's perfect," Corin said, at the fourth review session, and then, catching the way the word landed in the room, "I don't mean that the way it sounds."

"I know what you mean," Ilse said.

Nobody said the other thing out loud, not in that session, not for another two weeks, though Ilse understood, reading the survey data alone that night, that everyone in the room had already done the arithmetic silently and arrived at the same number she had: a dead moon meant that if the containment mechanism they hadn't designed yet failed catastrophically, whatever it did to the ground beneath it, it would not do to a world anyone was living on. It would not produce a second Verdent. It would produce, at worst, a crater on a rock nobody had ever wanted, and the fact that this calculation existed at all, unspoken, underneath the site selection, was the first moment Ilse understood, in her body rather than in the abstract language of the charter, exactly how much danger she had actually agreed to build.

She did say it out loud, eventually, to Tavik, alone, three weeks into the site survey, walking the perimeter of what would become the first construction boundary in an exosuit rated for atmospheres this moon didn't have.

"We picked it because nobody wanted it," she said. "That's the official reason. I keep thinking about the other reason. The one nobody's put in a report yet."

"Say it," Tavik said.

"If this goes wrong the way Verdent went wrong, it goes wrong here, on a rock with no atmosphere and no population, instead of somewhere two hundred thousand people are already living. I picked a place to fail safely before I've even built the thing that might fail. I don't know what it says about me that I did the arithmetic on our own catastrophe before we'd laid a single foundation."

Tavik was quiet for a long moment, boots crunching against regolith that had never felt a human footstep before that week, ground so undisturbed that their own footprints, Ilse realized, would likely still be legible here centuries after everything they were about to build on top of them had either succeeded or failed. "It says you learned the actual lesson of Verdent," he said finally. "Not the lesson people usually take from it, which is don't attempt things that might fail. The lesson is: if you're going to attempt something that might fail, fail somewhere the failure doesn't cost you a world. I spent forty years wishing someone had done that arithmetic before the first settlers moved onto Verdent's first continent. You did it before you'd poured a single foundation. That's not something to feel ashamed of. That's the first time in this entire project I've watched you get ahead of a mistake instead of answering for one that already happened."

IV

Ground broke on a Tuesday, by the calendar the survey team used, though calendars meant less on a moon with no day-night cycle worth the name, no atmosphere to color a sky that didn't exist in any way a human eye would recognize as sky. The first crews arrived in transport vessels that had been repurposed from settlement relief logistics, retrofitted in the eleven days of incorporation to carry construction equipment instead of emergency supplies, and Ilse watched the first prefab habitat module lowered onto the surface from an observation deck that would, within the year, no longer exist, replaced by something permanent.

The regolith took the module's footing with a small, undramatic shudder that traveled up through the observation deck's structure and into the soles of Ilse's boots, the first physical sensation, she realized, that this project had produced in nine months of theory and law and argument, the first time any of it had actually pushed back against the ground rather than against a Council chamber or a contractor's silence or a whiteboard. A second module followed the first within the hour, and a third before the transport vessel had finished its unloading cycle, and by the time the observation deck's shift ended, six modules sat where nothing had ever sat, arranged in the loose provisional grid the site plan called a village and that looked, from above, like the first sketch of something that did not yet deserve a better word. Ilse watched a small crew of new arrivals gather near the second module's entrance afterward, still in transit gear, one of them crouching to press a bare gloved hand flat against ground that had never held anyone's weight before, the way a person touches something to convince themselves it's actually real rather than the idea of a thing they'd signed a contract to believe in.

She had expected, going into that morning, to feel something like triumph. What she felt instead was closer to vertigo, watching a crane operator she had never met lower a structure onto ground that had been undisturbed for longer than her species had existed as a species, and understanding, in a way the charter's language had never quite made concrete for her, that everything happening below her was now, legally and practically, her responsibility in a way it had not been the week before.

"You look like you're going to be sick," Corin said, arriving beside her with two cups of something that was technically coffee and functionally closer to a caffeine delivery system than an actual beverage.

"I keep doing the math on how many people are down there," Ilse said. "Two hundred, this week. The projections say we'll be past four thousand within the year, past twelve thousand within three. I have never managed more than the six people in that mess hall. I don't know how to be responsible for twelve thousand people I've never met, on a project I designed on paper, in a room, with a whiteboard, believing the hardest part would be the mathematics."

"The mathematics was the hardest part you could do alone," Corin said. "This is the hardest part that requires everyone else, and you're not going to do it alone, because you already built the structure that makes sure you can't. Perrin didn't spend eleven days inventing a legal category so you could carry twelve thousand people by yourself. She invented it so there'd be a chain of people underneath you who could actually carry pieces of this, the way you carried pieces of the theory to me before you ever brought it to Tavik. You didn't do that eighteen months alone either. You just remember it that way because you were the one standing at the podium."

Ilse watched the crane release its cargo, watched the first module settle into ground that had never held weight like this before, and thought about the anonymous message she still hadn't answered, you are going to build something and then discover you cannot control what it becomes, and understood, watching twelve thousand projected workers become a real and immediate number instead of a line item in a resource allocation document, that the loss of control had started well before any machine existed to lose control of. It had started here, on a construction site, the moment the project stopped being an idea two people could hold in their heads and became a thing large enough to need people neither of them had ever met.

V

The workforce scaling problem arrived faster than the survey team's projections had suggested, because the projections had been built on models of prior construction efforts at a tenth of this scale, and nobody, Corin pointed out grimly in the second month, had ever actually tried to model what happened to hiring logistics when the underlying project was this large and this unprecedented.

They hired an operations director within the first six weeks, a woman named Sefu Ilunga who had spent fifteen years running settlement infrastructure logistics and who took the job, she told Ilse frankly in her first week, because she had read the charter twice and found herself unable to walk away from a project that had actually written down, in binding language, what it owed to the thing it was building.

"Most projects this size," Sefu said, in a status meeting in the fourth month, standing in front of a wall covered in workforce allocation charts that had already been redrawn three times that week, "you scale the workforce against a fixed set of known tasks. You know what the tasks are because someone's built something like this before. We don't have that. Every time engineering finalizes a subsystem, it turns out to require three trades nobody budgeted labor for, because nobody's ever built this subsystem before, at this scale, under these constraints. I've been doing this job fifteen years and I have never once had to invent a job category mid-project because the thing being built genuinely didn't exist as a category before we started building it."

"Give me an example," Ilse said.

"Containment fabrication," Sefu said. "Corin's team needs people who can work lab-grown structural lattice at industrial scale, and there is no existing trade for that, because nobody has ever tried to grow a structural lattice at industrial scale before. We're not hiring people with the skill. We're hiring people with adjacent skills and training them into a skill that doesn't have a name yet, and every week that training takes is a week the schedule slips, and every week the schedule slips is a week the Council's oversight committee gets a little more nervous about a project that isn't producing anything visible yet except larger and larger habitat modules and no actual machine."

Corin, at the far end of the table, hadn't spoken in several minutes, and Ilse recognized the quality of that silence, the same silence Corin had used in the mess hall eighteen months earlier before finding the third hole in the original proposal.

"Say it," Ilse said.

"We don't actually know how to build this yet," Corin said. "Not the containment. The power source underneath the containment. We've been so consumed by the org chart and the site and the workforce that nobody in engineering has said this plainly in a status meeting yet, and I think it needs to be said plainly: we do not currently have a power architecture capable of running a simulation at the scale the charter commits us to. We have theoretical requirements. We do not have a mechanism. Every other problem on this table is a hard problem with a known category, hiring, logistics, legal structure, things humanity has solved before at smaller scale and can solve again at this one, slowly, painfully, but solve. The power problem isn't in a known category. Nobody has ever needed to solve it before, because nobody has ever needed this much sustained, stable output in a form dense enough to run what Ilse is proposing to run. I've spent the last month reading every energy research archive I have clearance for, and I keep hitting the same wall: everything that's been tried at anything approaching this scale was tried, and it didn't work, and it was quietly wound down a generation ago, and nobody's picked it back up since, because nobody's needed to until now."

The room went quiet the way rooms went quiet when someone said the thing everyone had been circling without naming.

"How far are we from needing an answer," Ilse asked.

"Construction of the containment shell doesn't require an answer for another eight months, maybe ten," Corin said. "Construction of everything the containment shell has to actually contain requires an answer before we pour a single additional foundation past that point. We have runway. We don't have a plan, and I want that on the record in this meeting, not buried in a private conversation between the two of us three months from now when the schedule's already committed us to a shape we can't change."

Sefu's answer to the trades that had no name yet was, in the end, characteristically direct: she built a training bay out of the fourth habitat module, staffed it with the six engineers on Corin's team who best understood what the eventual containment structure would need to be, and told them to teach a discipline into existence one cohort at a time, ahead of the design that discipline would eventually serve.

Ilse sat in on the first cohort's opening session, uninvited, in the back row, because she had told herself she needed to see, firsthand, what her own promise actually required of the people building toward it. A structural fitter named Ingrid Bakke, twenty years into a career built on knowing exactly what a structure was for before she trusted her hands to it, asked the question that would recur through every cohort that followed.

"You're asking us to grow a material nobody's grown before, into a shape nobody's finalized yet, to hold something you haven't finished designing," Ingrid said, not hostile, just precise. "How do we know we're doing it right, if the thing we're supposed to be checking our work against doesn't exist yet either?"

Boro Adeyemi, the quiet materials engineer running the session, did not pretend the question had an easy answer. "You don't," he said. "Not yet. What you're learning this month isn't the final process. It's the discipline the final process will eventually be built out of: how carbon-monofilament structures actually grow, how they fail, how you read a growth pattern going wrong before it's gone all the way wrong. When the geometry's finalized, you'll already know the material well enough to trust your hands on it the same day you learn the new shape, instead of needing another six months to learn the material from nothing. I know that's not the answer that lets you sleep easily tonight. It's the only honest one I have."

Ingrid nodded slowly, the nod of someone settling for a true answer over a comfortable one, and Ilse thought of Iyoro's question in the hearing, what is owed to something built for a purpose it didn't choose, and recognized its nearer, plainer cousin: what was owed to four thousand people, soon twelve thousand, training toward a shape that did not yet exist, on the strength of a promise that it eventually would.

VI

It became, over the following weeks, the thing every other conversation eventually circled back to, the way a held breath eventually becomes the only thing a person can think about. The workforce kept scaling. Sefu kept inventing job categories that hadn't existed the month before. The habitat modules kept multiplying across the moon's dead surface, transforming what had been an unmarked expanse of regolith into something that looked, from the observation platform, increasingly like an actual settlement, lights and traffic and the hum of thousands of people living somewhere they had no biological business surviving without machinery keeping every one of them alive. And underneath all of it, in Corin's team's working sessions, in the growing stack of dead-end research summaries piling up on the whiteboard that had migrated with them from the mess hall to an actual engineering annex, the power question sat, unmoving, unsolved, resistant to every approach anyone on the team had brought to it.

"We keep reaching for words we haven't needed before," Corin told Ilse, in the fifth month, both of them standing in front of that whiteboard at an hour neither of them tracked anymore. "Unprecedented. Never attempted. No existing category. I've used all three phrases in status reports this month, and every time I write one down I feel like I'm admitting something I should have caught earlier: we scoped this project against the mathematics of what a simulation this size requires, and we never actually verified, before the Council vote, that a power source capable of feeding that mathematics was a thing that could exist. We proved the demand side. We never proved the supply side. We built an eleven-month charter, a legal structure, a construction site, a workforce in the thousands, all of it resting on an assumption that somewhere in the supply side, an answer existed, because it had to, because the alternative was too large to hold in your head while you were fighting the resource case in front of the Council."

Ilse looked at the whiteboard, at the accumulated dead ends: zero-point vacuum extraction, promising on paper, wound down a generation ago when the actual yield never scaled past laboratory curiosity. Casimir-effect containment, elegant in theory, the same story, real interest, real funding, real quiet abandonment once the numbers refused to grow past a scale too small to matter. Every approach humanity had actually tried, at any point in its recorded research history, for generating sustained power at a density approaching what this project needed, had been tried, had produced something real and measurable and interesting, and had stopped there, permanently, because interesting and measurable was not the same as usable at scale, and nobody had ever needed it to be, until now.

She had watched the Casimir line die herself, three weeks earlier, in a lab annex Corin's team had built out of a repurposed cargo module, standing behind a containment window while a junior researcher named Halden Otieno ran what everyone in the room already suspected would be the line's final test. The rig was small, plates separated by a gap measured in nanometers, instruments calibrated to catch a yield too small for anything but the most sensitive detection anyone had access to, and for eleven seconds it worked exactly the way the theory said it should, a real and measurable extraction of energy from the vacuum itself, proof that the underlying physics was not a fantasy.

"There it is," Halden said, quietly, watching the readout. "There's the effect. It's real. It's always been real."

"How much," Corin asked, already knowing the answer from the shape of the readout, asking anyway because someone in the room needed to say the number out loud.

"Enough to power a light for about four seconds," Halden said. "At a scale we could probably grow by two, maybe three orders of magnitude with another decade of engineering. We need nine orders of magnitude beyond where this rig sits today, and I don't have a decade, and I don't think nine orders of magnitude is a distance any amount of decades closes, not with this mechanism. The effect isn't the problem. The effect is real. The problem is that the effect doesn't want to scale, structurally, no matter how good the engineering around it gets. It's not a wall we haven't climbed yet. I think it might be a wall built out of the actual shape of the physics."

Nobody in the room argued with him. Corin logged the result, closed the file on the Casimir line formally that same afternoon, the second closed line in as many months, and told Ilse that evening that the closing of a real, honestly tested approach felt worse, somehow, than the mess hall's original uncertainty ever had, because uncertainty had at least still contained the shape of hope. A closed line contained only the documented fact of one more direction that wasn't the answer.

"I don't have an answer for you tonight," Ilse said. "I don't think either of us is going to find one standing in front of this board at an hour we should have stopped tracking three hours ago."

"I know," Corin said. "I'm not asking you for the answer tonight. I'm asking you to sit with the fact that I don't currently believe the answer exists in anything either of us already knows how to build. I think it's somewhere neither of us has looked yet, and I don't know how long it's going to take to find it, and I think, for the first time since the mess hall, I'm genuinely afraid we scoped a promise we can't keep."

Ilse thought about the charter clause Perrin had fought hardest for, the one binding the project to honesty even when the honest answer was uncomfortable, and understood that the clause had been written for exactly this moment, not for some future crisis involving the machine's own interiority, but for this: the plain, unglamorous admission, made to herself first and eventually to a Council that had already spent a planet's worth of resources trusting her, that she did not yet know how to build the thing she had promised them she could build.

"Then we say that," Ilse said. "Not to the Council yet. There's nothing to report yet except an open problem, and open problems aren't failures, they're the actual shape of research before it's finished being research. But we say it to each other, plainly, the way you said it to me in the mess hall eighteen months ago. We don't have an answer. We have a wall. I would rather stand in front of a wall honestly than pretend, to either of us, that it's a door we simply haven't opened yet."

Tavik had said little in the status meetings of the fifth and sixth months, a silence Ilse had noticed without commenting on, until he found her alone on the observation platform one evening, the same platform that had by then absorbed most of the project's hardest private conversations.

"I've been watching you build an org chart, a workforce, a legal structure, and a construction site," Tavik said, "and I've been sitting in rooms mostly saying nothing, because none of that is a thing I know how to help build. I spent my working life coordinating evacuations and survey commands, not incorporation documents. I don't think I'm useful to you standing in status meetings watching Sefu invent job titles."

"You're useful to me for reasons that aren't in a job title," Ilse said. "You're the reason half this Council believed we'd thought about the danger seriously enough to be trusted with it."

"That reason doesn't have work attached to it, most days," Tavik said. "I would like one that does, before restlessness turns into something worse. Corin's team is going to hit a materials problem eventually, underneath whatever the power question turns out to need, some resource this project doesn't currently have on hand and can't manufacture from nothing. When that happens, someone is going to need to go find it, out past anywhere charted, and I would rather that someone be a person who has spent a career running survey commands than a person learning the job for the first time under pressure. I'm not asking you for that assignment today. There's nothing to survey for yet. I'm telling you it exists, so that when the day comes, you remember I asked before you had to come looking for someone."

Ilse looked at him for a long moment, and thought, not for the first time, that Tavik had a way of naming a future need before anyone else in the room had finished recognizing it as a need at all, the same instinct that had handed the project its founding sentence eighteen months earlier without his quite meaning to.

"I'll remember," she said.

VII

The habitat count crossed four thousand workers in the sixth month, ahead of even Sefu's revised projections, and the moon that had held nothing but undisturbed regolith for longer than recorded history now held, spread across a construction footprint visible from orbit, the physical shape of a promise neither Ilse nor Corin yet knew how to finish keeping.

Ilse stood on the observation platform on the evening the count crossed four thousand, alone, in the hour she'd started reserving for the accounting she did with herself rather than with anyone else, and thought about the sentence Corin had said months earlier in the incorporation review, the single most legally exposed person in this quadrant, over a project that doesn't have a building yet, and understood that the building existed now, undeniable and enormous and full of people who had left settlements and families and entire lives to come work on something none of them, herself included, currently knew how to finish.

Corin found her there, as had become the pattern of the project's hardest evenings, carrying two cups of the same functional not-quite-coffee from six months earlier.

"I keep thinking about run four hundred and eleven," Ilse said, without turning around. "Four hundred and eleven times the same result, and I almost didn't run four hundred and twelve. I keep wondering what the equivalent of run four hundred and twelve looks like for this problem. Whether there's some question we haven't asked yet, some baseline we're still measuring against wrong, the same way I was measuring simulation cost against the wrong baseline for three years before I found the right one."

"Maybe," Corin said. "Or maybe this one doesn't have a four hundred and twelfth run waiting in it. Maybe some walls are just walls, and the honest answer is that we scoped something we can't build yet, and we have to say so, to the Council, to the workforce, to ourselves, and figure out what happens to twelve thousand people's lives when the thing they left everything to help build turns out to need an answer that doesn't exist."

"Do you believe that," Ilse asked. "Or are you saying it because someone in this project needs to hold the possibility of failure honestly, the way you held every objection in the mess hall, so I don't get to pretend it isn't there."

Corin was quiet for a long moment, looking out over four thousand lights scattered across ground that had never held a light before this year.

"I don't know," Corin said finally, and the admission cost something visible in the saying of it. "I know we haven't found it yet. I know every approach on record has hit the same ceiling humanity's hit for as long as anyone's been trying to solve this category of problem. I know that doesn't mean it isn't there. It just means it isn't anywhere we've thought to look. I've spent six months believing the theory was the hard part, the way you spent three years believing the cost was the hard part, before you found out the actual hard part was the baseline you were measuring against. I don't know what our baseline is wrong about yet. I just know we haven't found it, and four thousand people down there are building housing and infrastructure and a containment shell we don't yet know how to fill, on the strength of a promise we made in a Council chamber before either of us fully understood what keeping it would require."

Ilse looked out at the same lights, and thought, not for the first time since the vote, about Tavik's hundred and forty thousand, about the weight of a promise made to a room that couldn't yet know whether it would hold, and understood that the honest thing, the only thing the charter's own language actually permitted her to do, was to keep looking at the wall until it stopped being a wall, however long that took, rather than pretend, to the four thousand people below her or to the Council that had trusted her with their resources, that the door had already been found.

"We don't have it yet," she said. "We say that. Plainly. To the oversight committee, in the next report, not buried in a subclause, stated as the open question it actually is. We don't hide a wall we haven't gotten through. We say what we don't know, the same way I said it to Iyoro in the hearing, and we keep looking, and we let the record show exactly how honestly we looked, for exactly as long as it takes."

"And if it takes longer than the Council's patience," Corin said.

"Then it takes longer than the Council's patience," Ilse said, "and we find that out honestly too, instead of pretending, tonight, standing in front of four thousand lights we don't yet know how to justify, that we already know how this ends."

Perrin joined them on the platform before either had moved, carrying a tablet she did not immediately offer, which had become, over six months, its own kind of warning.

"The oversight committee's asked for its first formal briefing," Perrin said. "Not hostile yet. Curious, in the way that becomes hostile if it goes unanswered long enough. Four thousand workers, a construction footprint visible from orbit, and, as far as their briefing packet can tell, nothing built yet that resembles the machine the charter actually promised them. I can hold that off for another quarter with progress reports on infrastructure and workforce scaling. I don't know how many quarters after that before curious becomes something with teeth."

"Tell them the truth," Ilse said. "We don't have the power architecture yet. We're looking. We haven't found it."

"I will tell them exactly that," Perrin said, "in the next report, in plain language, the way you asked me to six months ago when you decided you'd rather stand in front of a wall honestly than pretend it was a door. I want you to understand what that honesty is going to cost, going forward, the same way I wanted you to understand what your name on a contract dispute would cost. Some of that committee will respect it, the way Iyoro respected the hearing. Some of it will hear an open question about the central mechanism and start asking whether the resource allocation was premature. I don't have a way to guarantee which reaction wins. I only have a way to make sure the honest version reaches them either way, because that was the promise you made, and I intend to keep making sure it gets kept, whatever it costs the project in the short run."

"Then that's the cost," Ilse said. "We agreed to it in the hearing before we agreed to anything else."

Neither of them moved from the platform for a long time after that, watching lights that represented decisions, some of them, made by people who had never met either of them, staked on the strength of a promise still waiting, six months into construction, for the older question underneath everything else to find its answer: not whether the theory held, not whether the workforce could scale, not whether the legal structure would survive contact with its first real dispute, but the older, plainer question sitting at the bottom of all of it, unglamorous and unsolved and growing larger every week the schedule advanced without it.

How do you power a machine nobody has ever needed to build before, at a scale nobody has ever needed to reach, using physics nobody has ever needed to push this far.

Nobody on the project had an answer yet. For the first time since the mess hall, eighteen months and a lifetime ago, Ilse allowed herself to sit with the unfamiliar sensation of not knowing whether one existed at all.

She thought, before she finally let Corin walk her back toward whatever passed for a bed that week, of her sister's transit route, flown safely a few hundred times before the hundred and first crossing found the one trajectory nobody had modeled precisely enough to catch. She had built this entire project on the conviction that precision was a thing you could eventually afford, if you were willing to pay for it before the cost came due instead of after. She had not yet considered, seriously, until this evening, what it would mean if precision itself turned out to be waiting on a piece of physics that did not yet exist, if the thing standing between four thousand people's trust and the promise she had made them was not a matter of resolve or resources but a fact about the universe nobody currently alive knew how to change. She did not know, watching the lights below her, whether that made the problem harder or simply older than she had understood it to be. She only knew that she was not going to stop looking, the same way she had not stopped at run four hundred and eleven, because stopping had never once, in her whole life, been the version of grief she knew how to carry.
