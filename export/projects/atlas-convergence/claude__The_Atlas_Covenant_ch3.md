THE SHAPE OF THE TRAP

I

The moon had no name of its own by the ninth month, only a survey designation three letters and a string of digits long that nobody used out loud, and somewhere in the process of pouring six thousand foundations into ground that had never held anything, the workforce had started calling it the Anvil instead, because that was what it felt like to build on: something meant to take a hammer blow and hold its shape anyway.

Ilse heard the name for the first time from a shift supervisor reporting a schedule delay, said it back to herself twice, and adopted it without asking anyone's permission, because it was the first piece of language this project had produced that nobody in a Council chamber had drafted, redlined, or ratified. It belonged to the people actually standing on the ground.

Corin's lab sat at the edge of the growing settlement, in a converted freight module that had been meant, on the original site plan, to hold three more months of prefab housing before Corin had quietly commandeered it for the power team instead, on the grounds that a research group chasing an unsolved physics problem needed walls it could write on more than four thousand new arrivals needed a faster move-in date. Ilse had approved the requisition without argument. She understood, by then, which of the two problems actually threatened to end the project first.

The two halves of the site had grown, over nine months, into something close to two different climates operating under the same sky that wasn't a sky at all, only vacuum and the gas giant's pale enormous curve hanging fixed in one quadrant of every window. On the construction side, progress had a shape a person could point to: a habitat ring finished, a water reclamation plant brought online, a school module opened for the growing number of workers who had started, against every planning assumption Sefu's original projections had made, bringing families rather than coming alone. On the power side, progress for nine months had meant something closer to subtraction, one more approach crossed off a whiteboard, one more archive read to exhaustion and found wanting, the grinding texture of research that hadn't yet found what it was looking for and could not yet prove it never would.

Ilse split her weeks between the two climates without ever fully belonging to either. She had built her career on mathematics precise enough to trust, and found herself, nine months into running an actual settlement, spending most of her days on a different kind of precision entirely: staffing ratios, housing allocations, the unglamorous arithmetic of keeping six thousand people fed, housed, and paid on a schedule that could not simply wait for the physics to finish being difficult before continuing to grow around it.

II

The zero-point line died the way the Casimir line had died three weeks earlier, honestly and on the record, though it took longer to kill because it had more places to hide.

Kwame Osei ran the final series of tests himself, a physicist Corin had recruited out of a settlement research post specifically for his stubbornness about not calling a result promising until he had tried, personally, to make it fail. He spent eleven days trying to make the zero-point extraction rig fail in a way that would explain why its yield refused to scale, and on the twelfth day he came to Corin's office with the answer nobody in the room wanted, delivered the way a person delivers a diagnosis they have already grieved on their own time so they can say it plainly to someone else.

"The vacuum fluctuation is real," Kwame said. "I want that on the record before I say anything else, because I don't want this filed the way the last team filed it a generation ago, as though the whole premise turned out to be nonsense. It isn't nonsense. It's real, it's measurable, and it will never be enough. The energy density available from vacuum fluctuation at any geometry we can actually build doesn't climb with scale the way we need it to. It climbs with surface area, and surface area is the one thing this project can't spend its way out of, because the geometry that would give us enough surface area to matter is larger than the moon we're standing on. This isn't an engineering problem we haven't solved yet. It's a mathematical ceiling built into the mechanism itself."

"How confident," Corin asked.

"As confident as I've ever been about anything I've spent eleven days trying to disprove and failed to disprove," Kwame said. "I'd stake the research post I gave up for this job on it."

Ilse had watched an earlier stage of the same test, weeks before Kwame's final verdict, standing behind the same containment window she'd once watched the Casimir rig run its own final measurement. The zero-point extraction apparatus was larger than the Casimir plates had been, a lattice of collector surfaces arranged in a geometry Kwame had spent months refining, and when he ran it at what he privately called, without much hope, its most optimistic configuration, the readout climbed for eleven seconds to a number that made Corin actually lean forward before it settled, inevitably, back to the same fraction of what the project needed that every earlier configuration had produced.

"There," Kwame said quietly, watching the number fall back to where the mathematics had always said it would. "That's the best this geometry does. That's the best any geometry like this can do, as far as I can prove. It isn't nothing. It would run a research station indefinitely, if that's ever what anyone needed it for. It will never run what we're actually trying to build, and I don't think another year of refinement changes that number by more than a rounding error."

Corin closed the file that afternoon, the third dead-end line in as many months, and did not pretend, in the status meeting that followed, that the closure was anything other than what it was. "We are now formally out of approaches with existing research behind them," Corin told the assembled team, standing in front of a whiteboard that had accumulated, over three months, an increasingly short list of things that hadn't worked and an empty column, still unfilled, for the thing that would. "Every avenue humanity has actually tried, at any point in its recorded history, for generating power at anything approaching the density this project requires, has now been tried again by this team, honestly, and has failed for the same structural reasons it failed the first time. I want to be plain about what that means. It does not mean the problem is unsolvable. It means the solution, if one exists, is not sitting in an archive somewhere waiting to be rediscovered. It has to be something nobody has thought to try yet, and I don't currently know what that is, and I don't think pretending otherwise in this meeting helps anyone in this room."

III

Ilse did not attend that meeting. She was, that same week, standing in a different kind of meeting entirely, in a prefab administrative module three kilometers from Corin's lab, watching Sefu walk a room of newly promoted shift leads through a staffing model that had been rewritten for the fourth time in as many months, because the workforce had crossed six thousand and showed no sign of leveling off before it reached the ten thousand the year-two projections called for.

She had stopped, by then, expecting the two halves of her week to feel like the same project. The construction side moved the way construction always moved, slowly, expensively, but along a path everyone in the room could see the shape of: more housing, more infrastructure, more trained hands, a settlement growing the way settlements had always grown, one module and one trained worker at a time. The power problem, when she thought about it at all during a staffing review, felt like a separate country she governed by report rather than by presence, a place whose weather she could not predict and whose maps nobody had drawn yet.

She found Corin on the observation platform that evening, the same platform that had become the place their hardest conversations happened by a kind of unspoken agreement neither of them had ever stated out loud.

"Kwame closed the zero-point line," Ilse said. It wasn't a question. Word traveled fast between the two halves of a project this size, however separate they felt from the inside.

"He did. Cleanly, honestly, the way I'd want any of my findings closed if I were the one delivering bad news. We are, as of this afternoon, formally out of ideas that already have research behind them." Corin looked out over the Anvil's spreading lights, six thousand of them now, a number that would have seemed impossible from this same platform nine months earlier. "I keep thinking about something you said, the night of the vote. That the mathematics was never actually the hardest part. That the hardest part was the baseline you were measuring against, and you didn't find the real one until you stopped asking the question you'd been asking for three years and asked a different one instead."

"You think we're asking the wrong question."

"I think every dead-end line in that lab shares one assumption we've never actually examined," Corin said. "We keep asking how to generate more energy. Extraction. Fluctuation. Every failed approach on that whiteboard is fundamentally a harvesting problem, how to pull power out of something that doesn't want to give it up easily. What if the actual constraint isn't extraction efficiency at all? What if we've been trying to squeeze water from a stone this whole time, when what we actually need is something that was never a stone in the first place. Something that doesn't have to be extracted from, because extraction was never its nature to begin with."

Ilse turned to look at him fully, recognizing, in the stillness of his posture, the same shape of thought she had watched him hold once before, in a mess hall eighteen months and a lifetime ago, right before he found the third hole in her original proposal.

"Say the rest of it," she said.

"I don't have the rest of it yet," Corin said. "I have the shape of a question I haven't finished asking. Give me a week."

IV

He did not need a week. He needed four days, and he spent all four of them alone in the converted freight module, running searches through physics archives with a rigor that made Kwame, checking in on him twice, quietly decide not to interrupt further.

The idea, when Corin finally brought it to the full team on the fifth morning, arrived the way Ilse's own idea had arrived at run four hundred and twelve: not as a triumphant breakthrough but as a plain, almost embarrassed correction of an assumption nobody had thought to question.

"Every approach we've tried treats energy as something you take from a system that would rather keep it," Corin said, standing in front of the same whiteboard, the accumulated dead ends erased for the first time in three months to make room for something new. "Vacuum fluctuation, Casimir geometry, every real research program humanity has ever run at this problem, all of them fight the same fight: pulling power out of a structure that resists giving it up, at a rate that never scales past a trickle. I want to ask a different question. What if there's a structure that doesn't resist at all? Not something we extract from. Something that only, ever, does one thing, which is emit."

Noor Hadad, the team's second physicist, a specialist in high-energy collider theory who had joined the project in its fourth month specifically because the recursive-mind problem interested her less than the power problem did, was the first to say the word out loud. She had spent eleven years, before the project, working on the negative results of the very search Corin was about to describe, an experience she mentioned to almost no one, because negative results rarely made for a career people asked about at length, and she had come to this project half hoping someone would eventually need the eleven years of failure she'd already accumulated on exactly this physics.

"You're talking about a white hole."

"I'm talking about a white hole," Corin confirmed.

The room went quiet in a way Ilse recognized from the mess hall years earlier, the silence of an idea large enough that nobody in the room wanted to be the first to either endorse it or dismiss it before understanding its full shape.

It was Noor who finally broke it, not with a technical objection but with the plainer one underneath every technical objection this project had ever survived. "You're describing something that has never existed in nature," she said. "Not once, not anywhere we've ever looked, in the entire observable history of the universe. There might be a reason for that beyond simple rarity. I want us to at least consider the possibility that nature doesn't produce white holes because something about their existence is fundamentally unstable in a way our mathematics hasn't caught yet, and that instability is exactly the kind of thing that wouldn't show up on a whiteboard, only in the moment something this size actually tries to exist."

"That's a fair question," Corin said, "and I don't have a clean answer for it, the same way Ilse didn't have a clean answer for Iyoro in the hearing. I can tell you the mathematics permits it. I can't tell you nature's silence on the subject is meaningless. It might mean nobody's ever had the narrow conditions required. It might mean something tries to happen every time those conditions get close and it fails in a way we can't currently predict. I think the only way to actually answer that question is the same way this entire project has answered every unanswerable question so far: build the smallest, most contained version of the test we can survive being wrong about, and let the physics tell us instead of guessing from a whiteboard."

"That's not a small ask," Noor said. "You're asking to attempt, at however small a scale, the one structure general relativity allows that has never once been observed to exist. If it goes wrong, we won't know why until after it's already gone wrong."

"I know," Corin said. "I'm asking anyway, for the same reason Ilse asked the Council to fund a machine neither of us could fully explain the danger of. I don't think there's a version of this project that doesn't eventually require someone to attempt something no one has ever safely verified in advance. I think the only honest choice left is where we attempt it, how contained we make the attempt, and how honestly we tell everyone downstream of us what we don't yet know."

"Walk us through it," Ilse said, because someone needed to, and because she had learned, over three years of asking Corin exactly this question, that the walking-through was where the real idea either survived or fell apart.

"A black hole only accretes," Corin said. "That's its entire nature, mathematically. Everything that crosses its horizon goes in and nothing, in any classical sense, comes back out. A white hole is the same mathematics run in reverse, a structure defined in general relativity as pure outward flux. Nothing crosses its horizon inward. It can only emit. It has never been observed, because nothing in the natural universe, as far as we've ever detected, produces the conditions required to create one, and most physicists who've studied the concept treat it as a mathematical curiosity rather than something you could ever actually build. But the mathematics doesn't forbid it. It only says nature has never happened to make one on its own."

"And you think we can," Noor said.

"I think we might be able to make a microscopic one," Corin said. "There is a real, currently active, currently unsuccessful research program searching for microscopic black holes at particle colliders, motivated by extra-dimension theories that would lower the effective Planck scale far enough to bring quantum gravity into range of achievable collision energies. It hasn't worked yet. Every search so far has come back negative. But the physics behind the search is sound, and nobody has ever aimed that same collision architecture at the other structure the mathematics allows. If the extra-dimension theories are correct, and if a collision at sufficient energy really can pinch spacetime into a horizon at all, there's no obvious reason the resulting structure has to be the accreting kind. The white hole configuration is the same mathematics, the same energy requirement, aimed at the other solution to the same equation. Nobody has tried it because nobody has ever needed a power source. Everyone chasing this research wanted a black hole, because a black hole is the interesting half of the pair to a physicist studying gravity. Nobody wanted the other half, because the other half only does one thing, and physicists don't generally get funded to build things that only emit."

"But you want it for exactly that reason," Ilse said.

"I want it for exactly that reason," Corin said. "A structure that can only emit, never accrete, is a source, not a sink. It doesn't fight you for the energy. It doesn't resist extraction, because there's no extraction happening at all. It just gives, continuously, by its own basic nature, for as long as it exists. If we can produce one, even microscopic, even briefly, we don't have a harvesting problem anymore. We have a containment problem instead, which is a completely different category of engineering, and one I actually believe we know how to attempt."

V

The containment question consumed the next six weeks, and Ilse found herself pulled into it more directly than she had been pulled into any technical problem since the original theory itself, not because her presence was strictly required, but because she found she could not stay away from a room where the actual shape of the machine she had proposed eighteen months earlier was, for the first time, being drawn on a board instead of gestured at in the abstract.

"Two boundaries," Corin explained, in the first working session Ilse sat in on. "The inner boundary holds the horizon itself in place, keeps it from drifting, keeps the emission directional rather than radiating uselessly in every direction at once. For that we don't need anything exotic. Electromagnetic field containment, the same basic principle used in existing fusion research, scaled and shaped for a structure this small and this intensely energetic. It's difficult engineering. It isn't new physics."

"And the outer boundary," Ilse asked.

"The outer boundary is the actual invention," Corin said, "and it's the part I don't yet know how to build. I know its shape. I know how it has to behave. I don't yet know what it has to be made of, and until I do, everything else about it is a drawing rather than an engineering plan. We need a structure that does two things simultaneously: holds the geometry of the containment field in place under continuous, extreme output, and harvests that output into usable power rather than letting it simply radiate away as waste heat and light. A structure assembled from parts can't do that. Parts have seams, and seams are where a structure this stressed eventually fails. It has to be grown as one continuous piece, into the exact geometry the containment requires, and I don't currently have a material that can survive being grown into that geometry in the first place. That's the piece I still need."

Noor had spent the previous two weeks reading everything the archive held on directed acoustic and photonic emission, and brought the second half of the mechanism to the same working session, sketched on a tablet she passed around the room rather than trusting to memory.

"Seed the lattice with emitter crystals," Noor said. "That gives you a photon channel, tuned to the frequency the horizon's emission actually produces. Drive the same lattice with SASER arrays, phonon lasers, which is real, existing hardware. They were first demonstrated more than a century ago in relative terms, reached coherent output in the megahertz-to-terahertz range within a year of the first demonstration, and multiple research institutions have kept the work alive since, quietly, without ever finding a use case dramatic enough to justify major investment. That gives you a phonon channel, tightly directional, driven by real, already-proven hardware rather than anything theoretical. Run both channels into the same lattice simultaneously, tuned correctly, and you don't just get two separate forms of harvesting running in parallel. You get something stranger. The photons and the lattice vibrations stop behaving as separate things and start behaving as one macroscopic quantum object, light and sound merged into a single collective state. There's a name for the theoretical version of this in the recent literature: a phonon-polariton condensate. It's never been built. It's been proposed, seriously, only very recently, as a real physical possibility rather than a mathematical curiosity. Nobody has had a reason to try building one at any meaningful scale, because nobody has ever needed to harvest an emission this intense, this continuously, off a source this small."

"You're telling me the harvesting mechanism and the containment structure are the same object," Ilse said slowly.

"I'm telling you they have to be," Corin said. "That's the part of this I find both elegant and, if I'm honest with this room the way I've tried to be honest with every room in this project, genuinely frightening. The lattice isn't a container with a separate power-collection system attached to it. It's a single continuous structure doing both jobs at once, because it has to be, because any seam between a containment function and a harvesting function is a seam under this much stress, and a seam under this much stress is where a structure like this eventually comes apart."

"What happens to one emitter crystal if it fails independently," Ilse asked. "Before we get to the whole structure. A single point, seeded into the lattice among thousands of others. Does the rest of the condensate simply route around it, the way a fault in a distributed system usually gets absorbed by everything healthy around it?"

Noor considered the question longer than Ilse expected before answering. "In an ordinary distributed system, yes, that's exactly what happens, and it's the entire reason distributed systems get built that way in the first place. A condensate doesn't behave like an ordinary distributed system, because a condensate is, by definition, one coherent quantum state rather than a collection of independent parts cooperating toward the same goal. If enough of the state is coherent, a local failure doesn't stay local. It can propagate through the whole condensate almost instantly, because the whole condensate is, in the relevant physical sense, one object rather than many. I want to answer your question honestly rather than reassuringly: I don't currently know how many emitter crystals can fail independently before the condensate state itself collapses, and I don't think we'll know until we've built something close enough to the real geometry to actually test it."

"So the redundancy everyone in this room has spent a career assuming a large structure should have," Ilse said, "doesn't apply here at all."

"Not in the way any of us were trained to expect," Corin said. "That's the honest cost of choosing a mechanism this elegant. Elegance, here, means the structure doesn't have separate failure domains to isolate a problem inside of. It has one domain, the whole thing, all the time. I don't say that to talk anyone in this room out of building it. I say it because I think every person who eventually works on the physical structure deserves to understand, from the very first day of construction, exactly what kind of object they're actually building, rather than discovering it only once something's already gone wrong."

VI

Nobody in the room needed to be told what that meant, but Corin said it plainly anyway, in the same working session, because the charter's language about honesty had never been written for the Council alone.

"If this fails," Corin said, "it doesn't fail in one subsystem while the others hold. There is no subsystem, in the way an engineer normally means the word. There's one structure, doing everything, all the time, under continuous load from something with no natural analog anywhere we've ever observed. When it fails, if it fails, it fails everywhere in the structure at once, because everywhere in the structure is the same object under the same stress. I don't currently know how you shut something like that down safely if it ever needs to be shut down. I suspect the answer isn't a switch. I suspect it's something closer to a sequence, done in a fixed order, over some span of time long enough to matter, and I don't have that sequence worked out, because we haven't built the thing the sequence would apply to yet. I want that uncertainty on the record now, in this room, before any of us gets far enough into the engineering to forget how early we still are."

Ilse thought, hearing it, of the Anvil, of the unspoken arithmetic that had chosen this dead moon over two living candidates months earlier, and understood, more fully than she had allowed herself to understand it at the time, exactly what that choice had actually been protecting against. She had picked a place to fail safely before anyone in this room had finished describing what the failure would even look like. She did not feel, hearing Corin's account of it now, that the earlier caution had been excessive. She felt, if anything, that it had not gone far enough, and resolved, quietly, to say so to the full engineering team before construction moved another meter closer to anything resembling ignition.

"We build the containment shell somewhere the eventual shutdown sequence gets a real budget of time and isolation," Ilse said. "Not an afterthought bolted on once the physics is finished. Part of the design, from the geometry up, on the assumption that someday, possibly decades from now, possibly sooner, someone is going to need to walk this back safely, and I want that person to have been thought about by us, now, before we've built anything they'll actually have to walk back."

Perrin found her an hour later, on the walk back from the lab to the administrative module, carrying the tablet that had by now become, over nine months, its own reliable warning.

"The oversight committee wants a substantive briefing," Perrin said. "Not the workforce and infrastructure updates I've been sending them. They want to know what the power architecture actually is, in terms specific enough to evaluate, and I no longer have a polite way to keep telling them we're still looking. Nine months of curious has started turning into nine months of unanswered, and those aren't the same thing to a committee that approved a resource figure this size."

"Tell them what we told the engineering team an hour ago," Ilse said. "We have a candidate mechanism. It's theoretical, unbuilt, and the person who designed it just told a room full of physicists, honestly, that he doesn't yet know how to shut it down safely if it ever needs shutting down. I'm not going to soften that for a committee any more than I'd soften it for myself."

"They're going to hear a project that just discovered its own machine might not have a safe off switch," Perrin said, "delivered by the person legally accountable for everything that happens on this moon. I want you to walk into that with your eyes open, the way you walked into the hearing with your eyes open. Some of them are going to hear honesty. Some of them are going to hear confirmation of exactly what Marrow warned this chamber about eighteen months ago."

"Then that's what they hear," Ilse said. "We agreed to this cost before we agreed to anything else. I'm not going to start negotiating with it now, nine months in, because the truth got harder to say than it used to be."

The briefing's contents did not stay contained to the oversight committee for long. Ilse had expected that, in the abstract, the way she had expected every prior piece of hard news to eventually travel from a closed meeting into the general hum of a settlement six thousand people strong, and she still found herself unprepared for how quickly it moved once it did. Within a week, the phrase without a safe off switch had turned into settlement-floor shorthand, repeated in the mess halls and the training bays with varying degrees of accuracy, and Sefu brought it to Ilse directly rather than let it keep spreading uncorrected.

"People are asking whether they're building their own coffin," Sefu said, flatly, the way she delivered every piece of news she judged too important to soften. "Not all of them. Most people down there still believe in what this project's trying to do, or they wouldn't have left whole lives behind to come work on it. But belief and fear aren't opposites, and I've watched both live in the same person at once for fifteen years of running sites like this. You built your name into every legal document this project has, which was the right call for accountability. It also means when something frightening leaks out of a closed meeting, your name is the one people attach the fear to."

"What do you want me to do," Ilse asked. "Tell them it isn't dangerous? It is dangerous. I'm not going to stand in front of six thousand people and tell them something false because the truth is inconvenient this month, the same way I wouldn't tell it to a Council chamber."

"I'm not asking you to lie," Sefu said. "I'm asking you to say the true thing to their faces instead of letting them hear a fragment of it secondhand from someone three shifts removed from the actual meeting. There's a difference between honesty and silence that gets filled in by rumor. You've been careful about the first. You've been careless, I think without meaning to, about the second."

Ilse held a town session in the largest habitat module three days later, unscripted, and answered every question the way she had answered Marrow in the hearing years earlier, without softening what she didn't know into something more comfortable than it was. She did not know, that evening, whether it changed anything in the settlement's underlying mood. She knew, walking back afterward, that she had rather be distrusted for the truth than trusted for a comfortable version of it, the same choice she had made once already, in a chamber far from here, before any of these six thousand people had ever heard her name.

Ingrid Bakke asked the last question of the evening, standing near the back of a room that had filled well past its intended capacity, and Ilse recognized her from the training bay months earlier, the same fitter who had once asked how anyone could learn to grow a material toward a shape that didn't exist yet.

"You told us, back when we started, that we'd know the material well enough to trust our hands to it once the geometry was finalized," Ingrid said. "Is it finalized now? Is what you just told this room the actual shape we're building toward, or is there still more of it you don't have yet?"

"There's more of it I don't have yet," Ilse said, because it was true, and because she had promised this room, and every room like it since the hearing, that she would not spend the truth carefully when spending it plainly was still an option. "The mechanism is real, on paper, in a way it wasn't a month ago. The engineering underneath it, the part your hands are actually going to need to trust, isn't finished. I don't know exactly when it will be. I know I'll tell you as soon as I know, the same way I'm telling you tonight what I don't yet know instead of pretending the gap isn't there."

Ingrid nodded once, the same settling nod Ilse remembered from the training bay, and did not ask a follow-up question, and Ilse understood, watching her sit back down among neighbors she had not known a year earlier, that the nod was not agreement so much as recognition, the hard-won respect of someone who had decided, some months earlier, to trust an uncomfortable answer over a comfortable one, and had not yet found a real reason to regret that choice.

VII

Corin's open question, the material the lattice actually had to be grown from, took two more weeks to answer, and the answer surfaced not from Corin's own search but from Kwame, tasked with drafting the first formal engineering specification for the same lattice, who hit a wall the physics alone had never required anyone to examine.

"We can describe the geometry," Kwame told the team, in the session where he brought the problem forward. "We can describe the growth process in principle. What we can't do, with anything currently available to this project, is actually grow it, because the material the geometry requires doesn't exist at the scale we need, and as far as I can determine, has never existed at that scale anywhere humanity has looked."

"What material," Ilse asked.

"Lonsdaleite," Kwame said. "Hexagonal diamond. Carbon, the same element as ordinary diamond, arranged in a different crystal structure, one that in principle should be stronger and more thermally resilient than standard cubic diamond, which is exactly the property profile this lattice needs to survive the load Corin's mechanism is going to put on it. The problem is that lonsdaleite, as a genuine, discrete bulk material, has been argued about for decades. It was first identified in fragments from a meteorite impact, a graphite-bearing body striking something hard enough to lock the graphite's atoms into diamond's arrangement while it kept graphite's original hexagonal geometry instead of cubic diamond's usual one. That's not a process anyone can run on demand. It's a single violent instant, one impact event, and every confirmed sample humanity has ever recovered came from the aftermath of an impact like that, not from a lab. There have been a handful of very recent attempts to synthesize it deliberately, and one of them, within roughly the last year, succeeded in producing a genuinely pure, well-oriented block of the material for the first time in history. It tops out at millimeter scale. The containment lattice this project needs is not millimeter scale. It needs a structure large enough to hold a horizon and channel its emission, grown from a material nobody has ever produced in a quantity larger than a fingernail."

"So we're stopped again," Noor said, not as an accusation, simply as a fact laid on the table.

"Not entirely," Kwame said, and something in his voice made Ilse look up from the specification sheet she'd been reading. "This project has one confirmed sample of lonsdaleite already in its possession. A trace fragment, recovered years ago from a comet that passed through this system during a routine long-range survey, catalogued at the time as an anomaly nobody followed up on, because nobody, at the time, had any reason to care what a comet happened to be carrying. It's not enough material to build anything. It's barely enough to run a confirming analysis. But it is enough to know, with certainty, exactly what we're looking for, down to the crystal structure, and it's enough, if the survey records are precise enough, to run that comet's trajectory backward and find out where it actually came from."

The room was quiet again, a different quiet than the one that had followed Corin's white hole proposal, closer to the stillness Ilse remembered from the hearing chamber, the moment before a chain of consequence made itself visible to everyone in the room at once.

"You're saying there might be more of it somewhere out past this system," Ilse said.

"I'm saying there's exactly one lead, and it's a cold one, years old, pointing at a trajectory nobody's ever had a reason to chase before now," Kwame said. "I'm saying if this project wants to build the thing Corin just described, someone is eventually going to have to go find where that comet came from, and I don't know what's waiting at the other end of that trajectory, because nobody has ever had a reason to look."

He brought the fragment itself to the next working session, sealed in an analysis case small enough to fit in one hand, a chip of material barely larger than a grain of sand that did not, to Ilse's eye, look like anything worth an expedition. Kwame ran the case's readout for the room anyway, the crystal lattice pattern displayed on a screen large enough for everyone to see the hexagonal symmetry the fragment carried instead of cubic diamond's usual arrangement, proof, in a form small enough to lose in a pocket, that the thing they needed actually existed somewhere and was not merely a shape on a whiteboard.

"I've run this confirmation four times since the survey team first catalogued it," Kwame said. "Not because I doubted the original result. Because I kept expecting to find a reason it couldn't be real, the way every other approach in this project has turned out to have a reason it couldn't be real, and I never found one. It's genuine hexagonal diamond. It came from somewhere that isn't here. That's the entire extent of what I can currently tell you about it, and it's more than this project had a week ago."

Ilse held the case for a long moment before passing it on, turning it slightly to catch the room's light against a fragment too small to see clearly without the display's magnification, and thought, absurdly, of the resource figure she had watched settle onto her name the night of the vote, a number too large to hold in her head as anything but an abstraction. This was the opposite kind of impossible: a fragment small enough to hold in a closed fist, on which the entire physical mechanism Corin had spent five days discovering now depended completely.

VIII

Ilse found Tavik that same evening, on the observation platform, where he had taken, over the preceding months, to spending his evenings alone in a way she had noticed without commenting on until now.

"I have something for you," she said. "Not an assignment yet. A trajectory, and a reason someone is eventually going to need to chase it."

Tavik turned from the rail, and something in his posture changed, the alertness of a man who had spent a career waiting for exactly this kind of sentence and had, for months, been waiting for it here instead, on a dead moon, watching other people build things he didn't yet have a role in building.

"Tell me," he said.

She told him what Kwame had found, the comet, the trace fragment, the cold trajectory nobody had ever had reason to follow, and watched him listen the way he had listened to her original proposal years earlier, in full, without interrupting, until she had finished.

"You said this wasn't an assignment yet," Tavik said, when she'd finished.

"It isn't. We don't have a ship suited for it. We don't have a survey command assembled. We don't even know yet whether what's out there is findable at all, or whether the trajectory decays into noise a few systems out. I'm telling you because you asked me to remember, months ago, and because I think, of everyone on this project, you're the only person who's spent a career doing exactly what this is going to require: going somewhere uncharted, on the strength of an old, cold lead, because the alternative is not going at all and hoping the answer finds you instead."

Tavik looked out over the Anvil's spreading lights, thousands of them now, arranged across ground that had held nothing at all a year earlier, and Ilse thought, watching him, of the way he had once described sitting with the dead of Verdent, alone, on the night of the ratification vote, telling them something was finally being built.

"I've spent my whole life arriving after the thing already happened," Tavik said finally. "Verdent, the evacuation, forty years of grief I never got to spend usefully on anything except remembering. I would like, before I'm done, to be the one who goes and finds something, instead of the one who cleans up after it's already been lost." He looked at her directly. "Tell me when there's a ship. I'll be ready before it is."

"It won't be safe," Ilse said. "Whatever's out past that trajectory, we don't know it, we can't chart it, and I can't promise you a survey command comes home from uncharted territory the way it left. I've spent this entire year learning how much I don't know how to promise people, and I'd rather say that to you plainly now than let you volunteer for something you think I've already made safe."

"I buried a hundred and forty thousand people who trusted a model that told them the ground under their feet was safer than it was," Tavik said. "I am not asking you to promise me safety. I've never once believed in it, not since Verdent, not in any form that held up under real pressure. I'm asking you to let me go do something that might actually matter, on the far side of a risk I've chosen with my eyes open, instead of spending whatever years I have left standing on a platform, watching other people build the thing I only ever got to argue for in a chamber. That's not a small difference to me, Ilse. It might be the only difference that's mattered to me since I was young enough not to have buried anyone yet."

IX

Ilse did not sleep well that night, though the reason had shifted, over the preceding weeks, from the shape it had taken on the night of the vote. She was no longer lying awake wondering whether the theory would hold. Corin's mechanism, on paper, held together in a way none of the earlier dead ends ever had, elegant in exactly the way true things were usually elegant, once you finally stopped forcing the wrong question onto them.

What kept her awake instead was smaller and, in its own way, harder to hold: the understanding that every piece of the mechanism they had just assembled on a whiteboard, the horizon that could only give and never take, the single continuous structure with no seam to fail safely along, the material that existed, as far as anyone could currently prove, in one confirmed fragment recovered from a comet nobody had thought to chase until tonight, all of it converged on the same plain fact underneath the physics. They had solved the shape of the machine. They had not solved how to build it, and the gap between those two things was not a matter of more research time or a larger budget. It was a gap shaped like an expedition into territory nobody on this project had ever charted, chasing a trail years cold, toward an answer that might not exist at the end of it at all.

She thought of Corin's question from months earlier, standing on this same platform: do you understand yet what we actually just did. She had answered it then in terms of the promise, the shape of a place to be wrong safely instead of expensively. She understood now that the promise had a second half she hadn't fully reckoned with that night, the half that required someone to go looking, in person, for the raw material the promise itself depended on, and that the someone had a name now, and had asked for the job himself, on a platform that had become, over the better part of a year, the place this project kept discovering exactly how much it was actually going to cost.

She did not have an answer for that cost yet, not the specific one, not the trajectory's true end or what waited along it. She had, for the first time since the vote, something more concrete than the mathematics alone: a mechanism that might actually work, a wall it would take to build it, and a name for the person willing to walk out past the edge of everything charted to find out whether the wall could be climbed at all.

Corin found her still awake near the hour the moon's artificial cycle called morning, the way Corin had found her awake on the night of the ratification vote, and sat down across from her without needing to ask what kept her from sleeping.

"Noor asked me something after the session broke up," Corin said. "She asked whether I'd thought about the fact that we're proposing to build the one object in this entire mechanism that has never once occurred naturally, anywhere, in the whole recorded history of observation. I told her I had. I don't think I told her the rest of what I actually think, which is that I find that fact almost comforting rather than frightening, and I don't know if that says something good about me or something I should be worried about."

"Say the rest of it," Ilse said, the way she always asked him to, the way she'd asked him in the mess hall three years earlier when he'd first found the hole in her original proposal.

"Nature never needed a white hole," Corin said. "Nature already had everything it needed to run for however many billion years it's been running: stars, gravity, the ordinary machinery of the universe doing what it does without anyone asking it to give anything back on purpose. We need one because we're asking for something nature never had to solve, a source dense enough and stable enough to run a simulation of an entire reality. I don't think the absence of a natural white hole tells us the mathematics is wrong. I think it tells us we're the first thing in this universe, as far as anyone has ever observed, that actually needed one to exist. That's either the most frightening sentence I've said in three years of working on this project, or it's the plainest possible description of what building something unprecedented has always actually meant, since long before either of us was born."

"Which do you think it is," Ilse asked.

"I think it's both," Corin said, "the same way the lever in the mess hall was both the most elegant idea I'd ever heard and the most expensive way anyone had ever found to be wrong about something. I don't think those two readings ever stopped being true at the same time, for anything this project has done since. I've stopped expecting one of them to eventually cancel the other out. I think we're just going to keep carrying both, the whole way through, however far through turns out to be."

They sat together a while longer, neither of them speaking, watching the same pale unmoving curve of the gas giant that had watched over every hard conversation this project had ever had, on a platform, on a moon, in a system that had never once, across its whole long silent history, needed to produce the thing two exhausted engineers were now quietly asking it, without permission, to finally hold.
