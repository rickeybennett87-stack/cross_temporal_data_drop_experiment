NOTHING SPENT YET

I

The Constantine had spent eleven years in a reserve fleet nobody outside the Compact's own logistics office remembered existed, mothballed after a border dispute that had ended, as most border disputes eventually ended, in a treaty nobody celebrated and a warship nobody needed anymore. Perrin found it the way she found most things this project needed, by reading procurement registries other people had stopped reading years earlier, and brought the file to Ilse on an otherwise ordinary morning with the satisfaction of someone who had been searching for a month and had not expected to actually succeed.

A few months had passed since the night on the observation platform when Ilse had first told Tavik about the comet's trajectory, months spent almost entirely on the unglamorous work of turning a promise into an actual asset, the same slow translation from intention to instrument that had defined every stage of this project since its very first vote. Ilse had stopped expecting that translation to ever feel fast. She had only, by now, learned to recognize its shape well enough to stop being surprised by how long it always seemed to take.

"A Lucifer-class heavy gunship," Perrin said, sliding the tablet across the administrative module's table. "Reserve fleet, technically still Compact property, technically still combat-rated, and technically available for reassignment to any project holding a Council-recognized charter, which as of eighteen months ago happens to include us. Nobody's flown it in over a decade. It still has a working chromatic shift drive, which is the only part of this ship I actually understand well enough to know we need it, and a hull rated for punishment neither of us has any intention of testing on purpose."

"Why a gunship," Ilse asked. "We're sending a survey command, not a war party."

"Because nothing else in any registry I can access has both the range and the drive to chase a comet's trajectory back further than any charted survey has ever gone, and because a hull built to take a hit is not a bad thing to hand someone flying into territory nobody's mapped," Perrin said. "I'm not going to pretend I love the optics of repurposing a warship for this. I checked. There isn't a better option, and I've spent three weeks looking."

The requisition took another six weeks to clear, not because anyone objected in principle, but because a Council resolution built for construction contracts and workforce allocation had never anticipated a research project needing to reassign a mothballed warship, and Perrin spent most of those six weeks translating the request into language a procedural board could actually approve without inventing an entirely new category, the way she'd once had to invent one for the incorporation itself.

"I keep thinking this project should stop needing new legal categories eventually," Perrin said, the day the reassignment finally cleared, handing Ilse a signature page that would put the Constantine's custody, on paper, under the same trust that already carried every other liability this project had accumulated.

"I don't think it's going to stop," Ilse said, signing it without hesitation, the way signing things like this had stopped costing her the sleepless nights it once had. "I think we're just going to keep needing new categories until the project's finished needing anything at all, and I don't know when that is."

Anke Solberg met the Constantine for the first time three days after the reassignment cleared, walking its dormant corridors with a maintenance tablet and a flashlight, cataloguing a decade of quiet neglect the reserve fleet's own paperwork had never fully admitted to. She found Ilse there on the second day of the survey, standing in a cargo bay that had once carried munitions and would now carry, if the mission succeeded, nothing heavier than a sealed sample case.

"It's a better hull than the paperwork made it sound," Anke said, by way of introduction, before Ilse had finished deciding how to announce herself. "Whoever mothballed this ship did it properly. Half the systems are dormant rather than degraded, which is a difference that matters more than most people realize. Dormant, I can wake up. Degraded, I'd have had to tell you to find a different ship entirely."

"How long before it's ready," Ilse asked.

"Six weeks, the way I told Tavik," Anke said. "I want to be honest with you about what those six weeks actually buy. I can make this ship safe to fly into known space. I cannot promise you what happens to a decade-dormant chromatic core the first time it's asked to sustain output across territory nobody's ever charted. Nothing in this ship's maintenance history anticipated that kind of use. I'll tell you the truth about every system I can test. I won't pretend I can test the one thing this mission actually depends on until it's already underway."

"That's the same shape of honesty this whole project runs on," Ilse said. "I'd rather have it from you than a comfortable estimate I couldn't verify."

"Then we'll get along fine," Anke said, already turning back to her tablet, the conversation, for her, already finished the way it usually was for people who measured their own worth by what they could actually fix rather than by what they could say about fixing it.

II

Tavik chose his crew the way Ilse remembered him listening in the mess hall years earlier, without hurry, without visible preference, watching each candidate longer than the interview itself seemed to require. He conducted every interview himself, in the same small module Anke had commandeered for the refit, refusing Sefu's offer to screen the applicant pool down to a shortlist first.

"I'd rather see all of them," Tavik told her, when she raised it. "I've made the mistake before of trusting someone else's shortlist and finding out afterward the person I actually needed had been filtered out before I ever got the chance to meet them. I'm not making that mistake again on a crew this small. Four people is not a number where any of them can be the wrong choice and still have three others quietly compensate for it."

Mirek Okonkwo came first, a pilot who had spent a decade flying supply routes through the same kind of debris-strewn transit corridors that had killed Ilse's sister, and who told Tavik plainly, in the interview, that he'd taken every precision-navigation course available to him afterward specifically because he'd lost a partner to exactly that kind of corridor and had decided, privately, years before this project ever existed, that he was going to be the reason it didn't happen to someone else on his watch.

"I don't need you to be fearless," Tavik told him. "I need you to fly like the debris field already killed someone you loved, because it has, and I'd rather have that in the cockpit than confidence that hasn't been tested yet."

"That's the only kind of pilot I know how to be anymore," Mirek said. "I stopped being the other kind a long time ago."

Tomasz Berhane came next, a survey specialist whose entire professional life had been spent reading trajectory data for exactly the kind of long-range backward reconstruction this expedition actually required, recruited out of an observatory post he'd held for eleven years without once, he admitted frankly, working on anything a Council chamber would have called urgent.

"I've spent a career proving comets came from where the mathematics said they came from," Tomasz said. "Nobody's ever needed me to prove it in order to go there afterward. I'd like to know what that feels like, at least once, before I'm too old to be useful on a ship like this one."

Anke Solberg rounded out the crew, an engineer whose specialty was keeping older hardware alive well past the point most technicians declared it unsalvageable, which made her, in Tavik's judgment, the single most important person he could bring onto a ship that had spent eleven years accumulating exactly the kind of quiet mechanical decay reserve fleets never fully disclosed in their maintenance logs.

"I've read the Constantine's full service record," Anke told him, in her interview, with a bluntness Tavik had learned, over four decades, to value above almost any other quality a colleague could offer him. "It's a good hull. It is not, currently, a good ship. I can make it a good ship again, given six weeks and full access to the yards, and I want it understood now, before we leave, that six weeks isn't optional. I'm not flying anyone into uncharted space on hardware I haven't personally verified down to the last coupling."

"Take your six weeks," Tavik said. "I'd rather leave late than not come back at all."

He interviewed eleven other candidates before settling on the three, and told Ilse afterward, in a debrief she had not strictly needed to attend but had come to anyway, that the rejections had cost him more than the choices had.

"Most of them were good," Tavik said. "Competent, willing, exactly the kind of person this project should be proud to have on its rolls. I turned them away for reasons that had nothing to do with competence. A woman who flew supply routes for a decade without ever once losing a ship, better instincts than Mirek's on paper, and I still chose him, because when I asked her what she'd do if we met something we couldn't outrun, she gave me a tactical answer. Mirek gave me a grief answer. I've buried enough people to know which one actually holds up when the moment arrives."

"That's not a comfortable way to build a crew," Ilse said.

"No," Tavik agreed. "It's the only way I know how to build one that comes home. Verdent taught me that competence isn't the thing that saves anyone when a plan meets reality it didn't anticipate. What saves people is someone in the room who's already lived through losing something, and decided, afterward, exactly what they were willing to do differently the next time. I went looking for people who'd already made that decision, before I ever needed them to prove it."

III

The Constantine departed from the Anvil's makeshift dock on a morning Ilse had cleared her entire schedule for, the first departure of its kind this project had ever staged, four people walking across a gantry built originally for construction freight rather than for anyone's goodbye.

She had not planned to say anything beyond the ordinary courtesies of a send-off, and found, standing at the base of the gantry with Corin beside her, that the ordinary courtesies would not come.

"You don't have to make this into another Verdent," Ilse told Tavik, the words arriving before she had fully decided to say them. "I need you to hear that plainly, the same way I've tried to say everything else to you plainly. I am not sending you out there to redeem forty years of grief. I'm sending you because you're the person best equipped to bring a crew home, and I would like all four of you to actually come home, not to have proven something along the way."

"I know the difference," Tavik said. "I've known it since the platform, months ago, when I asked you for this. I'm not chasing redemption, Ilse. Redemption implies there's a debt I think I can pay off, and I stopped believing in that arithmetic a long time before I ever met you. I'm chasing the plain satisfaction of being useful to something before I'm finished, in a way Verdent never let me be. That's a smaller thing than redemption. I've found, over four decades, that it's also the only version of that feeling that's ever actually held up."

Anke and Mirek had already boarded by then, running final systems checks Ilse could hear faintly through the open airlock, and Tomasz lingered a moment longer at the base of the gantry, looking back at the settlement's spreading lights as though committing the sight to memory in case it turned out to be the last time he saw it.

"Eleven days," Tomasz said, to no one in particular, and then, catching Ilse's eye, added, "Assuming my reconstruction holds. I'd like it noted, for the record, that I told you the confidence interval honestly before we ever left the dock."

"Noted," Ilse said. "I'd rather have your honest seventy percent than anyone else's confident hundred."

Corin said little through the entire farewell, standing slightly apart, and only spoke once the airlock had sealed and the Constantine's docking clamps had begun their release sequence.

"I keep thinking about the fact that we built an entire legal category just to let this ship exist under our charter," Corin said, "and none of that paperwork says anything about what happens to the four people inside it if the thing they're chasing turns out not to be there at all, or turns out to be something worse than absence. I don't like how much of this project's actual weight lives in places the charter never anticipated."

"Neither do I," Ilse said. "I don't think there was ever a version of this where every weight got anticipated in advance. I think that's just what it costs to be first at something. Somebody has to find out what the charter didn't think to say, and pay whatever that costs, so the next version of this project has language for it."

The Constantine cleared the dock in silence, running lights steady against the dark, and Ilse watched it shrink to a single point and then to nothing at all, standing on the gantry long after Corin had gone back inside, carrying the unfamiliar weight of having sent someone she had come to care for into territory she could not see and could not help him from, for the first time since this project had begun.

IV

The Constantine's first jump happened on a morning that felt, to Tavik, almost anticlimactic after months of acquisition and refit, a quiet countdown in a cockpit built for a crew twice the size actually occupying it, and then the disorienting wrench of chromatic shift engaging, spacetime folding through a drive lineage older than any of them, proven, reliable, indifferent to the fact that the four people aboard it were about to see more open, uncharted space in a single voyage than any of them had seen in their entire lives combined.

"First jump complete," Mirek reported, reading instruments that had settled, after the wrench passed, into a steadiness that surprised him more than he let show. "Drive's clean. No decay in the luminance core, nothing Anke's refit didn't already account for."

"Told you," Anke said, from the engineering console, without looking up. "Six weeks."

Tavik stood at the center of a bridge that had, until six months ago, existed only as a survey designation and an old comet's trajectory in an archive nobody had ever thought to check, and felt something he had not expected to feel again after Verdent, an unguarded quiet that was not peace exactly, but was close enough to it that he let himself sit inside it a moment longer than he strictly needed to.

"How far to the trajectory's first waypoint," he asked.

"Eleven days at this drive's sustained output," Tomasz said, reading his own instruments, the trajectory reconstruction he'd built from a single comet's decade-old survey record now scrolling in front of him as an actual navigational plot rather than an academic exercise. "Assuming the comet's original path held the shape my reconstruction says it held. I want to be honest about the margin of error before we're eleven days out and it matters. I'd put confidence at somewhere between seventy and eighty percent that we're chasing the right thread at all, not a hundred."

"Seventy to eighty percent is more confidence than anyone had about this project the night the Council voted on it," Tavik said. "I'll take those odds."

The days that followed settled into a rhythm none of the four had experienced before, the compressed intimacy of a small crew on a long transit, meals eaten in shifts around the same narrow table, watches traded in a cycle Mirek had drawn up before they'd even cleared the Anvil's home system. Tavik used the time to learn his crew the way he had once learned an evacuation zone, methodically, without hurry, cataloguing their skills and, more carefully still, the shape of what each of them was actually afraid of, information he suspected he would need long before any of them volunteered it outright.

Tomasz, it turned out, was afraid of being wrong in a way that cost someone else something, a fear he confessed on the fourth night, unprompted, watching his own trajectory plot the way a man watches a bridge he isn't yet certain will hold his weight. "I've spent a career being wrong safely," he told Tavik. "A miscalculated comet origin costs a paper a correction, maybe a colleague's afternoon reading it. Out here, if I'm wrong, it costs eleven days and however many after that finding our way back to a thread that might not even exist. I don't think I fully understood the difference until we were already underway."

"You'll be wrong eventually," Tavik told him, plainly, the same plainness he'd have wanted from anyone telling him something true. "Everyone chasing an uncertain thread is wrong sometimes. The measure of a survey specialist isn't whether the reconstruction holds. It's whether you tell the crew the moment you suspect it's stopped holding, instead of waiting until the silence costs more than the correction would have."

"I can do that," Tomasz said. "I don't know that I've ever had to, before this."

"Neither have I," Tavik said. "Most of what I'm asking of everyone on this ship, I'm asking for the first time myself."

Anke's fear, when it eventually surfaced, took a different shape entirely, and she offered it without being asked, the seventh night, over a dormant systems check that had turned up nothing wrong but had kept her at her console long past the end of her shift anyway. "I'm not afraid of the drive failing," she told Tavik, when he found her still there. "I've checked it myself, six weeks running, and I trust my own work more than I trust most things in this universe. I'm afraid of something failing that I never thought to check, because nobody's ever needed to check it before, on a mission shaped like this one. That's a different kind of fear than Tomasz's. His is about being wrong. Mine is about not knowing there was a question to be right or wrong about in the first place."

"That's the same fear this whole project has been carrying since the very first year of it," Tavik said. "Ilse's team back on the Anvil is living inside that exact question right now, about the machine itself. I don't think there's a version of doing something for the first time that doesn't carry it. I've made my peace with it as much as I'm able. I don't know that peace is the right word. Familiarity, maybe. I've been afraid of exactly this kind of unknown long enough now that it doesn't cost me sleep the way it used to."

"Does it get easier," Anke asked.

"No," Tavik said. "It gets more familiar. I've decided, somewhere along the way, that those are different things, and I'd rather be honest with you about which one I've actually got to offer."

V

Delegate Hassoun requested the meeting formally, through Perrin's office, rather than the informal channel most oversight committee members used for routine questions, and Ilse understood, reading the request, exactly what that formality was meant to signal before she ever sat down across from him.

"Eighteen months," Hassoun said, without preamble, in a conference module Perrin had booked for the occasion, spread out a set of figures Ilse recognized as her own resource reports, annotated in a hand that was not Perrin's. "Eighteen months, and the settlements I represent have watched their own supply allocations tighten twice in that span, both times attributed, in the Council's own budget language, to the resource commitment this project holds. I sat in that hearing three years ago and I asked you a resource question you answered honestly, and I want to be equally honest with you now. My constituents are not asking me whether your project is theoretically sound. They're asking me why their children's schools are running reduced hours while a moon nobody lives on grows a machine that hasn't produced anything they can see yet."

"I understand the question," Ilse said. "I don't have a comfortable answer to it, and I told this Council I wouldn't manufacture one."

"Then give me the uncomfortable one," Hassoun said. "Plainly. I came here for that, not for reassurance."

Ilse laid her own figures beside his, the two sets of numbers sitting on the same table for the first time in either of their offices, and let him see the gap between them before she said anything at all. "Your reports show forty-one settlements absorbing a combined resource tightening measured against this project's allocation," she said. "My reports show the same allocation, measured against every prototype and every catastrophic failure this species has ever paid for the expensive way, still coming in smaller than a single generation's worth of accidents like Verdent. Both sets of numbers are true. I'm not going to pretend your constituents should find that comforting when their own children's schools are the ones running short hours this month, because comfort was never the actual promise I made this Council. The promise was honesty, and honesty says the ledger balances in the long run and does not balance yet, today, for the people you represent."

"That's an argument for patience," Hassoun said. "Patience is not a resource my constituents have in unlimited supply."

"No," Ilse agreed. "It's the only resource I actually have to offer them right now, and I know that isn't enough. I don't have a faster version of true. I only have the true version, at the pace it's actually arriving."

"The mechanism is real," Ilse said. "We solved the power architecture five months ago, on paper, in a way we hadn't solved it before. We do not yet have the material to build it, and the only lead we have on that material is a survey command that left this system eleven days ago chasing a trajectory that might not pan out at all. I cannot tell you when this project starts producing something your constituents can see. I can tell you that every resource spent so far has been spent on real, documented, honestly reported progress, and that I would rather you distrust the pace of that progress than trust a timeline I invented to make this meeting easier."

Hassoun was quiet for a long moment, weighing an answer he had not expected against the one he had prepared to hear.

"You haven't changed since the hearing," he said finally. "I mean that as an observation, not entirely as a compliment. I voted against easing your resource allocation twice this year, and I'll tell you honestly, both times were close votes on my own committee, closer than I expected them to be given how little visible progress you have to show. I don't know how many more close votes this project survives before the settlements I represent stop accepting honest uncertainty as an answer and start demanding a result instead."

"I don't know either," Ilse said. "I'd rather know that uncertainty is real than pretend I've already solved it."

"That's the same answer you gave in the chamber three years ago," Hassoun said, standing to leave. "I voted for the resource allocation that day because Iyoro convinced me honesty was worth funding even when it wasn't comfortable. I want you to understand that conviction has a floor, Dr. Okwuosa. I don't know exactly where it is. I would very much like this project to find its result before either of us finds out."

Perrin, who had sat through the entire exchange without speaking, walked Ilse back to the administrative module afterward, and did not soften what she had just watched.

"He's not wrong to be worried," Perrin said. "I've been tracking the settlement resentment reports for four months, and they're not slowing down. Hassoun represents forty-one settlements. Most of them signed onto this project's resource allocation without ever fully understanding what an open-ended timeline actually meant for their own budgets downstream. I don't think that's a failure of anyone's honesty. I think it's a failure of imagination on their part, three years ago, about what patience this scale of project would actually require, and now the bill for that failure is arriving in the form of school hours and supply tightening, and it isn't landing on us. It's landing on people who never got a vote."

"What do you want me to do differently," Ilse asked.

"Nothing, today," Perrin said. "I want you to understand that the floor Hassoun mentioned isn't abstract. I've watched committees like his turn on projects less exposed than this one, and it doesn't happen gradually. It happens the way the ratification vote happened, a slow accumulation of individual delegates deciding, privately, that they've reached their own limit, and then all at once, in a single session, the majority isn't there anymore. I don't have a countdown for you. I have a pattern I've seen before, and I'd rather you carry it with you than be surprised by it the day it actually arrives."

VI

Eleven days out, on schedule, Tomasz's trajectory reconstruction resolved into a live sensor contact well before anyone aboard the Constantine expected one, a single ship, small, moving with the evasive irregularity of something that had already noticed them first.

The eleven days leading to it had passed the way Tomasz's confidence interval had promised they might, uneventfully, the crew settling deeper into the rhythm Tavik had spent those same days quietly studying, until the trajectory reconstruction began throwing off readings none of them had briefed for: gravitational perturbations too small to be natural debris, a faint and irregular electromagnetic signature that Tomasz flagged twice in his logs before he was willing to say the word out loud to anyone but himself. He said it to Tavik alone, first, the night before the contact actually resolved.

"I think there's something occupying this system," Tomasz told him, in the narrow galley that had become, by unspoken agreement, the place the crew's harder conversations happened when they happened at all. "Not just passing through. Something with enough mass and enough regularity in its movement that I'd call it inhabited if I were writing the survey report from a comfortable observatory instead of flying through it. I didn't want to say it to the whole crew until I was sure, and I'm still not entirely sure. I wanted you to know before the rest of them did, in case you wanted to change our approach before we're already inside whatever this is."

"We hold course," Tavik said, after a long moment. "We don't change our profile, we don't slow down in a way that reads as hesitation, and we don't speed up in a way that reads as aggression. Whatever's out here, I'd rather it read us as exactly what we are, moving exactly the way we'd move if we had nothing to hide, because we don't."

The bridge went quiet the way Ilse would later hear it described secondhand, four people who had spent eleven days building a rhythm together suddenly aware, all at once, that the rhythm had never actually been tested against anything real.

"Contact," Mirek said, instruments confirming what his own eyes had caught half a second earlier. "Unknown configuration, moving away from us at speed. Whatever it is, it's already running."

"Running from what," Anke asked, watching her own board rather than the tactical display, out of a habit built from years of assuming the engineering answer mattered as much as the sensor answer.

"From us," Tavik said, quietly, watching the contact's vector curve away from the Constantine's own heading with an urgency that read, even across the distance, as something closer to fear than caution. "Hold our course. Don't pursue. Whatever that ship is, it decided what we are before we ever got close enough to tell it otherwise, and I would rather it keep believing whatever kept it running than find out it was wrong."

"You think it's hostile," Tomasz said.

"I think it's scared," Tavik said. "I've commanded evacuation logistics long enough to recognize the shape of a ship that's already decided fighting isn't an option. That's not aggression. That's the flight pattern of something that's run from whatever we look like before, more than once, and lived through it by running fast enough. I don't know what we look like to it. I know I'd rather leave that question unanswered than force an answer out of a ship that's already decided the only safe move is distance."

The contact vanished from sensor range within the hour, not destroyed, not engaged, simply gone, faster than the Constantine's own sustained speed could have matched even if Tavik had ordered pursuit, which he had not.

Mirek held his own instruments a while longer after the contact dropped off the board entirely, running the encounter's recorded telemetry back at quarter speed, the way a pilot replays a near miss he can't yet let go of. "It changed heading twice in the last four seconds before it disappeared," he said. "Not evasive maneuvering against us specifically. That's a ship correcting for something in its own drive, mid-flight, under stress it wasn't built to sustain for long. Whatever we look like to it, it was willing to push its own hardware past a safe margin rather than stay in range of us a second longer than it had to."

"That's not a rational response to an unknown ship," Tomasz said. "That's a learned response to a known one."

"Write it down exactly like that," Tavik said. "Every detail, timestamped, before any of us starts smoothing it into a story that makes more sense than what actually happened. I want the report that reaches Ilse to carry what we actually saw, not what we've decided it meant by the time we're home to explain it."

VII

The workforce strain surfaced, back on the Anvil, in a form small enough that Sefu almost didn't bring it to Ilse at all, a scheduling dispute between two shift crews over overtime allocation that had, by the time it reached her desk, already produced a walkout threat from the second shift's informal representatives.

"It's not really about the overtime," Sefu told Ilse, laying out the dispute's actual shape rather than its stated one. "The overtime's real, and it's a legitimate grievance, but underneath it, what I'm actually hearing is people who've been here two years, watched the settlement grow past ten thousand, and still don't have a clear answer to the question every one of them eventually asks: when does this actually finish being construction and start being the thing we came here to build. Hassoun's committee isn't the only one asking that question. It's just the version with a Council seat attached to it."

"What do you need from me," Ilse asked.

"Not a solution to the overtime dispute. I can solve that myself, and I will, by this afternoon," Sefu said. "I need you to understand that this is the first crack in something I've watched build for months, quietly, under the surface of every schedule I've had to redraw. People believed in this project enough to leave whole lives behind for it. Belief has a maintenance cost, the same way any structure does, and I don't think anyone on this project's leadership side has fully budgeted for what happens if that cost comes due before the machine does anything visible enough to refill it."

She solved the overtime dispute the way she'd promised, by early afternoon, splitting the disputed shift hours evenly between the two crews and adding a rotation clause neither side had thought to ask for, and the second shift's informal representatives withdrew the walkout threat within the hour, satisfied in the narrow, immediate way people were satisfied when a concrete grievance actually got addressed. Sefu did not mistake that satisfaction for the larger question having been answered, and told Ilse so plainly, in a message she sent that evening rather than waiting for the next scheduled status meeting.

"The overtime's fixed," Sefu wrote. "The question underneath it isn't, and it won't be fixed by anything I can do from my own office. I wanted you to have that distinction clearly, in writing, rather than let today's resolution read as more reassuring than it actually is."

Ilse thought of Hassoun's floor, the one he'd told her he didn't know the exact location of, and understood that Sefu was describing the same floor from a different angle, closer to the ground, measured in shift schedules instead of Council votes.

"I don't have anything to refill it with yet," Ilse said. "Not until the expedition either finds what it's looking for or doesn't."

"I know," Sefu said. "I'm not asking you to manufacture something you don't have. I'm asking you to know the cost is accumulating, on both sides of this project at once, so that when it finally comes due, wherever it comes due first, you're not hearing about it for the first time in that exact moment."

Ilse walked the training bay that evening, unannounced, the way she had made a habit of doing since the first town session, and found Ingrid Bakke among the second shift's crew, still in her work gear, waiting out the last hour before the overtime dispute's resolution reached the floor.

"Word travels fast," Ilse said.

"It always does, on a settlement this size acting like it's still small enough for everyone to know everyone," Ingrid said. "I wasn't one of the ones threatening to walk. I want you to know that before you assume I was."

"I didn't assume it," Ilse said. "I came to ask you something else. Sefu thinks this dispute is really about a question underneath the overtime. When does construction finish being construction. I'd like to know if that's true, from someone who isn't required to answer diplomatically."

Ingrid considered the question longer than Ilse expected. "It's true for some of us," she said finally. "Not all. I didn't come here for the machine, if I'm honest. I came here because the charter said something I'd never heard a project say out loud before, that it would tell the truth even when the truth was inconvenient, and I wanted to work somewhere that actually meant that. The machine finishing or not finishing matters less to me than whether you keep doing what you did in that town hall, every time it gets harder to do it. Some of the others feel differently. They came for the machine. I understand why the wait is starting to cost them more than it's costing me."

"And if the wait gets longer," Ilse asked, "before I have anything to show for it beyond another honest answer."

"Then I stay," Ingrid said, "and I imagine some of the others don't, and I don't think either choice makes anyone here a worse person than the other. I just think you should know which kind of belief you're actually spending, when you spend it, because it isn't all the same currency."

VIII

Aboard the Constantine, the crew processed the encounter each in their own register, the way Ilse imagined any crew processed a thing large enough to matter and small enough to have cost nothing.

"It never even hailed us," Mirek said, hours later, still turning the encounter over the way a pilot turns over any near miss, looking for the shape of what almost happened. "Didn't fire, didn't call out, didn't do anything except run the second we were close enough to register. That's not a ship deciding we're dangerous. That's a ship that already knows exactly what dangerous looks like, and decided we were close enough to the shape of it not to risk finding out for certain."

"The shape of what, though," Tomasz asked. "We don't match anything in the sparse cultural-contact archive this mission was briefed with. Whatever profile that ship read off us, it wasn't built from anything humanity's ever logged."

"Maybe it wasn't reading us at all," Anke said, from engineering, joining the conversation the way she usually did, several beats behind everyone else and then landing somewhere none of them had considered. "Maybe it was reading the hull. We're flying a heavy gunship, refit or not. Whatever's out here, if it's ever had reason to fear something shaped like this silhouette before, it wouldn't need to know a single thing about us personally to decide running was the only sane option. It just needs to recognize the shape."

Tavik said nothing for a long moment, turning the thought over with a stillness Ilse would have recognized from the observation platform, the same stillness that had once, years earlier, produced the sentence that ended up carrying more weight in a Council hearing than any equation anyone else in that room had derived.

"If that's true," he said finally, "then whatever ran from us today wasn't running from anything we did. It was running from something that happened to it before we ever existed, using a shape it's learned, the hard way, to fear on sight. I don't like what that implies about what's actually out here, waiting for us further down this trajectory. I like even less not knowing which direction the fear runs, whether everything we meet decides we're dangerous and runs, the way that ship did, or whether something out here reads this hull the opposite way, and doesn't run at all."

Tomasz spent the rest of that shift running the encounter's telemetry against every archived cultural-contact record his briefing packet contained, and found nothing, a negative result he reported to the bridge with the same careful precision he'd used to report his confidence interval before departure. "I want to be clear about what a negative result actually means here," he said. "It doesn't mean nothing like this exists. It means nothing like this has ever been logged by anyone who made it home afterward to log it. I don't know if that should make us more careful or less. I know it means we're the first data point in whatever record eventually gets built out of this."

"Then we write the record carefully," Tavik said, "the same way I asked Mirek to. Somebody's going to read this someday and need it to be honest, the way I needed Verdent's records to be honest and mostly wasn't given that. We can at least do that much for whoever comes after us."

Nobody on the bridge had an answer for that beyond his own resolve, and Tavik did not ask the larger question again that evening, though it followed him back to his quarters the way Verdent's arithmetic had once followed him for forty years, sitting just under the surface of everything else he was thinking about.

Mirek found him there an hour later, off shift, carrying two ration packs neither of them particularly wanted, using the errand the way Ilse suspected he'd learned to use small errands his whole career, as an excuse to check on a commander who would never have admitted to needing checking on.

"You've been quiet since the bridge," Mirek said.

"I'm thinking about the two ways this trajectory could end," Tavik said. "Everything out here decides we're dangerous, the way that ship did, and we come home having learned only that something we don't understand is afraid of us. Or something out here decides the opposite, and doesn't run, and we find out the hard way what a silhouette like ours actually costs when the thing looking at it isn't scared enough to leave."

"Which one are you expecting," Mirek asked.

"I don't let myself expect either one anymore," Tavik said. "I spent forty years expecting outcomes and being wrong often enough that I stopped trusting my own expectations to mean anything. I try, now, to just carry the question honestly until the universe answers it, instead of pretending I already know which answer is coming. It's a harder way to live. I've found it's also the only way that doesn't leave me lying to the people counting on me."

Mirek set one of the ration packs down without eating it, a quiet gesture from a man who had come for a different reason than the food. "For what it's worth," he said, "I'd rather serve under someone who admits he doesn't know than someone who's certain and wrong. I had a commander like that once, on a supply route. Certainty's what got the partner I mentioned in my interview killed. I don't need you to know how this ends, Tavik. I need you to keep telling us the truth about what you don't know, the way you have so far."

"That's the only promise I've got left to make anyone," Tavik said. "I intend to keep it as long as I'm able, and I'd rather you hold me to it than let me forget I made it."

IX

Ilse stood on the observation platform the night the Constantine's status report came in confirming the sighting, reading it twice before she let herself feel anything about it, a discipline she had built over three years of learning not to react to a report until she'd actually understood what it said rather than what she feared it might say, a habit that had cost her a great deal of composure to build over those three years, and had, more than once since, saved her from misreading a piece of good news as bad, or a piece of bad news as something smaller and safer than it actually was.

No cost paid. No contact made. A ship that ran, a crew that came home from the encounter exactly as whole as it had left, and underneath all of it, the first confirmed proof that whatever waited along that cold trajectory was not empty space at all, but territory something else already lived in, already feared, already ran from, for reasons this project had not yet begun to understand.

She thought of the gantry, of the unfamiliar weight she had carried standing there after the Constantine cleared the dock, and understood, reading the report a second time, that the weight had not actually lifted in the eleven days since, only changed shape, from the fear of sending someone into the unknown to the fear of what the unknown had just confirmed was actually out there, waiting, already afraid of something before her people had ever arrived to be feared themselves.

Corin found her there, the way Corin always did, on evenings this platform had learned to specialize in.

"Hassoun's floor," Corin said, without preamble, having clearly heard some version of the confrontation already through whatever channel carried news across the settlement faster than official reports ever managed. "Sefu's walkout. The Constantine's first contact. All in the same week."

"None of it's cost yet," Ilse said. "Not really. A hard conversation, a dispute Sefu already solved, a ship that ran before anything happened. If I'm honest, that's the strangest part of tonight. Everything that could have gone wrong this week, in either direction, on either side of this project, came right up to the edge of costing something and then didn't."

"That won't hold forever," Corin said. "You know that."

"I know that," Ilse said. "I think I've known it since the vote. I think what surprised me tonight wasn't that the cost is coming. It's how many separate directions it's coming from at once, all of them rising together, none of them arriving yet. I keep waiting to feel like we're ahead of something. Tonight I only feel like we're standing very still in the moment before several different things all decide, independently, whether this is the week they finally land."

Corin was quiet for a while, watching the same lights Ilse had been watching, before saying the thing that had clearly brought him to the platform in the first place rather than any of the week's smaller crises.

"I keep coming back to something Anke said, secondhand, in the report," Corin said. "That the ship might not have been reading them at all. Just the hull. The shape of a thing it already knew to fear, with no need to know anything else about who was actually flying it." He looked at Ilse directly. "I think about that in terms of what we're building here too. Every settlement watching this project from a distance, every delegate on Hassoun's committee, every worker deciding whether to stay another year. None of them can actually see the machine. They can't read what it is any more than that ship could read the Constantine's crew. All any of them have is the shape of it, from the outside, and the shape is a moon's worth of resources and no visible result, and I don't know that we get to blame anyone for reacting to the shape the way that ship reacted to a silhouette it had learned, somewhere, to be afraid of."

"You think we look dangerous," Ilse said. "From the outside."

"I think we look unaccountable," Corin said, "which is close enough to dangerous that I'm not sure the distinction matters to someone standing where Hassoun stands, watching a resource figure grow without a result attached to it. I don't have a fix for that tonight. I don't think there is one, short of the machine actually working. I just think it's worth naming, the same way Tavik apparently thought it was worth naming on that bridge. We keep assuming the danger in this project is the thing we're building. I think there's a second danger running alongside it this whole time, which is simply what we look like, from a distance, to people who can't see inside the reasons the way we can."

Ilse thought of Ingrid, in the training bay, distinguishing between the belief that had brought her here and the belief that had brought others, two different currencies being spent against the same silence, and understood that Corin was describing the same asymmetry from the other end of the settlement, the distance between what a thing actually was and what it looked like from outside the room where the truth of it lived.

"Then we keep doing what we've done since the hearing," Ilse said. "We tell the truth as plainly as we can to whoever's close enough to hear it, and we accept that the ones too far away to hear it are going to keep reacting to the shape instead of the substance, and there isn't a version of this project honest enough to fix that from here. Only the machine finishing ever fixes that. Everything else is just us, standing very still, hoping the shape holds a little longer."

They stood together a while longer, the gas giant's pale curve fixed in its permanent place above them, the Anvil's spreading lights below, and somewhere past the edge of every chart this project had ever drawn, a ship full of people neither of them could see, carrying a question home that none of the four of them yet had the words to answer, moving steadily closer, one uneventful day at a time, toward whatever this trajectory actually intended to cost them.
