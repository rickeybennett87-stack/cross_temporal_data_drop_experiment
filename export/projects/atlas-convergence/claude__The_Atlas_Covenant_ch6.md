ASSUMED COMPLIANCE

I

The report came in on the same relay cadence every expedition report had come in on since the Constantine left, a compressed data burst timed to the narrow windows when the ship's position and the Anvil's own orbital swing lined up close enough to punch a signal through, and Ilse had trained herself, over four months, not to feel anything when the alert chimed until she'd actually opened the file.

She felt something this time before she'd read a single line, because the burst was three times the size of every report before it, and size, on a channel this expensive, only ever meant one thing: raw data nobody back home had processed yet.

She read Tavik's summary first, the way she always did, four sentences confirming the crew alive, the hull intact enough to continue, and then the line that made her stop breathing normally for a moment: engagement with unidentified hostile vessel, sixteen days prior, burst transmission received during exchange, untranslated, attached in full, recommend immediate priority analysis.

She sat with the console's light the only light in the room, the rest of the administrative module dark and quiet around her, and found herself doing the thing she'd done at every turning point of this project since run four hundred and eleven: reading the same sentence twice, as if a second pass might soften what the first one had already delivered whole.

It didn't.

She did not wait for morning. She did not wait for Perrin, or for the standing protocol that routed anything flagged hostile-contact through a three-person review before it reached a decision-maker's desk. She pulled the raw signal file herself, at two in the morning, on a console in an administrative module three other people were sleeping in the next room over, and sent it directly to the one person on the Anvil who had spent two years building translation tools for exactly this contingency and had never once been asked to use them for anything but archived comet debris.

Yusuf Karimi answered on the third ping, voice thick with sleep, and stopped sounding sleepy about four seconds into hearing what she was sending him.

"How fast," Ilse said, before he could ask anything else.

"I don't know yet," Yusuf said. "I don't know what I'm looking at yet. Give me the file and give me an hour before you ask me that question again."

"You have forty minutes," Ilse said, "because I am not going to be able to sit in this room for an hour not knowing, and I would rather you resent me a little than have me pacing outside your door."

He gave her thirty-eight.

II

She had called Corin and Perrin in by the time Yusuf's message came through, both of them arriving still half-dressed, neither of them asking why the hour didn't matter, because both of them had learned, over three years, exactly what kind of news justified this one.

Yusuf's face filled the console screen, and Ilse understood, before he said a word, that whatever he had found had cost him something to sit with alone for thirty-eight minutes.

"It's not clean," he said. "Compressed, degraded, recorded raw off a live channel during active combat, so there are gaps I've had to fill with probability weighting rather than certainty. I want that caveat on the record before I read it to you, because I don't want you building a war plan on three words I guessed at."

"Read it," Ilse said.

"Power down drives and submit to the Gek First Spawn," Yusuf said. "Prepare to be boarded. Or you will be destroyed."

Nobody spoke.

Ilse had spent three years learning to sit in silences that other people rushed to fill, and she made herself sit in this one anyway, because filling it too fast felt, in that moment, like a way of pretending the sentence weighed less than it did.

It was Perrin who broke it, and not with a question about linguistics.

"They shot first," Perrin said. "They shot first, disabled a third of the shielding before anyone on that ship had finished registering contact, and only then told them what surrendering would have looked like. That's not a warning. A warning comes before the shot. That's an order given to something they'd already decided didn't get the warning."

"That's not how first contact is supposed to go," Corin said, quieter than Ilse had heard him in a long time. "Every model any of us have ever built for this, every settlement's own contact protocol, assumes hesitation on both sides, some attempt to establish intent before anyone commits to violence. This isn't hesitation. This is a group that opened with force because force was the only opening they thought the situation required."

"They weren't afraid of us," Ilse said, and heard her own voice come out flatter than she meant it to. "That's what I keep coming back to. Every account I've ever read of first contact between strangers, human history included, assumes some baseline uncertainty on both sides, because neither side knows yet whether the other is dangerous. This wasn't uncertain. They fired the way you fire at something you've already fully assessed and found beneath negotiation."

"Assumed compliance," Perrin said. "That's the phrase I keep landing on. They didn't ask the Constantine to surrender. They told it to, past tense, like the outcome was already settled and the message was just informing the losing side of what happens next if they're slow about it."

"And First Spawn," Corin said. "That's not a name for a ship. That's a rank, or a designation inside something larger, the same way a naval unit identifies itself by fleet and division rather than by the individual hull. Whatever the Gek are, and I don't have anything to tell you about that word beyond the fact that it's the first time any of us have heard it, First Spawn implies there's a Second, a Third, a structure underneath it large enough to need the distinction at all."

Ilse looked at the two sentences sitting side by side on her console, the order and the threat, and felt the shape of the thing arrive whole rather than in pieces, the way a bad diagnosis sometimes arrives all at once instead of building gradually.

"Say it," she told them. "Whichever one of us says it first, say it, because I don't think staying in the room where nobody's said it yet helps any of us think clearly."

Corin was the one who said it.

"This might be the start of a war," Corin said. "Not a border skirmish. Not a misunderstanding between two survey ships that can be walked back with an apology. A structured, hierarchical, and by every indication we currently have, confident hostile power just found four of our people alone in open space and treated the encounter as a foregone conclusion. I don't know what's behind that confidence. I know I would not build a doctrine like that around a species I thought I could lose to."

Nobody argued with him.

III

Perrin was the first one to move, because moving was the only thing she knew how to do with a sentence that size before it had finished landing on her.

"If this is what it sounds like," she said, "it isn't a settlement problem. It's a Council problem, and if we sit on it past the next relay window trying to decide how frightened to sound, we'll be the ones explaining, later, why forty other settlements found out from a rumor instead of from us." She was already pulling up the standing interstellar alert format on a second console, hands moving faster than her voice, the two of them working in different registers at once. "I want a threat notice drafted before sunrise. Not analysis. Not reassurance. A hostile contact, confirmed, organized, unprovoked, transmitted in language a Second and a Third might exist behind. Every settlement within relay range needs that sentence exactly as ugly as it actually is."

"You think it's already past us," Corin said.

"I think a species confident enough to skip the warning shot for one ship isn't going to stop being confident once they find the rest of us," Perrin said. "I think the only responsible assumption left is that whatever found the Constantine either already knows this system exists, or will, and that every day we spend deciding how to phrase that to people softens the warning by exactly the amount of time it takes someone else to react to it instead."

Ilse thought of the founding charter's oldest promise, the one Tavik had put into words in a mess hall eleven months before she was ever born into this version of her life, a place to be wrong safely, at any scale, and understood, watching Perrin's hands move, that the promise had just acquired a second meaning neither of them had drafted for. It was one thing to be wrong safely about a machine. It was another thing entirely to discover the safety they had spent three years building might not extend past the edge of their own system.

"Draft it," Ilse said. "I want to read every word before it goes out, and I want it to say what actually happened, not what makes this settlement look more prepared than it is. If this becomes the first shot in something larger, I would rather every settlement that hears about it from us know exactly how little we actually understand, than trust a confidence I can't back up."

"That's going to frighten people," Perrin said.

"They should be frightened," Ilse said. "I'd rather they be frightened and warned than calm and unprepared. I've spent three years telling this settlement the truth even when it cost me something to say it. I'm not going to start softening it now, on the one occasion where the truth might actually save someone."

Corin stayed after Perrin left to draft the notice, watching Ilse read the translation for what had to be the tenth time, the words no smaller on the eleventh reading than they had been on the first.

"You're thinking about the machine," Corin said. It wasn't a question.

"I'm thinking about what it would mean to have it finished already," Ilse said. "Not the power. The promise underneath it. A place to try things wrong without a civilization paying for the mistake. I keep wondering whether something like that could have told us this was coming before it cost four people their next nine days of sleep and one of them, apparently, considerably more than that." She set the translation down. "I don't think it could have. I don't think anything could have told us a species existed before it introduced itself with a warning shot. I think I'm just looking for somewhere to put the feeling that we built something meant to prevent exactly this kind of cost, and it wasn't finished in time to prevent this one."

"That's not a failure of the project," Corin said. "That's just the shape of building something slower than the universe's own timeline for finding you."

"I know," Ilse said. "Knowing it doesn't change how it sits."

IV

The news did not stay contained to that room, and it did not need Sefu's help to travel this time, because Perrin had learned, months ago now, that the gap between an event and the settlement's own account of it was the gap rumor lived in, and she was not going to leave this one open.

She drafted the settlement notice herself before dawn, plain language, no euphemism, an encounter with a hostile unidentified power, injuries but no fatalities among the crew, an active threat whose scope was not yet known, and Ilse read it once, made one change, and told her to send it before either of them lost the nerve that honesty required.

The reaction reached the administrative corridor within the hour, and it did not arrive as questions.

Hassoun's committee called an emergency session before noon, and Hassoun himself, standing in the doorway of Ilse's office before the formal request had even finished routing through Perrin's channel, did not wait for procedure either.

"You are telling six thousand people," Hassoun said, "eleven thousand if you count the whole workforce, that there is a power out there confident enough to shoot our people first and dictate terms second, and you found this out because you sent four of them alone into open space on the strength of a comet fragment and a hunch about lonsdaleite."

"Yes," Ilse said.

"I want you to understand what that does to every argument I have made on your behalf for three years," Hassoun said. "I told my constituents this project cost less than the alternative. I did not tell them the alternative might now include a hostile power that knows this system exists and considers us beneath the courtesy of a warning shot."

"I don't have a way to make that sound smaller than it is," Ilse said. "I'm not going to try. Whatever you need to say to your committee, I'll stand behind the same words I'd use myself. We do not know the scope of this. We do not know if First Spawn refers to one hostile actor or the leading edge of something larger. What I can tell you is that the crew survived, that the settlement's own security posture is being reviewed as of this morning, and that I am not going to spend the next month pretending this is smaller than it feels, to you or to anyone standing behind you."

"That is not going to be enough for some of them," Hassoun said.

"I know," Ilse said. "It has never once been enough. It's the only thing I have that's actually true."

By the second day the mess halls were carrying a different texture than they had in months, not panic exactly, something quieter and more corrosive, workers checking the sky through the observation windows on breaks the way people check weather before a storm they've been told is coming but can't yet see.

Ilse found the change in a single face before Sefu ever brought her the shape of it in the aggregate. She was crossing the training bay's outer corridor, on her way to the security briefing Sefu had called for that afternoon, when she recognized a young man standing near the entrance, one of the three trainees who had cleared the growth chamber before it failed, the one who had asked her, in the raw hour after Ingrid's death, whether he and the others had done something wrong to be the ones left standing.

"Emre," he said, when she stopped in front of him and admitted, plainly, that she still didn't know his name. "Emre Solak. You told me it wasn't a test either of us had passed or failed. I've thought about that a great deal since."

"I remember what I told you," Ilse said. "I meant it. I still mean it."

"I believe you," Emre said. "That's not why I'm standing here instead of in the bay. I keep thinking, since the notice went out, that Ingrid died because something in this project we built ourselves wasn't finished yet. Now there's something out there that might not even wait for us to finish it. I don't know how to be afraid of one thing without it swallowing the ability to be afraid of the other properly. I think I'm just afraid all the time now, and I don't know which part of it I'm supposed to be doing something about."

"Both," Ilse said. "I don't have a version of that answer where you only have to carry one fear at a time. I wish I did. What I can tell you is that being afraid of both things at once doesn't mean either fear is wrong, and it doesn't mean you're carrying it worse than anyone else standing in this corridor with you right now."

Emre nodded, the same settling motion Ilse had come to recognize in people who had decided, however reluctantly, to keep standing where they were rather than walk away from it, and returned to the bay without saying anything further, and Ilse carried the shape of his question with her the rest of the day, because she did not have a better answer for it than the one she'd just given, and she suspected she was going to need one before this was over.

Sefu brought her the wider version of it that afternoon, in the same flat register she used for anything too important to soften.

"They're not asking whether the machine will finish anymore," Sefu said. "They're asking whether finishing it fast enough still matters if something out there decides to end this before it does. I don't have an answer for that one. I'm not sure you do either."

"I don't," Ilse said. "I have the same answer I've had since the hearing. We keep building the thing that lets us stop losing people the expensive way, and we tell the truth about how far we still have to go, and we hope the two clocks, the machine's and whatever's coming, run in an order that doesn't cost us everything before we're ready. I know that isn't reassurance. I stopped being able to offer reassurance a long time ago. I can only offer the truth, at whatever pace it actually arrives."

"Security's drafting a patrol rotation," Sefu said. "Nothing that could stop what hit the Constantine. Enough to buy warning time if something comes through this system the same way. I wanted you to hear it from me before you heard it as another rumor moving faster than the actual plan."

"Do it," Ilse said. "Tell them to be honest about what the patrols can and can't actually do. I'd rather people know exactly how thin that protection is than trust something sturdier than it is."

She found Boro in the training bay that same evening, the first time she had seen him back inside it since the morning Sefu came to tell her the first of Ingrid's cohort had asked to see the chamber. The growth frame had been rebuilt weeks earlier, a cleaner geometry replacing the one that failed, and Boro stood in front of it now with his arms crossed, not working, just looking, the way a person looks at a scar to remind themselves it healed rather than to reopen it.

"You heard," Ilse said.

"Everyone's heard," Boro said. "I keep waiting to feel it the way I felt Ingrid. I don't think I can, yet. It's too large and too far away to sit in the same place in me that she sits in. I think that should probably worry me more than it does."

"It doesn't worry me," Ilse said. "I don't think grief and fear are supposed to occupy the same space at the same size. I think that's just what it costs to be a person carrying both of them at once."

Boro was quiet for a moment, running one hand along the growth frame's outer housing without seeming to notice he was doing it. "I keep thinking about something you told me, that day," he said. "That the material did what it did, not me. I believed you. I think I mostly still believe you. Now there's something out there that decided, apparently without a second thought, that four of our people weren't worth a warning shot, and I find myself wondering whether finishing this lattice fast enough is the only thing standing between that decision and the rest of us. I don't know if that's a fair weight to put back on my own hands after I only just managed to set the last one down."

"It isn't fair," Ilse said. "I'm not going to tell you it is, because I don't think you'd believe me, and I don't think you should. I can tell you that the weight isn't only yours to carry. It's mine, and Corin's, and every person on this project who decided, three years ago, that the machine mattered enough to spend a moon's worth of resources building it. You don't get to volunteer for the entire weight just because your hands are the ones that touch the material last."

"I want to keep working," Boro said. "Not because I've stopped being afraid of the lattice. Because I think, now, there's a second reason to finish it that I didn't have three weeks ago, and I don't want to spend whatever time we actually have left standing in front of this frame deciding whether I've earned the right to touch it again."

"Then work," Ilse said. "Carefully. Honestly, the way you always have. I'm not going to ask you to work faster because something out there might be coming. I watched what happens when this project tries to outrun its own caution. I'd rather finish this slower and finish it whole than finish it quickly and lose someone else to the difference."

Boro nodded, and for the first time since Ilse had found him there, something in his shoulders eased, not fully, not the way it might have a year earlier, but enough that she recognized it as the beginning of a man deciding to keep standing where he was rather than walk away from it.

V

The formal request arrived through Perrin's office on the fourth day, routed with the same procedural weight the original charter review had carried three years earlier, and Ilse recognized the shape of it before she'd read past the first line.

"Delegate Okonkwo-Marrow," Perrin said, setting the transcript down between them. "Marrow's successor on the Outer Trade Compact seat, since Marrow's retirement two years ago, though I understand the family resemblance in the argument is not a coincidence. He's requesting an emergency Council session to review the project's mandate restrictions in light of, and I'm reading his own language here, 'a confirmed existential threat that renders the charter's civilian-only clause a luxury this species can no longer responsibly afford.'"

"He wants the weapon," Ilse said.

"He wants the option of the weapon," Perrin said. "He's careful about the distinction in writing, which tells me he expects this to be contested. He's not asking the Council to authorize military use today. He's asking it to authorize beginning the modeling work now, so that the capability exists if a vote to use it ever becomes necessary later."

"That's the same argument his predecessor made in the original hearing," Corin said. "Word for word, almost. What prevents this Council, or any future Council, from repurposing a research instrument into the most dangerous strategic weapon this species has ever built. Marrow asked that as a warning. His successor is asking it as an application."

Ilse read the request twice, the same instinct that had made her read Yusuf's translation twice, though this time she was looking for a way out of the sentence rather than a way to soften it, and did not find one.

"He's not wrong that the threat is real," she said. "I'm not going to stand in front of that chamber and pretend Marrow's original fear was overblown, three years after I promised the Council we'd built the highest barrier we knew how to build against exactly this kind of pressure. I think he's wrong about what building the capability now actually buys anyone. A model that can simulate conflict outcomes doesn't exist in isolation from the rest of what this system does. If we start that work, even framed as contingency planning, even framed as an option rather than a use, we've crossed the one line the charter was written specifically to make expensive to cross. And I don't think a species confident enough to fire without a warning shot is going to be deterred by our contingency planning. I think we'd be building the temptation Marrow warned about, on the promise that we'd only ever look at it and never use it, and I don't believe any promise like that survives contact with an actual war, if that's what's coming."

"You're going to refuse him," Perrin said. It wasn't a question, but it carried the weight of one.

"I'm going to refuse the request as written," Ilse said. "I'm not going to refuse the underlying question, because I think it's a fair one and I don't get to dismiss it just because I don't like where it leads. I'll stand in front of that chamber and tell them exactly what I told Iyoro three years ago. The charter requires unanimous ratification to expand the mandate, not administrative convenience, not an emergency exception carved out the first time the pressure got real enough to make one look reasonable. If this Council believes the danger has changed enough to justify changing the charter, it can do that the way the charter says it has to be done, in the open, on the record, with every delegate who voted for the original restriction having to vote again, in front of the people who'll remember exactly what they said the first time."

"That could take months," Corin said. "Longer, if it's contested the way I think it will be."

"Then it takes months," Ilse said. "I'm not going to let the fastest possible response to a threat also be the one that quietly erodes the only promise standing between this project and becoming the thing Marrow was afraid of from the very first hearing. Iyoro voted for us because we told her the truth instead of a comfortable answer. I'm not going to spend that trust the first time keeping it costs something real."

Perrin was quiet for a moment, weighing something Ilse couldn't fully read on her face. "I agree with you," she said finally. "I want you to understand what agreeing with you is going to cost both of us over the next several months, because Okonkwo-Marrow is not going to be the only delegate who thinks contingency planning sounds reasonable when the alternative is doing nothing while something out there decides whether we're worth destroying. Some of the people who voted for the charter's restriction three years ago are going to vote to loosen it now, and they're not going to be wrong to be afraid. They're just going to be wrong about what the fear should cost us."

"I know," Ilse said. "I'd rather be the person holding the line and losing some of that vote than the person who moved the line the first time it became inconvenient to hold. I've spent three years telling this settlement that honesty doesn't get easier to keep just because it gets more expensive. I don't get to stop believing that the first time the expense is real instead of theoretical."

The emergency session convened four days later, in a smaller chamber than the one Ilse remembered from the original hearing, a relay-linked gallery rather than a physical gathering, most of the delegates appearing on screens rather than in the room, and Ilse found the smaller scale did nothing to soften what waited for her inside it.

Delegate Okonkwo-Marrow opened without preamble, the family resemblance Perrin had warned her about visible in the shape of the argument before it was visible in anything else. "Dr. Okwuosa," he said, "I want to be plain about what I am and am not asking this Council to approve today. I am not asking for authorization to use this system as a weapon. I am asking this Council to authorize the modeling work required to know, in advance, whether we could, if the day ever comes when not knowing costs us everything. I would like you to explain to this chamber why that modest and entirely defensive request should be refused, given what your own settlement's alert notice told forty other settlements four days ago."

"I'll explain it plainly," Ilse said, "the same way I explained the original restriction to this Council three years ago, because I don't think the honest answer has changed just because the fear behind the question has gotten larger. A model capable of simulating conflict outcomes with any real fidelity does not stay a defensive curiosity once it exists. It becomes a capability, and capabilities get used by whoever holds them, eventually, regardless of the intentions attached to the day they were built. I am not questioning your intentions, Delegate. I am telling you that intentions are not what this charter was built to constrain. It was built to constrain what becomes possible once the capability exists at all, because possibility, once built, does not require anyone's continued good faith to eventually get exercised."

"Then tell this chamber what you propose instead," Okonkwo-Marrow said, "while something out there may already be deciding whether we are worth a second visit."

"I propose what the charter already requires," Ilse said. "If this Council believes the danger has grown large enough to justify changing the mandate, change it the way the charter says it must be changed. Unanimous ratification, on the record, argued in the open, the way the original restriction was argued in the open three years ago. I am not asking this chamber to trust me over its own fear. I am asking it to make this decision the hard way, in daylight, rather than the easy way, in an emergency session convened four days after a threat nobody had time to fully understand yet."

Delegate Iyoro, silent through the exchange until that point, spoke for the first time, her voice carrying the same weight it had carried the day she cast one of the deciding votes on the project's founding. "I want the record to reflect something," Iyoro said. "I voted for this project's restriction because Dr. Okwuosa told this chamber, honestly, that she did not know what her machine might become, and that she intended to hold the line on that uncertainty rather than resolve it in whatever direction proved convenient. I am hearing the same honesty today, under considerably more pressure than she faced three years ago. I do not think fear is a reason to abandon a standard we adopted specifically to survive the moment fear made it inconvenient. I would like this Council to vote on the actual charter amendment, in the open, rather than granting an emergency carve-out that quietly accomplishes the same thing without ever having to be argued in daylight."

The session did not resolve that day. It did not resolve that week. Ilse left the gallery with no ruling, no vote, and no promise beyond Iyoro's insistence that whatever changed would have to change the hard way, and found, walking back to the administrative module afterward, that she could not decide whether that outcome felt like a victory or simply the postponement of a fight she had not yet found a way to actually win.

VI

The Constantine received the colony's formal response nine days later, on the same relay cadence, and Anke was the one on shift when the alert chimed, calling the others to the bridge before she'd finished reading it herself.

"Translation confirmed," she read aloud, once Tavik, Mirek, and Tomasz had gathered close enough to hear. "Colony leadership assessment: hostile contact represents organized structure, scale unknown. Interstellar alert issued to all settlements within relay range. Recommend continued caution, no unprovoked engagement, prioritize survival and return of expedition data over completion of primary objective if forced to choose. Political situation at home destabilizing. Proceed at your discretion. We trust your judgment more than we trust our own certainty about what's actually out there."

Mirek was quiet for a moment. "That's not an order," he said. "That's Ilse telling us she doesn't know either, and trusting us to decide anyway."

"It's the only kind of order she's ever given anyone," Tavik said. "I'd have been more worried if she'd sent us something cleaner than that. Clean orders come from people pretending to know more than they do. I'd rather trust the uncertain ones."

Tomasz looked up from the trajectory plot he hadn't stopped refining since the ambush. "Destabilizing," he repeated. "That word's doing a lot of work in one sentence. What does she think is coming apart down there."

"Doesn't matter what we think," Tavik said. "It's not something we can fix from out here. What matters is what we do with the discretion she just handed us." He looked at each of them in turn, and something in the set of his shoulders made even Mirek go still. "We're closer to the trajectory's end than we are to home. Turning back doesn't make us safer, it just spends nine more days finding that out the hard way, in the same stretch of space where we already got hit once. I want to keep moving. I want to do it slower, quieter, and readier than we've been moving so far. I'm not going to pretend that's a comfortable choice. I think it's the only one that doesn't waste what already happened to us."

"You're asking us to vote," Anke said.

"I'm asking you what you think," Tavik said. "I've made the decision I think is right. I'm not going to hide behind a vote to make it easier to carry if it goes wrong. I want to hear what each of you actually believes before I commit us to it, because I'd rather change my mind now than find out later I should have."

Anke went first. "Core's stable enough for another push," she said. "I don't love it. I don't think loving it was ever going to be part of this job."

"I can fly it quieter," Mirek said. "Lower emissions profile, slower approach vectors, more time spent listening before we move. I lost someone once to a corridor I trusted because nothing had ever gone wrong there before. I'm not going to make that mistake twice in the same year."

Tomasz was the last to answer, and when he did, his voice had none of the uncertainty it usually carried into a room full of people waiting on his math. "I want to find what we came for," he said. "Not because I've stopped being afraid. Because I've done the numbers on turning back, and they don't actually make anyone safer, they just make the whole trip cost something for nothing. I'd rather it cost something for a reason."

Tavik nodded once, and did not thank any of them, because thanking them would have made it sound like a favor rather than what it actually was, four people choosing the same hard thing with their eyes open.

"Then we keep going," he said. "Quieter. Slower. Ready."

VII

Anke found him three hours later, in the narrow crew quarters he used as a medical bay whenever the real one was occupied with equipment rather than people, and she had not come looking for what she found.

She had come to log a routine inventory count of the trauma kit's remaining suture strips, a task that had nothing to do with Tavik at all, and she found the kit's seal already broken, more strips gone than the injury log accounted for, and Tavik himself with his shirt half off and a dressing half changed, caught in the exact position a person is caught in when they've timed every previous change around everyone else's schedule and miscounted, once, by twenty minutes.

Neither of them spoke for a moment that felt considerably longer than it was.

"That's not a bruise," Anke said finally, looking at the wound rather than at him, because looking at him first would have meant starting the conversation with an accusation instead of a fact.

"No," Tavik agreed. "It isn't."

She crossed the small room and knelt beside him before he could stop her, and he let her, which told her almost as much as the wound itself did, because the Tavik she'd watched run this ship for four months would have found a way to wave her off if he'd had anything left in him to wave with.

The gash ran deeper than a strut's edge should have opened on someone wearing a properly sealed suit, and the tissue around it carried a heat and a discoloration that had nothing to do with a clean wound healing on schedule.

"This is infected," Anke said. "This has been infected for days. How long has it actually looked like this."

"Long enough that I stopped expecting it to look different," Tavik said.

"You told Mirek it was a scrape."

"I told Mirek what he needed to hear to keep flying this ship instead of watching me," Tavik said. "I'd tell you the same thing if I thought you'd believe it, and I think we're past the point where you would."

Anke sat back on her heels, and something in her chest did something she did not have a clean name for, an anger that wasn't quite anger, a fear that wasn't quite fear, both of them tangled around the plain fact of a man she had trusted with her life bleeding quietly in a room by himself for over a week.

"I need you to understand what I'm looking at," she said. "This isn't a scratch that heals if you stop mentioning it. Left alone, this becomes sepsis. I don't have the equipment on this ship to treat sepsis if it gets that far. I have a field kit built for cuts and burns and the occasional broken bone, not for a systemic infection with nine more days of transit standing between us and anything better."

"I know," Tavik said.

"Then why," Anke said, and heard her own voice crack in a way she hadn't planned for. "Why would you let it get this far rather than say something the day it happened."

"Because the day it happened, we were sixteen days from an ambush that had already cost this crew three injuries and every ounce of certainty any of us had left," Tavik said. "Because I looked at what this crew could actually absorb, that week, and I decided a fourth casualty, the one everyone actually looks to for the decisions, wasn't a cost this ship could carry on top of everything else. I've made worse calculations than that one, Anke, with worse outcomes attached to them. I know exactly what it costs to decide, on someone else's behalf, what they can and can't bear to know. I decided it anyway. I'd like to tell you I regret it. I don't, not yet, because the alternative was grounding this mission psychologically at the exact moment it needed everyone steady, over an injury I still believed I could manage myself."

"You can't manage this yourself," Anke said. "Not anymore. Maybe you never could."

"No," Tavik agreed. "I don't think I can. That's the part I need from you now, and it's not treatment, because I don't think you have what treatment would actually require. It's silence. A few more days. Long enough to see whether we're close enough to something worth finding that turning back costs more than continuing."

Anke stared at him for a long moment, running the calculation she had spent her whole career trained to run and never once expected to run against her own commander: what a system does when the thing keeping it stable is also the thing quietly failing inside it.

"If I say yes," she said slowly, "I need you to understand what that costs me. I didn't take this job to become the person who watches you die by inches and calls it discretion. If this gets worse, I'm not asking you anymore. I'm telling Mirek and Tomasz, and I'm doing it whether you've decided it's the right moment or not."

"That's fair," Tavik said. "I wouldn't ask you to hold it past that line. I'm only asking for the days we actually have left before we know what we're walking into."

"I hate this," Anke said, quiet enough that it barely carried the short distance between them. "I want you to know that plainly, because I don't think I'm going to say it again. I hate that I'm about to agree to this. I hate that some part of me still thinks you're right to ask. I don't know how to hold both of those at once without it costing me something I don't have a name for yet."

"I don't know how to make that easier for you," Tavik said. "I wish I did. I think the honest answer is that it isn't going to get easier, and you're going to carry it anyway, the same way I've been carrying worse things than this since before you were born."

She finished the dressing herself, properly this time, her hands steadier than her voice had been a moment earlier, and said nothing else before she left, because there was nothing left in the room that either of them could say that wouldn't have made it worse rather than better.

She did not sleep well that night, running the same two facts against each other on a loop that refused to resolve: that she had just agreed to hide a dying man's condition from the two people who trusted him most, and that some quiet, exhausted part of her still believed, against every professional instinct she had, that he might actually be right to ask it of her.

Tavik sat alone a long while after she left, the dressing fresh and tight against skin that no longer felt entirely like his own, and did not reach for sleep the way the hour would have told anyone else to. He thought of Ilse on the observation platform, months ago now, telling him she couldn't survive a story where he didn't come back whole, and understood that he had just spent whatever whole still meant to either of them on a decision he was not sure he could defend to her face if she were the one asking instead of Anke. He did not regret it. He told himself that plainly, the way he told himself most things now, without the comfort of pretending the telling made it easier to carry. He had buried a hundred and forty thousand people once for a mistake nobody had chosen on purpose, and he had promised himself, somewhere in the long grief that followed, that if he were ever given a choice this size again, he would choose it with his eyes open rather than discover its cost secondhand, the way he'd discovered Verdent's. This was that choice, made with his eyes open, and he intended to keep making it for as many more days as the trajectory required, whatever it ended up costing the four of them, or the three of them, before it was through.

VIII

Ilse found the observation platform empty for the first time in months, the way a room sometimes empties itself right before it fills with everyone who needs it at once, and stood there alone long enough to watch the gas giant's pale curve hold its same fixed place above a settlement that had, in the space of two weeks, learned to look at the sky differently than it had before the alert went out.

Corin found her there near midnight, carrying nothing, the way he'd carried nothing the night Ingrid died.

"Perrin's alert has been running twelve days now," Corin said. "Six settlements have acknowledged receipt. None of them have offered anything back beyond acknowledgment. I don't know if that's caution or if it's the sound of forty settlements deciding, individually, that they'd rather wait and see whether the danger is real before committing anything to it in writing."

"I'd have done the same thing, in their position," Ilse said. "I don't think I get to resent anyone for being as careful with their own people as I've tried to be with mine."

"The Constantine's latest report came in an hour before I found you here," Corin said. "Nothing new on the trajectory. Anke flagged a minor irregularity in the core's containment readings, nothing urgent, logged for follow-up rather than alarm. Tavik signed off on all of it personally, same as always."

Ilse felt something small and wordless move through her at that, a thing she could not have named if Corin had asked her to, and did not examine it any further than she examined most of the small, unplaceable weights this project handed her on a given week. "Good," she said. "I'd rather they flag the small things than let them accumulate into something bigger before anyone mentions it."

They stood together for a while, watching the same lights they'd watched through every hard turn this project had survived so far, and Ilse found herself thinking, not of the Gek, not of the war Corin had been the first to name out loud, but of the founding charter's oldest sentence, the one Tavik had given her a lifetime ago in a room full of skeptical delegates: a place to be wrong safely, at any scale, so that civilization-breaking failure becomes a phase of research rather than a fact of history.

"We built this to be the place we could afford to be wrong," Ilse said. "I don't think anyone building it ever imagined the thing we'd need to be right about first was whether something out there was going to give us the time to finish it at all."

"No," Corin agreed. "I don't think anyone imagined that. I don't think anyone ever does, until the thing they didn't imagine is the one standing in front of them."

"Do you think we have the time," Ilse asked.

"I don't know," Corin said. "I think that's the only honest answer either of us has left to give anyone tonight, and I think we're going to be giving it a great deal, from now on, to a great many people who are going to like it less than they liked any answer we've given them so far."

"Okonkwo-Marrow will bring the charter question back," Ilse said. "Not this month. Not while the memory of Iyoro's speech is still fresh enough to embarrass anyone who pushes too soon. But he'll bring it back, and the vote will be closer next time than it was today, because fear doesn't get less persuasive with age. It gets more patient."

"Then we hold it again," Corin said, "for as long as holding it is the honest thing to do. I don't have a better plan than that. I don't think there's ever been a better plan than that, for anything this project has faced since the mess hall."

Ilse thought of Emre Solak, standing in the training bay corridor asking her how to carry two fears at once without either one swallowing the other, and understood that she had not actually answered him, not really, only told him the truth about not having a cleaner answer to give, the same truth she seemed to keep handing to everyone who asked her something that mattered, over and over, in every register this project had required of her since the day she wrote a number down and refused to delete it.

Below them, the Anvil's lights held their pattern the way they always had, indifferent to the sentence sitting in a settlement notice now traveling outward at the speed of every relay station between here and the edge of what anyone had ever charted, and Ilse understood, watching them, that whatever came next was no longer a question this project could answer by itself, on its own schedule, in its own time. Something else was already deciding that, somewhere past the edge of every chart they had, and all any of them could do, for now, was keep building toward the one promise still entirely theirs to keep: that whatever arrived to test it, they would meet it having told each other, and everyone standing behind them, the truth.
