THE ATLAS CONVERGENCE
Chapter One — draft (test one)


PREVENTABLE


The coffee, Ilse noticed, had grown a skin. She had let it sit there cold for hours and it had gone and done that, of all things, and she had watched it happen from a foot away without a single thought.

The office was dark. She had not turned on more than the desk lamp, and now the dark had the texture it got late at night. Close. Leaning on the glass. A shiver went through her. She had always hated the dark. Forty years old and still spooked by an unlit room, and she had never once talked herself out of it.

"What am I doing wrong," she said. Out loud. To nobody.

She had stopped noticing when she started doing that. Talking to the empty room. It happened when a problem got big enough, and this one had been big since the spring, and she was no nearer solving it tonight than she had been in March.

"There has to be a way." She rubbed her face. "There has to be."

That afternoon Devlin had told her no again, kindly, hands flat on the table the way he always did it. The Board would not spend eleven million dollars to find out whether a thing worked when for nine they could build it and see. That had been the objection in June. It had been the objection in September. It was a good objection. She had answered it the only way she knew, which was straight. The nine buys you one answer about one build. My eleven buys you the answer about every build like it, for as long as the model holds. Devlin had nodded. It was true. The Board passed anyway. True and funded were different rooms.

She had a history with that gap. She had told a project lead at Corran Dynamics that a weld spec would not survive its duty cycle. She had been right. That was the quiet end of her at Corran. She had told a review board, gently, in closed session, that their trial numbers did not say what they wanted them to say, and they thanked her for her candor and did not renew her. It went that way in every room. She said the sentence nobody wanted said, it was always true, it always cost her, and she had never learned to keep her mouth shut, because the other kind of person watched the weld fail and said nothing, and she could not make herself be that.

The reason she was in this building at all was the Coleford gates.

She still opened the report some days. The one written afterward. Scour-channel failure, a mode any decent hydrology model would have flagged in an afternoon. Nobody ran the model. It would have cost under two hundred thousand, and there was no line anywhere for finding out what you did not yet have a reason to fear. Thirty-one people were on the low road when the gates let go. She had read the thirty-one names once, on purpose, and never again.

And it kept not working. Not the model. The pitch. Four hundred and ten runs, all of them clean, all of them saying yes, and she still could not sell it, because every time she walked into that room she set her number down next to the wrong one. She said predict it, and she pointed at build it and see. And every time, somebody at the table ran the arithmetic she had handed them and found a cheaper way to be wrong. Then she went back to her desk and queued another run, like the runs were the thing in her way.

She was staring at four hundred and ten now, not really seeing it, and the thought came in from the side, the way the real ones did. Not an answer. A door she had been leaning on for months, and now there was a handle in it.

She had been asking whether predicting the failure was cheaper than the failure.

That was never the weight on the other side. One bad build was never the weight on the other side. The weight on the other side was Coleford, and the weld at Corran, and every span and hull and dosing schedule and flight envelope that a known answer would have held together. All of them. The ones behind her and the ones still coming. Set against a number like that, her eleven million was not a cost. It was rounding. It had always been rounding. She had been standing in that room apologizing for the price of a smoke detector and pointing at the fire.

Something opened up under her ribs, wide and warm, the way a house feels when every light in it comes on at once.

Two years. She had been in this building two years, asking a question with the wrong number under it, and the right one had been sitting there the whole time, plain as a curb. She wanted to laugh. She also wanted to put her head down on the desk and leave it there a while. Why had she not seen it. Costing things was the one thing she was actually good at, and she had been carrying the wrong total in her own head since the spring.

She picked up the cold cup out of habit, looked at the skin on it, set it back down.

Monday she would go in and she would not lead with the model. She would lead with the ledger. She would put down the number nobody costs, because nobody keeps a column for it, and she would say that every dollar not spent on knowing gets borrowed against somebody who is not in the room yet, and the note comes due every time, paid in full, in people. She would watch Devlin's hands go flat on the table, and she would keep talking.

It was either the most arrogant thing anyone had said in that room or the only honest one. She had lost the ability to tell those apart somewhere around the two-hundredth run. She was going to say it anyway. She always did. It had cost her every job she had ever almost kept.

She knew how to pitch it now. Everything after this was arithmetic.
