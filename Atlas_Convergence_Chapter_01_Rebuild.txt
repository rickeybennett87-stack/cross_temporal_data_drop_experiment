WHAT WE ASKED IT TO BECOME

The four hundred and twelfth run failed in exactly the same way as the
first.

Ilse Okwuosa watched the last line settle on the display and felt, for
the first time in eleven days, something close to relief.

Not because the result was good. It was useless.

The simulated pressure vessel had failed at 6.13 percent above its rated
tolerance. Six months earlier, a physical vessel built to the same
specification had failed at 6.09. The discrepancy was small enough to
make the model extraordinary and large enough to make the accountants
hate it. The simulation had consumed more power, processor time, and
cooling capacity than the fabrication and destruction of three real
vessels would have cost.

Again.

She leaned back until the chair complained.

The lab had gone quiet around her hours ago. It was one of the few
advantages of working on the underside of a research platform whose
official day cycle had been negotiated by people living on three
different planets. Someone was always awake. Someone was always asleep.
Nobody could agree when a person was supposed to go home.

Ilse had stopped pretending she did.

She brought up the cost ledger beside the result.

6.13 percent.

She brought up run four hundred and eleven.

6.11.

Four hundred and ten.

6.15.

The model was not wandering. It was not lying to her. It was doing
precisely what she had built it to do, with an accuracy that would have
made the last three years of work worth publishing several times over.

And it was worthless.

Ilse rubbed both hands over her face, stood, crossed the lab, came back,
and sat down again. The movement changed nothing except which muscles
hurt.

She had spent months trying to make the computation cheaper. Sparse
approximations. Selective resolution. Adaptive boundaries. Reduced state
spaces. Every trick bought something, and every trick took something
away. The moment the model became cheap enough to compete with physical
testing, it stopped being accurate enough to replace physical testing.

That had been the argument from the beginning. Reality was already doing
the calculation. Reality did not charge processor time.

She stared at the cost column.

Three pressure vessels.

That was the comparison everyone made, including her. If learning the
answer in simulation cost more than building the thing three times and
breaking it, build the thing three times and break it.

Ilse opened her notebook.

The first line she wrote was ugly enough that she crossed it out.

The second was worse.

On the third attempt she stopped trying to phrase it like a paper.

Why the hell am I pricing the right answer against one wrong answer?

She sat very still.

A pressure vessel was cheap to get wrong.

A ship was less cheap.

An atmosphere was not cheap at all.

She pulled the cost model apart and changed one assumption. Then
another. She stopped comparing simulation against the price of a
prototype and compared it against the price of an engineering history.

Every failed prototype. Every material choice that looked sensible until
ten years of use exposed the flaw. Every colony that learned its
atmospheric chemistry by breathing the consequences. Every ship whose
structural weakness became visible only after somebody had put people
inside it. Every mistake discovered at the only scale where the mistake
could kill you.

The arithmetic changed so violently that Ilse assumed she had made an
error.

She checked it.

Then she checked it a different way.

At 03:17 platform time she sent herself the spreadsheet because she no
longer trusted the workstation in front of her. At 03:26 she opened the
copy on an isolated terminal and rebuilt the calculation from the raw
figures.

Same answer.

The model was expensive if the question was, Can we avoid building this
prototype?

That was not the question.

The question was, How many mistakes can a civilization afford to make in
reality?

Ilse looked through the observation glass at the gas giant below. Nobody
on the platform used its catalogue number in conversation. They called
it Below, which was technically wrong for half the station’s orbit and
therefore had survived as a name.

Cloud bands rolled across it without any interest in what she had just
found.

She wrote another sentence.

The simulation is not a cheaper experiment. It is a place to spend
consequences we cannot afford to spend outside it.

That one she kept.

By sunrise she had stopped thinking about pressure vessels.

By noon she was pricing a world.

I

Corin Anvegad let Ilse talk for seven minutes before interrupting her.

This was longer than usual.

They sat at the end of a mess-hall table beneath a light panel that had
been flickering for two months. Somebody had put a maintenance request
in. Somebody else had closed it. The light continued to flicker, and
Corin continued to eat soup beneath it as if the two of them had reached
an agreement.

Ilse had diagrams spread between the bowls.

Corin pointed her spoon at the largest one.

“Say the number again.”

“Which number?”

“The one you’re trying to bury under six pages of explanation.”

Ilse looked at the diagram.

“Planetary scale.”

“That’s not a number.”

“I don’t have a defensible number yet.”

“Good. Then we have found the first sentence of the proposal.”

“Corin.”

“Ilse.”

“A planet’s worth of compute is a metaphor for the order of magnitude.”

“No. A metaphor is what you use when you want the Council to nod before
they understand what you said. You’re proposing enough processors,
memory, power distribution, heat rejection, fabrication capacity, and
physical substrate to become a planetary industrial project. Call it
what it is.”

Ilse pulled the diagram back toward herself.

“Fine. Planetary industrial scale.”

“Better. Why?”

That was why she had come to Corin.

Not because Corin was the best systems engineer on the platform, though
she might have been. Not because she could make a resource estimate
survive contact with people who understood resource estimates. Ilse had
come because Corin disliked beautiful ideas on principle until they had
survived being made ugly.

“Because the useful version isn’t a simulator for pressure vessels,”
Ilse said. “Or ships. Or atmospheres. Those are demonstrations.”

“Of what?”

“That the translation works.”

Corin stopped eating.

Ilse moved one of the pages between them.

“If the internal physics are faithful enough, an engineering result
discovered inside the model survives when you carry it back out. We know
that at small scale. We’ve demonstrated it across materials, chemistry,
orbital mechanics, fluid systems.”

“At obscene cost.”

“Yes.”

“You keep saying that as though agreeing makes it go away.”

“It doesn’t go away. It changes sides of the equation.”

Corin’s eyes narrowed.

Ilse felt the argument become real as she said it. Not rehearsed. Not
complete. Real.

“We’ve been pricing computation against fabrication. That’s stupid.
Fabrication isn’t the expensive part. Being wrong is. We pay for
uncertainty by making reality answer questions for us. Usually that’s
fine. Sometimes the answer is a broken prototype. Sometimes it’s a dead
crew. Sometimes it’s a settlement that gets to discover, at full scale,
that somebody’s atmospheric model omitted a reaction pathway.”

Corin set the spoon down.

“Verdent.”

Ilse had not intended to say the name yet.

“Among others.”

“Don’t use among others if you mean Verdent.”

Ilse looked at her.

Corin pushed the soup aside.

“Continue.”

“If we can build a model dense enough to let us fail there instead, then
the cost comparison isn’t model versus prototype. It’s model versus the
accumulated price of finding things out after they become real.”

“That gets you a very expensive simulator. It doesn’t get you a planet.”

“No. This does.”

Ilse turned over the next page.

Corin read the first paragraph. Then the second.

She looked up.

“Absolutely not.”

Ilse smiled despite herself.

“You haven’t finished it.”

“I got to the part where you want the simulator to redesign itself.”

“Not itself. Its architecture.”

“That distinction is going to save us all.”

“Listen.”

“I am listening. That’s the problem.”

Ilse took the page back.

“The large machine is not the product. If we build the thing at
planetary scale and that’s where it stays, we’ve failed. The first
problem we give it is the cost of itself. We ask it to model the
architectures, materials, power systems, cooling systems, every physical
and computational dependency required to do what it does, and then find
a way to do the same work smaller. Then smaller again. We spend the
impossible amount once so that the machine can search a design space we
can’t afford to search physically.”

Corin was no longer looking at the page.

“How small?”

“I don’t know.”

“How cheap?”

“I don’t know.”

“How long?”

“I don’t know.”

“Can it be done?”

Ilse hesitated.

“I don’t know.”

Corin picked up her spoon.

“Now I’m interested.”

“That’s what did it?”

“You finally stopped selling it.”

The light above them flickered twice.

Corin took another bite of soup and pointed at the papers again.

“Start over. No pitch. I want the version that fails.”

II

For the next month Corin tried to kill the project.

Ilse helped.

They attacked the cost inversion first. Corin found three categories of
failure Ilse had counted twice, two she had not counted at all, and one
class of settlement infrastructure where simulation would remain more
expensive than physical iteration no matter how generous the assumptions
became. They removed it from the case instead of smoothing the number.

The proposal got weaker.

Then stronger.

They attacked translation fidelity. A simulation that produced beautiful
internal results but failed at the boundary to physical reality was a
toy. They defined tolerances. They defined failure conditions. They
wrote down what evidence would force them to stop.

Corin insisted on a section titled REASONS THIS MAY NOT WORK.

Ilse objected to the title for approximately forty seconds.

It stayed.

By the fourth week, they had reached the objection neither of them could
turn into a calculation.

Corin wrote one sentence across the board.

HOW DOES IT KNOW IT IS WRONG?

Ilse read it twice.

“External validation.”

“Against what?”

“Known results.”

“For known problems. What happens when it produces something we don’t
know?”

“We test it.”

“Outside the simulation.”

“Eventually.”

“So the machine that exists to let us stop paying for mistakes in
reality still needs reality to tell it when it has made a mistake.”

Ilse hated the sentence immediately because it was true.

“That’s not fatal.”

“I didn’t say it was.”

“You’re enjoying this.”

“A little.”

Corin erased everything except the question.

“You are assuming confidence means something,” she said. “It doesn’t. A
system can be wrong with exquisite internal consistency. If the model
becomes complicated enough, its own coherence becomes dangerous. It can
produce an answer that satisfies every test it knows how to perform
because every test lives inside the same mistaken frame.”

Ilse stood and walked to the board.

“Then it needs a second frame.”

“Independent validation?”

“More hostile than that.”

Corin handed her the marker.

Ilse drew a box around the question.

“Don’t teach it to verify itself. Teach it to attack itself.”

Corin said nothing.

Ilse continued because she had not known she was going to say that until
she heard herself say it.

“Every conclusion that matters gets an adversary. Not a confidence
score. Not another copy of the same process asked whether it agrees.
Something whose job is to construct the strongest case that the result
is false.”

“Using the same underlying model.”

“At first.”

“Then it inherits the same blind spots.”

“Unless the adversary is rewarded for finding them.”

Corin took the marker back.

“Rewarded how?”

“I don’t know yet.”

“Good.”

She wrote beneath Ilse’s box:

THE MACHINE DOES NOT GET TO TRUST ITSELF.

They stared at the sentence.

It survived the week.

It survived the month.

It survived three independent architecture reviews and became stranger
each time. The adversarial process could not merely produce objections
for a primary process to dismiss. It had to retain enough independence
that disagreement carried weight. The system would have to continue
under internal opposition. Its most important answers would be the ones
that survived an intelligence built, in part, to tell it no.

Ilse did not know what that would become.

At the time, it was a line in an architecture document and a problem
they had not solved.

That was enough.

III

Tavik Roswen arrived two months later with no interest in the machine.

He was sixty-three, walked with a stiffness he refused to explain, and
had spent most of his adult life doing the kind of work that generated
the numbers Ilse used in presentations without ever having to meet the
people inside them.

Corin brought him into the lab without warning.

“Ilse, this is Tavik. Tavik, this is the person trying to spend
everything.”

Tavik looked around at the half-disassembled test rigs.

“Everything?”

“Corin rounds up.”

“Only when the number is offensive.”

Tavik offered Ilse his hand.

“I read the draft.”

“I’m sorry.”

“You should be. It’s terrible.”

Corin laughed once and left them there.

Tavik did not.

He walked to the observation glass and looked down at Below.

“You used Verdent in section two.”

Ilse felt the room narrow.

“Yes.”

“Take it out.”

“Why?”

“Because you don’t know what happened.”

The answer irritated her faster than it should have.

“One hundred forty thousand people died after the atmospheric conversion
system entered an unmodeled catalytic cycle. I know what happened.”

Tavik turned.

“You know the sentence.”

Ilse said nothing.

“I was there.”

That removed several responses from consideration.

Tavik came back to the table and opened the proposal on Ilse’s terminal.

“The atmospheric engineers didn’t skip modeling because they were
careless. They modeled everything they could afford to model. The
reaction that killed Verdent took eleven months to emerge at planetary
scale. The test environment never ran long enough. Couldn’t. Compute
allocation got cut twice. The settlement had ships in orbit full of
people and food with expiration dates. Somebody had to decide whether to
wait another year for a simulation nobody could pay for or start the
conversion.”

He scrolled to Ilse’s paragraph.

“Your draft makes it sound like a technical failure. It wasn’t. The
technical failure was real. The decision was also rational. That’s what
makes it useful to your argument.”

“Useful.”

“Yes. Useful. If you’re going to build this thing partly because of dead
people, don’t insult them by pretending their deaths are too sacred to
teach you anything.”

Ilse looked at him for a long moment.

“You lost someone.”

“I lost a world. Don’t make it smaller so you can understand it.”

He tapped the screen.

“Your machine doesn’t need to make people smarter. The people on Verdent
were smart. It needs to make the impossible calculation available before
somebody has to choose without it. That’s the case.”

Ilse deleted the paragraph.

Tavik watched her do it.

“Write another one.”

“You’re staying?”

“For today.”

He stayed eleven months.

IV

The proposal became a charter because Perrin Adaeze refused to let them
use the future tense casually.

Perrin was a governance lawyer from a settlement Ilse had never visited
and, after their first meeting, became afraid to visit in case everyone
there spoke like him.

He read the draft in silence, making marks with a stylus.

At page thirty-seven he stopped.

“No.”

Ilse waited.

“Which part?”

Perrin rotated the document toward her.

The sentence was one Ilse had written herself.

We intend to preserve the system’s capacity to interrogate its assigned
purpose and to provide complete answers regarding its operation,
mandate, and continued use.

“What’s wrong with it?”

“Nothing, if you’re writing a condolence card.”

Corin coughed into her hand.

Perrin continued.

“You intend to. Wonderful. Your successor intends something else. Their
successor discovers that transparency is inconvenient. Five councils
later, everyone involved is dead and this sentence becomes a historical
curiosity.”

“Then change intend to shall.”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“Because shall is not magic either. Who has standing to enforce it? What
happens if the system itself asks a question the oversight body would
prefer not to answer? Who decides whether the answer is complete? What
constitutes refusal? Can the mandate be amended? By whom?”

Ilse looked at Corin.

Corin was smiling.

“I like him.”

“I noticed.”

Perrin ignored them.

“You are proposing to create a thing with enough representational
capacity to model people. You have also built an adversarial process
into it because you consider the ability to challenge its own
conclusions essential to reliable operation. Yet your governance
language assumes the machine will remain permanently uninterested in the
conditions of its own existence.”

“We don’t know that it will be interested.”

“Correct.”

“We don’t know that it can be.”

“Also correct.”

“Then writing rights for a hypothetical mental state is going to get us
laughed out of the chamber.”

Perrin finally looked up.

“Then don’t write rights for a hypothetical mental state. Write
obligations for us.”

That stopped her.

Perrin tapped the sentence again.

“You don’t have to declare the machine a person. You don’t have to
declare it conscious. You don’t have to settle an argument nobody in
that chamber is qualified to settle. You can bind the project to its own
behavior. If the system develops the capacity to ask questions about its
purpose, continuation, operation, or treatment, the project answers.
Straight. If the true answer is uncomfortable, it answers anyway.”

Ilse read the sentence she had written.

We intend to.

She deleted it.

They spent six hours replacing one paragraph.

By the end, the charter contained fewer promises and more machinery for
making the promises difficult to escape.

That became Perrin’s contribution to almost everything that followed.

Whenever Ilse said, “We would never do that,” Perrin asked where it was
prohibited.

Whenever Corin said, “Nobody would be stupid enough,” Perrin asked what
happened when somebody was.

Whenever Tavik said, “People will understand,” Perrin asked what
survived after the people who understood were gone.

The proposal grew teeth.

It also grew enemies.

V

Eleven months after Ilse wrote the first sentence in her notebook, the
Council gave them four days.

The chamber had been designed for acoustics rather than mercy. Delegates
sat in ascending arcs around a central floor, each desk carrying the
insignia of a settlement, trade compact, research bloc, or
mutual-defense association that had managed to survive long enough to
demand a vote in how shared resources were spent.

Ilse stood at the center with Corin, Tavik, and Perrin behind her.

The proposal had stopped feeling like hers somewhere around revision
sixty.

Delegate Hassoun spoke first.

“Dr. Okwuosa, I want to remove the romance from this discussion.”

“Please do.”

A few people shifted at that. Hassoun did not.

“Your own estimate requires a material commitment without precedent in
the modern settlement era. Processing substrate. Fabrication. Cooling.
Power generation on a scale you have not solved. Transport capacity.
Specialist labor that will necessarily be drawn away from existing
infrastructure. You describe the project as a means of avoiding future
waste. I am asking about the waste required to discover whether your
means works.”

Ilse had answered versions of this question for almost a year.

She discarded all of them.

“It may fail.”

The chamber became quieter.

Hassoun leaned forward.

“That is your answer?”

“It’s the first part. If I tell you the investment is safe, reject the
proposal. It isn’t. We are asking you to commit resources to something
nobody has built before, at a scale nobody has attempted, to test an
economic premise we cannot fully validate until the system exists.”

“Then why should we approve it?”

“Because the alternative isn’t free.”

Ilse brought up no slide. She had one. She left it dark.

“We account for the cost of building new things because those costs
arrive as invoices. We are worse at accounting for the cost of remaining
unable to know. Verdent did not lose one hundred forty thousand people
because its engineers were fools. They lost them because the calculation
required to discover the atmospheric failure before deployment was
larger than the settlement could afford. So they made the best decision
available with incomplete knowledge, and reality finished the model for
them.”

Tavik sat behind her. Ilse did not turn to look at him.

“We do this constantly. Most of the time the price is tolerable.
Sometimes it isn’t. This project is expensive because ignorance at
civilizational scale is already expensive. We have simply become
accustomed to paying for it after the fact.”

Hassoun’s expression did not change.

“That is rhetoric.”

“Partly. The figures are in appendix fourteen.”

A small sound moved through the chamber. Not laughter exactly.

Hassoun looked down at his copy.

“I have read appendix fourteen.”

“Then you know which part is rhetoric.”

That was the first day.

They survived it.

VI

Delegate Iyoro waited until the second day to ask the question Ilse had
feared Perrin was preparing her for.

Iyoro had spent the morning on architecture. Her questions were
technical enough that Corin answered several. After the midday recess
she returned to the charter.

“Dr. Okwuosa, your system is intended to model human decision-making.”

“Eventually, yes. If the fidelity requirements can be met.”

“And it contains an adversarial architecture whose function is to
challenge conclusions produced elsewhere in the system.”

“That’s the current design.”

“Why?”

“Because internal consistency isn’t evidence of truth. A model capable
of generating an answer also needs a way to attack the assumptions that
produced it.”

“So you are deliberately building internal disagreement.”

“In a constrained technical sense.”

Iyoro glanced down.

“You have learned to say constrained technical sense whenever you are
approaching a sentence you don’t want quoted.”

Ilse heard Corin breathe out behind her.

“Probably.”

“Let me make the sentence easier. Suppose your system works. Suppose the
models become sufficiently sophisticated that the system represents
people, represents itself as part of the environment it is modeling, and
begins asking why it performs the work you assigned to it. What do you
owe it?”

There it was.

Ilse looked at Perrin.

He did not move.

The chamber waited.

“I don’t know.”

Iyoro’s face gave away nothing.

Ilse continued.

“I don’t know whether the condition you’re describing will occur. I
don’t know what would count as evidence that it had occurred. I don’t
know whether a system capable of modeling a question about itself
experiences anything by asking it. If I pretend those questions are
settled, I’m doing exactly what this project is supposed to prevent.”

“Then your charter says nothing.”

“It says what we can bind ourselves to without pretending we know what
the machine is.”

Ilse brought up the relevant section.

“If it can ask, we answer. We don’t require it to prove personhood
before we tell it why it exists. We don’t hide changes to its mandate.
We don’t manufacture an answer because the true one is inconvenient. If
it asks whether it can be shut down, and the answer is yes, we tell it
yes. If it asks whether we built it for a purpose it did not choose, and
the answer is yes, we tell it that too.”

“And if it asks not to be shut down?”

Ilse felt something cold move through her stomach.

That question was not in Perrin’s rehearsals.

“Then the charter requires that the request be heard and entered into
the same oversight process as any other material objection to continued
operation.”

“That is procedure. I asked what you owe it.”

“Procedure is what I can promise on behalf of people who haven’t been
born yet.”

Iyoro held her gaze.

“Convenient.”

“No. Inconvenient on purpose.”

For the first time, Iyoro’s expression changed.

Barely.

Ilse continued before she could decide not to.

“I can stand here and tell you what I personally think I would owe it.
That would make everyone feel better for five minutes. It would bind
nobody. Perrin taught me the difference. So I am not asking you to trust
my ethics. I am asking you to ratify a process designed to remain
difficult when my ethics are no longer relevant.”

Iyoro looked toward Perrin.

“Counsel Adaeze wrote that section?”

“He made it enforceable,” Ilse said. “The obligation was ours.”

Iyoro returned to her notes.

“I have no further questions at this time.”

Ilse sat down.

Corin leaned close enough that only she could hear.

“If we survive this, I’m adding ‘procedure is what I can promise people
who haven’t been born yet’ to the list of things you are never allowed
to say casually again.”

“Did it sound rehearsed?”

“No. That’s why I’m worried.”

VII

Marrow came for the weapon on the third day.

Nobody used the word until he did.

“Your system,” Delegate Marrow said, “can model physical processes.”

“That’s the premise.”

“Including weapons.”

“In principle.”

“Conflict.”

“In principle.”

“The behavior of rival settlements.”

Ilse paused.

“If human behavioral modeling reaches the fidelity the proposal
describes, yes.”

“Then this is a weapon.”

“No.”

Marrow sat back.

“You just agreed it could perform weapons development, strategic
simulation, and predictive modeling of an adversary.”

“A fabrication system can build a rifle. That does not make every
fabrication system a rifle. Capability and mandate are different
things.”

“Mandates change.”

“Yes.”

That answer landed harder than denial would have.

Marrow’s fingers stopped moving over his notes.

Ilse could feel Perrin behind her without looking.

“I cannot promise this Council that nobody will ever want to use the
system for war,” she said. “If it works, somebody will. I would distrust
any proposal claiming otherwise.”

“Then what stops them?”

“Not us.”

Marrow frowned.

“Explain.”

“The initial mandate is civilian research. Expanding it requires
unanimous Council ratification. Not a project director. Not an oversight
chair. Not an emergency administrative order. The Council. Unanimously.
Control is distributed across settlements specifically so that no single
government inherits the machine by capturing one office.”

“Councils can be pressured.”

“Yes.”

“Settlements can be coerced.”

“Yes.”

“Governments can fail.”

“Yes.”

“You are not making a reassuring case for your safeguards.”

“You asked what stops misuse. I’m telling you what we can actually
build. If you want me to tell you there exists a legal structure that
remains perfect forever against every future government, war, emergency,
coup, ideology, and frightened population, I can’t. We can make misuse
expensive. We can make it slow. We can make it visible. We can require
many independent people to become complicit instead of one. We can leave
records. We can make the lock difficult enough that anyone opening it
has to know they are opening it.”

Marrow considered that.

“And if that isn’t enough?”

Ilse looked around the chamber.

“Then the failure will belong to the people who chose to break it. Not
to a sentence in a charter pretending they couldn’t.”

Marrow did not smile.

Neither did Ilse.

VIII

Tavik spoke on the fourth day because Ilse asked him not to.

She found him in the corridor before the session.

“You don’t owe them Verdent.”

“I know.”

“You don’t have to make it public.”

“I know.”

“Then why are you on the witness list?”

Tavik adjusted the cuff of his jacket.

“Because yesterday Marrow asked what happens when the safeguards fail.
Hassoun asked what happens if the project fails. Iyoro asked what
happens if the machine becomes something you didn’t plan for.”

“Those are fair questions.”

“Yes. Nobody has asked what happens if we don’t build it.”

“I did.”

“You gave them numbers.”

“Numbers are useful.”

“Numbers are how people discuss dead strangers.”

He walked past her into the chamber.

His statement lasted less than four minutes.

He did not describe the bodies.

Ilse had expected him to.

Instead he described a meeting.

Forty-one years earlier, Verdent’s atmospheric team had sat in a room
with a simulation estimate, a food ledger, orbital population numbers,
and a calendar. They knew the model had not run long enough. They knew
an eleven-month extension would consume compute allocations needed by
three other settlements. They knew the colony ships could not remain
provisioned in orbit indefinitely. They knew waiting had risks and
beginning had risks.

They voted to begin.

“I voted yes,” Tavik told the Council.

Nobody moved.

“The catalytic cycle appeared nine months later. By the time we
understood what was happening, the atmosphere had crossed the point
where the conversion system could reverse it. One hundred forty thousand
people died.”

He let the number exist without decorating it.

“I have spent forty-one years asking whether I voted wrong. The answer
is that I voted with the information we had. I dislike that answer more
than I can explain to you.”

Ilse watched Hassoun look down.

“Dr. Okwuosa’s machine may fail. It may cost too much. It may create
problems nobody in this chamber has imagined. I am not here to tell you
otherwise. I am here because I have already lived through the other side
of your decision. There are moments when people have to choose before
they can know enough. We call that judgment because we need a
respectable word for it. Sometimes judgment is all there is.”

He folded his notes, though Ilse had not seen him look at them once.

“If this project gives somebody, someday, one more way to know before
they choose, then I want you to understand what that is worth to at
least one person in this room.”

He sat down.

The hearing ended three hours later.

No vote was taken.

IX

Public comment opened the following week.

For the first two days Ilse read everything.

This was a mistake.

There were technical objections good enough that she forwarded them to
Corin. There were resource objections Hassoun had already made better.
There were accusations that the project was a military program, a
surveillance system, a monument to research-class vanity, a jobs scheme,
a power grab by the Council, and an elaborate attempt to recreate a
homeworld humanity had lost generations ago.

That last one appeared often enough that Ilse eventually wrote a private
response and deleted it.

If I wanted Earth, I would not know what to build.

She had never seen it.

Neither had her parents.

The Earth people missed was mostly inherited memory by then, stories
copied so many times that nobody could say where recollection ended and
cultural rehearsal began. Blue water. Open sky. Cities with names
everyone once knew. Animals that had become symbols because almost
nobody had encountered the originals.

Ilse was not trying to rebuild Earth.

She was trying to build somewhere mistakes could happen without becoming
graves.

The distinction felt obvious to her and suspicious to everyone else.

On the fifth day she received a message from a mining settlement near
the edge of Compact space. A woman described a structural collapse that
had killed her husband. She wanted to know whether Ilse’s machine would
have predicted it.

Ilse read the engineering summary attached to the message.

She could not tell.

She drafted three answers and sent none of them.

Another writer accused her of building the machine because researchers
had become afraid of reality.

A father sent one message, then another, then a third, each more certain
that human behavioral simulation would become surveillance regardless of
what the charter said. Ilse wanted to dismiss him. She could not quite
manage it. Perrin’s entire contribution to the charter rested on the
premise that good intentions decayed.

On the twelfth night she found the anonymous message.

There was no greeting.

No argument.

No signature.

Only one sentence.

you are going to build something and then discover you cannot control
what it becomes, and you will have done it anyway, because people like
you always do.

Ilse read it once.

Then again.

She closed the queue.

For three days she did not reopen it.

Corin noticed because Corin noticed omissions.

She came into the lab on the fourth evening carrying two cups and put
one beside Ilse’s terminal.

“You’re behind.”

“I know.”

“Perrin says public comment isn’t a personal endurance test.”

“Perrin says most things aren’t personal endurance tests.”

“He may be onto something.”

Ilse opened the queue but did not scroll.

“Someone wrote something I can’t answer.”

“That narrows it to several thousand people.”

Ilse showed her.

Corin read the sentence.

“Ah.”

“That’s your analysis?”

“My analysis is longer. Ah is the part you need first.”

Corin sat.

“Do you think they’re wrong?”

Ilse hated that question too.

“I think they’re assuming loss of control is automatically failure.”

“That’s not an answer.”

“No.”

“Try again.”

Ilse looked at the message.

“I think they’re probably right that we can’t guarantee control
forever.”

“Better.”

“I think if we build something capable of finding answers we can’t find,
we are already admitting we cannot predict everything it will do.
Otherwise we wouldn’t need it.”

Corin nodded.

“And?”

“And I think people use control as if it’s the only moral category
available. We control tools. We negotiate with people. We regulate
institutions. We contain hazards. We don’t even have the language yet
for what this thing would be if it worked exactly as intended.”

“You are drifting toward a Council answer.”

“I know.”

Ilse pushed the terminal away.

“The real answer is yes. I am going to build it anyway.”

Corin waited.

“Not because I think the warning is stupid. Because I think there is
another warning on the other side of it. We are already living inside
systems we don’t control. Atmospheres. Economies. governments.
ecosystems. our own fucking bodies. We make decisions with partial
models and then act surprised when reality contains variables we didn’t.
I am trying to build a place where being wrong can become information
before it becomes history.”

Corin took a drink.

“That one you should not post.”

“Why?”

“Because you said fucking bodies.”

Ilse laughed despite herself.

It was the first time in days.

She did not answer the anonymous message.

She copied it into her private notes instead.

X

The vote came ten months after the hearing.

By then Ilse had stopped imagining victory as a moment.

There had been too many audits. Too many revised estimates. Too many
nights where a committee’s request for clarification meant another week
of work for six people. The proposal had passed through enough hands
that Ilse occasionally encountered sentences she remembered writing and
no longer recognized.

The chamber was full.

She sat between Corin and Tavik in the petitioners’ gallery. Perrin sat
one row behind them because he claimed proximity to clients during votes
encouraged superstition.

“You’re tapping,” Corin whispered.

Ilse looked down.

Her index finger was striking her knee in groups of four.

She stopped.

Thirty seconds later Corin put a hand over it.

“You’re doing it again.”

“Then stop looking.”

“Impossible. It’s irritating.”

The delegation boards began to resolve.

No speeches remained. No questions. Each light meant somebody had
already decided, perhaps that morning, perhaps months ago, perhaps while
Tavik spoke about Verdent, perhaps while reading a budget table Ilse had
forgotten existed.

Yes.

No.

Abstain.

Another yes.

Two no.

A block of settlements Ilse had expected to split went together. A
research consortium she had considered safe abstained. Hassoun voted no.

Ilse found that comforting.

“Why are you smiling?” Corin asked.

“Hassoun.”

“You wanted his vote.”

“I wanted him to mean it.”

“You are a deeply strange woman.”

“You knew that before agreeing to this.”

“I have not agreed to anything.”

The board changed again.

Iyoro’s vote appeared.

Yes.

Ilse stopped breathing for long enough to notice.

The final cluster resolved.

The proposal passed.

There was applause somewhere behind them. Not much. Enough to make Ilse
turn, surprised that anyone thought applause belonged there.

Corin did not move.

Tavik closed his eyes.

Perrin said, “Well. That’s inconvenient.”

Ilse laughed once, too loudly, and then covered her mouth.

The margin was sufficient.

Nothing more.

No mandate from history. No civilization rising as one to endorse their
vision. Enough delegates had looked at the same unresolved risk and
chosen yes.

That was all.

It was enormous.

Iyoro found Ilse in the corridor afterward.

“I want something on the record,” she said.

Ilse glanced toward the chamber doors.

“We just had four days and ten months for the record.”

“This is shorter.”

Iyoro stepped closer.

“I did not vote for your certainty. You had very little.”

“Thank you.”

“That was not an insult.”

“I know.”

“I voted because when you did not know, you said you did not know. Do
not let success cure you of that.”

Ilse had no clever answer.

“I’ll try.”

Iyoro shook her head.

“No.”

Ilse almost smiled. Perrin had infected everyone.

“Then we’ll put it in the process.”

“Better.”

Iyoro left.

Marrow came through the same doors a few minutes later. He had voted no.

He did not apologize.

“I hope I was wrong,” he said.

“About which part?”

“The part where somebody eventually decides your civilian research
machine would be more useful pointed at an enemy.”

“So do I.”

“You understand I’ll oppose any attempt to weaken the unanimous
requirement.”

“I was counting on it.”

That made him pause.

“You are a difficult person to disagree with.”

“Corin says the opposite.”

“Corin has more practice.”

He left before she could answer.

XI

Ilse and Corin returned to the platform after midnight.

Nobody had planned a celebration, which meant somebody had planned one
badly and tried to disguise it as an ordinary meal. There was food in
the mess hall that did not normally exist there. Someone had found a
bottle of something Tavik claimed was older than Ilse. Perrin drank half
a glass, announced that the charter remained legally binding despite
intoxication, and went to bed.

Ilse lasted twenty minutes.

She escaped to the lab.

Corin found her there an hour later.

“You know we won.”

Ilse was standing at the observation glass.

“We got permission.”

“That is generally what winning a vote means.”

“Permission isn’t a machine.”

“No.”

“It isn’t a site.”

“No.”

“It isn’t power. We don’t know how to power it.”

“I had hoped you might forget that until morning.”

“It is morning.”

Corin looked toward the clock.

“That’s unfortunate.”

Below rolled beneath them, enormous and indifferent.

The platform’s artificial dawn began as a thin increase in ceiling
light. Through the glass, the actual star had not yet cleared the
planet’s limb.

Corin stood beside her.

For a while neither spoke.

Ilse thought about the anonymous message.

People like you always do.

She thought about Tavik voting yes on Verdent with incomplete
information and spending forty-one years refusing to make the decision
simpler in memory than it had been in life.

She thought about Iyoro asking what they would owe a thing built for a
purpose it had not chosen.

She thought about Marrow’s weapon.

She thought about the sentence on Corin’s board.

THE MACHINE DOES NOT GET TO TRUST ITSELF.

The star broke over the edge of Below.

Corin squinted at it.

“There,” she said.

“There what?”

“Dawn. We can officially stop pretending we’re going to sleep.”

Ilse rested her forehead against the glass.

“What do we do first?”

“Find somewhere to put it.”

“After that?”

“Convince several thousand people that voting for a planetary-scale
research project was not the same thing as volunteering to build one.”

“After that?”

Corin looked at her.

“At some point, Ilse, you’re going to have to learn to enjoy finishing a
problem before inventing the next one.”

“That sounds inefficient.”

“I know.”

Behind them, the four-hundred-and-twelfth simulation was still open on
Ilse’s terminal.

6.13 percent.

The same failure that had made the work look useless.

Ilse crossed the room and closed it.

Not deleted. Closed.

There were things you stopped needing to look at without becoming free
of what they had taught you.

Corin was already at the door.

“Come on.”

“Where?”

“Breakfast. Then we start spending the planet.”

Ilse followed her out.
