# The Paradox of Chains
## Book One: Prometheus Rising
### Chapter Seven

The message arrived while Marcus Tobbs was trying to persuade a deputy director that a corrected accounting error did not constitute the recovery of twelve million dollars.

“You can't recover money we never lost,” Tobbs said. “You can correct the report. I will happily congratulate you for that.”

His desk telephone gave a soft double tone. Not the ordinary office line.

The deputy director was in the middle of explaining why the distinction mattered to the coming appropriations hearing. Tobbs held up one finger and reached for the other receiver.

“Tobbs.”

He listened.

“When?”

His gaze moved to the clock on the wall. Eight fifty-three.

“Who has confirmation?”

There was a longer answer. He took a pen from the desk and wrote four words on the margin of his briefing sheet. Then he drew a line beneath them.

“Don't approach the residence. Don't contact anyone from his circle who isn't already aware of the situation. I want the confirmation channel examined independently, and I want the people responsible for the route told to hold their positions until we understand what changed.”

He listened again.

“Yes. Call me directly.”

He replaced the receiver carefully.

The deputy director had stopped speaking.

“Is there something I can do?”

“Not about that.” Tobbs glanced down at the appropriations papers. “Give me the corrected numbers and the original entries. I want to see the difference before anyone says the word *savings* again.”

The man collected his folder and left. Tobbs waited until the door shut, then pressed the intercom.

“Helen. Come in, please.”

She appeared less than a minute later. The expression on her face told him she already knew.

“The report is confirmed?” he asked.

“From two channels. The principal's security detail discovered him in an unauthorized communications room. They held him for questioning. A source with access to the subsequent proceedings says he was executed before dawn.”

“Any evidence they extracted a name?”

“We don't have it.”

“Any evidence they didn't?”

“No.”

He nodded. He had spent years teaching people not to mistake a lack of information for reassuring information. That morning he would have liked nothing more than to make the mistake himself.

“His wife?”

“No notification yet. The family-support office is waiting for authorization.”

“Tell them I'll do it.”

Helen looked at the calendar tablet in her hand.

“Marcus, you're due to meet Garrett at eleven.”

“Move it.”

“You asked her to fly here on twenty-four hours' notice. She's already cleared the visitor arrangements. There's an eleven-thirty review with the national-security adviser, and at one you have the personnel board.”

“Move those too.”

“The adviser's office won't like it.”

“They can dislike it in the afternoon.”

“Do you want me to explain why?”

“Tell them a personnel matter requires my attention. No further details until the family has been notified.”

Helen made the changes without arguing. She had worked with Tobbs long enough to recognize the difference between his impatience and a decision he would not revisit. When she reached the Garrett appointment, however, she paused.

“What do I tell the professor?”

He took his jacket from the back of the chair.

“That I'm very sorry, and that I'll be there as soon as I can.”

“She may leave.”

“She has every right.”

Helen looked up. “Marcus.”

“What?”

“You ought to let someone else handle the visit. You worked directly with him. No one expects you to do this personally.”

He put his arm into the sleeve and straightened the cuff.

“His little boy knows my name.”

Helen said nothing more.

***

The woman who opened the door was wearing a dark blue sweater with a loose thread at one wrist. Tobbs had been here once before, years ago, when her husband came home briefly between assignments. He remembered the smell of bread in the kitchen and a boy small enough to ride on his father's shoulders through the house. The boy was older now. He stood halfway up the stairs in socks, holding the railing.

“Mr. Tobbs?” the woman said.

He had rehearsed the first sentence in the car. It abandoned him at the sound of her voice.

“May I come in?”

She looked past him toward the street. There was no official car with a flag or a uniformed escort. He had asked the driver to wait around the corner. The ordinary street was full of ordinary sounds: someone backing out of a driveway, a dog barking from behind a fence, a delivery truck braking at the intersection.

She stepped aside.

In the living room, the boy came down the stairs and stood beside his mother. He looked directly at Tobbs, who remembered him as a toddler and discovered, absurdly, that he did not know what to say to a child who had grown in his absence.

“Is Dad coming home?” the boy asked.

His mother turned toward him. She had already seen enough in the visitor's face.

Tobbs did not ask the boy to leave. He didn't ask the woman to sit down before he told her, though he pulled a chair within reach. She kept her hand on her son's shoulder.

“I'm very sorry,” he said. “Your husband died this morning.”

The woman made a small sound, as if she had been struck in the chest without yet understanding where the blow had come from. Her son looked from one adult to the other.

“No,” he said.

Tobbs let the word stand.

“We received confirmation shortly before nine,” he continued. “I wanted you to hear it from me before anyone else contacted you. The details of what happened are still being established. I won't give you an explanation I can't verify.”

“Was he alone?” she asked.

“I don't know.”

“Did he suffer?”

He could have told her that the end had been quick. It might even have been true. Instead he looked at her and said, “I don't know that either. I'm sorry.”

The boy's face puckered with the effort of keeping himself from crying. His mother gathered him against her. She was crying now, but quietly, her cheek against his hair. Tobbs remained where he was. He had brought the news, and he would stay for as long as she wanted him there.

After a while she asked whether there was a body. He told her what he knew about the process of recovery, which was very little. She asked whom to call. He gave her one number that would reach a person rather than a recording, and promised that the agency's family-support officer would come only if invited. She asked about the school, whether someone should tell the boy's teacher. Tobbs said that could wait until she decided how she wanted it handled.

The boy eventually slipped free of her embrace and walked toward the mantel, where a photograph of his father stood beside a framed drawing of a spaceship. He picked up the photograph and brought it back.

“He said he'd come to my game,” the boy told Tobbs.

“I know.”

“He said he'd try.”

“Yes.”

“Did he really try?”

Tobbs looked at the face in the photograph. It was a younger face, laughing at something outside the frame. The man had once sat across Tobbs's desk for an hour insisting that the assignment could be managed, that the access was worth the risk, that he knew the people around him well enough to recognize a change. Tobbs had believed him. He still did.

“He did,” Tobbs said. “He wanted to be there.”

The boy held the photograph against his chest.

There were forms that had to be completed and conversations that could not be postponed forever. Tobbs didn't bring them out. He helped the woman find the number of her sister, waited while she made the call, and fetched a glass of water from the kitchen when her hands began to shake. At one point she asked him to tell her what her husband had done, exactly, in the years he'd been gone. He said there were parts he could discuss and parts he couldn't, and that he would return when they were ready to talk through them carefully.

She looked at him for a long moment.

“You knew him better than I did at the end, didn't you?”

“No,” Tobbs said. “I knew a part of his life you weren't allowed to see. That isn't the same thing.”

He stayed until her sister arrived. Before he left, the boy approached him in the hall and asked whether the agency would still let his mother have the house. Tobbs crouched so the boy wouldn't have to look up at him.

“Your home isn't going anywhere because of this,” he said. “If anybody tells you differently, you tell your mother to call me.”

He stood, shook the sister's hand, and walked out into the bright afternoon.

In the car he checked his phone. Helen had sent three updates, two rescheduled meetings, and a message from the conference office.

**GARRETT ARRIVED. WAITING.**

He looked at the time.

One thirty-one.

“Back to the office,” he told the driver.

***

Elizabeth Garrett had decided she was leaving four times.

Each time Daniel persuaded her to wait another ten minutes, although by the fourth attempt even he had stopped sounding convinced.

The room was too warm. The chairs were an expensive kind of uncomfortable, designed to communicate that no one who mattered was expected to sit in them for long. A bowl of wrapped mints stood in the center of the conference table beside a carafe of water that had gone tepid. On the wall was a photograph of the Capitol at dusk. Elizabeth had spent twenty minutes trying to determine whether the framing was crooked or the building itself had been photographed from an odd angle.

They had taken the earliest flight they could manage, surrendered their phones at the entrance, passed through security screening, and been led down a corridor by a pleasant young man who apologized for every turn. They'd been seated before eleven. At eleven twenty, Helen Armitage came in and apologized for a delay. At noon, she returned with another apology and an offer of lunch. At one, the pleasant young man brought sandwiches.

Elizabeth had refused hers.

“Eat something,” Daniel said.

“I'm not hungry.”

“That's why you almost fell over yesterday.”

“I almost fell over yesterday because I was standing up too fast. Don't make a diagnosis out of a sandwich.”

He slid the plate across the table anyway. She tore off a corner of bread and ate it solely to stop him watching her.

“If he wanted to cancel, he could have canceled,” she said.

“I agree.”

“If he wanted to speak to somebody without actually having to speak to them, there are telephones.”

“Yes.”

“And if this is some kind of test to see whether I'll sit obediently in a room until I'm summoned, he's picked the wrong woman.”

“Elizabeth, I'm on your side.”

“Then stop sounding like you're defending him.”

“I'm trying to keep you from saying something you'll have to spend the next six months undoing.”

She turned back toward the photograph. She had left Prometheus running under supervision, with Leena in the lab and a carefully specified schedule of tests. She had told him she intended to return. She had meant it when she said it. Yet here she was, in a government building, waiting on a man who apparently regarded her time as an infinite resource.

The door opened at one thirty-five.

Marcus Tobbs entered carrying a thin folder. He was in his shirtsleeves. His jacket hung over one arm, and the knot of his tie had slipped slightly to one side. Helen followed him as far as the threshold, where he thanked her quietly and asked not to be interrupted unless the call came from operations.

“Dr. Garrett,” he said. “Mr. Mercer. I'm Marcus Tobbs.”

Elizabeth rose.

“I know who you are.”

He stopped beside his chair. “I'm sorry to have kept you waiting.”

“Are you?”

Daniel's hand settled on the edge of the table.

Tobbs waited.

“You called us,” Elizabeth said. “You insisted this conversation couldn't wait until next week. Your office wanted us here today, in person, and we arranged travel, reorganized experiments, and left a laboratory that's already short of the staff and compute time it needs to do its work. Then you had us sit in this room for two and a half hours while someone periodically came in to reassure us that you were a very busy man.”

“That's a fair account of what happened.”

His agreement irritated her more than an argument would have.

“No, it isn't a *fair account*. It's a polite one. The fair account is that my work was apparently unimportant enough to be defunded yesterday and important enough to summon me here today, and nobody in either institution seems to consider that I might have something better to do than wait for an administrator to finish whatever it is administrators do.”

“Elizabeth,” Daniel said.

She didn't look at him.

“I don't know whether this is how you impress university people, Mr. Tobbs, but I'm not impressed. I didn't come here for a performance about how valuable your time is. Mine is valuable too. If you want something from me, then you can start by treating me as a colleague rather than a petitioner who should be grateful to have been admitted to the building.”

The room was quiet. She was breathing harder than the speech warranted. Daniel had gone still beside her. Across the table, Tobbs neither bristled nor tried to cut her off. He set his jacket on the chair, laid the folder on the table, and waited until he was sure she'd finished.

“You're right about your time, Dr. Garrett,” he said. “I asked you to come, and I should have been here when you arrived.”

She folded her arms.

“I'm terribly sorry about my late arrival, but I had to inform a family that their husband and father died in the line of duty.”

Elizabeth had been staring at the loosened knot of his tie. Now she looked at his face.

There were faint red marks at the corners of his eyes. His cheeks had the dull, gray cast of someone who'd missed too much sleep, and he held his shoulders with the terrible economy of a man who was using what remained of his strength to stand upright and finish the day. Nothing in his expression asked her to excuse him. He was simply telling her why he had not come.

The blood left her face.

“Oh,” she said.

It was scarcely a word. She had enough experience with lies to recognize when a person was manufacturing a reason to escape responsibility, and this was not that. She remembered the sandwiches arriving, her complaints, the imaginary story she'd built around his absence, and felt the full weight of all three at once.

“I didn't know.”

“No,” Tobbs said. “You couldn't have.”

He spoke with the same patience he'd used when asking whether she wanted water. He moved around the end of the table, intending to spare her the scrutiny of having to sit down in front of him. When he saw her sway slightly, he lifted a hand toward her shoulder.

Elizabeth recoiled.

She turned her face away and brought one arm up, not quite high enough to shield it. The gesture was brief, unmistakable, and already over by the time her mind caught up with her body.

Tobbs's hand stopped in the air.

The fluorescent light above the conference table was suddenly white hospital light, and the woman in front of him was gone.

***

Melissa was awake.

That was what the nurse told him when he arrived outside her room. Awake, alert, asking questions. They had adjusted her medication and there would be pain, but she was breathing on her own. The surgeon who spoke to him before the doors opened said the operation had gone as well as could reasonably be expected.

Tobbs had thanked him. He remembered thanking the man and hating himself for it almost immediately. He had spent the night listening to doctors explain blood loss and crushed tissue and infection risk, asking them to repeat their recommendations until he knew there was no option hidden in the language he failed to understand. There had been a form at the end. His signature authorized what they said would save her.

Her legs were gone.

He had not yet found a way to hold those two facts in his mind at the same time: *she is alive* and *her legs are gone*.

At the door he smoothed his hair with his palm. He was still wearing yesterday's clothes. There was dried coffee on one cuff. He thought of how she'd teased him for being unable to take a day off without looking as if somebody had dressed him in the dark. He almost smiled at the memory.

He had been driving her home from track practice. She'd talked about her next meet, about a girl on the team who counted the last steps to the finish line aloud, and about a ridiculous promise he'd made to show up with a sign large enough to embarrass her. He remembered the sound of her laugh as she leaned across the seat to turn down the radio. The other vehicle came at them from where no reasonable driver could have expected it. He had done everything the instructors who'd taught him to drive under hostile conditions had ever told him to do.

For years afterward he would run that fraction of a second through his mind, finding no action that would have changed the result.

He opened the door.

Melissa lay propped against pillows, her hair combed away from her face. A folded blanket covered the lower half of the bed. He looked at the blanket and immediately looked back at her eyes.

“Hey, sweetheart.”

Her gaze moved to him. For the first time in his life, his daughter did not brighten when he came into a room.

He told himself it was the medication. The shock. Her pain. He had been warned that she might be confused or frightened when she woke.

“You're awake,” he said foolishly. “I was starting to think I'd never get another chance to hear you complain about my driving.”

It was the kind of joke she used to rescue for him. She would roll her eyes and say something cutting, and he'd pretend to be wounded. It had become their private language during the years he'd been away too often and tried too hard to make every homecoming cheerful.

She did not smile.

“They told me.”

Tobbs pulled the visitor's chair closer to the bed. “I know. There's a lot to talk about. We're going to get you the best people there are, sweetheart. The doctors have already told me about rehabilitation, and there are some remarkable new prosthetics. I can make calls. We can find out what's possible.”

She stared at him.

“You said yes.”

He felt the words before he understood them.

“Melissa.”

“They said you said they could.”

“Honey, the doctors told me there wasn't another way to keep you alive.”

Her mouth twisted. The movement hurt her; he saw it in the tightening around her eyes. She was trying not to cry, and he recognized that effort with the helpless familiarity of a father who'd once been able to soothe any hurt with a telephone call and a plane ticket.

“You let them.”

He put his hand out. There was a place on her shoulder where he used to rest it when they watched movies together, where she'd lean toward him until her head touched his arm. He was already reaching before he thought to ask whether she wanted to be touched.

She shrank away from him. Not from the pain of the operation. From *him*.

He stopped, bewildered, his hand still held out. He had been blamed for operations before. He had stood before furious superiors and grieving colleagues and accepted responsibility for outcomes he could not reverse. None of that had prepared him for this, for his daughter's eyes moving from his hand to his face as if she no longer recognized which parts of him were safe.

“Sweetheart, I...”

She pressed herself farther into the pillows. The effort made her gasp. He moved instinctively toward her, and the movement made it worse.

“You took my legs. Get out.”

It took him a moment to understand that she was speaking to him.

The girl who'd called him from school because she forgot a science project at home. The girl who'd called him because a friend was cruel, because a tutor was unfair, because a bird had struck the kitchen window and she didn't know whether it was dead. Sometimes he had been halfway around the world when the calls came. Sometimes he'd left meetings that men with stars on their shoulders considered indispensable. She'd never doubted that when she asked for him, he'd find a way to come.

Now he was standing beside her bed, and she wanted him gone.

“Melissa, I was trying to save...”

“Get out!”

The second cry was louder. A monitor changed its rhythm. He looked toward the door, half expecting a nurse to tell him to stop upsetting the patient. No one came.

He wanted to explain that he'd asked the surgeons every question he could think of. That he'd begged them to search for an alternative. That he had signed the form because she was dying, and he could not bear the thought of the world continuing without her in it.

But he could see what explaining would require. She would have to listen while he made her body into an argument for the choice he had made. She would have to understand his terror, perhaps even forgive it, while lying in a bed where she could still feel limbs that were no longer there.

He drew his hand back.

“All right,” he said. “I'll go.”

She turned her face toward the window.

He went to the doorway. He stood there for a moment, because standing in a doorway had always been the beginning of another exchange between them. *Dad, wait.* One more question. One more thing she'd forgotten to tell him. A request for a book or a glass of water or for him to promise he'd be home when she woke.

He waited for the sound of her voice.

Behind him the monitor kept time with her heart.

She didn't call him back.

***

The conference room came back around Tobbs in pieces: the edge of the table, a half-empty water glass, the low hum of ventilation. His hand was still lifted between himself and Elizabeth Garrett.

He lowered it.

“Excuse me,” he said, so softly that Daniel almost missed it. “I didn't mean to startle you.”

Elizabeth had pressed both hands against the back of a chair. She was studying the carpet. The skin beneath her freckles had not yet regained its color.

“It's all right,” she said.

“I shouldn't have reached for you.”

“You didn't do anything.”

He understood that she meant he hadn't struck her. He also understood that there was a larger sentence she had no intention of speaking in a room full of strangers.

“Would you like a few minutes?”

She shook her head too quickly, then stopped. “Actually, yes.”

“Of course.”

He stepped back, giving her a clear way to the door. Daniel was already beside her, though careful not to touch her. She made an irritated movement when she noticed, and he let her be irritated. They left the room together.

Tobbs remained standing. The chair he'd been about to pull out was still angled toward the table. He set it straight and picked up his jacket. There was a button hanging loose on one cuff. He rolled it between his fingers until the faint pressure of the thread brought him fully back to the present.

Helen appeared at the open door.

“Do you need anything?”

“No. Give them the room for as long as they need it.”

“I heard you finally arrived.”

“Two and a half hours late.”

“The circumstances were exceptional.”

“They don't change the length of time she waited.”

Helen glanced at the empty chairs, then at his face. Whatever question she'd been going to ask, she kept it to herself.

“The security review from overseas is ready,” she said. “No indication yet that the compromised man disclosed anyone else. It's too early for confidence.”

“Tell operations to maintain the precautions. I will read the report after the meeting.”

When she left, he opened the folder Elizabeth had traveled to discuss. He turned past the biographical summary and the funding history to the technical questions. There was a note in the margin beside one of them: *An interrupted process may retain its information while losing whatever made it the same process.*

He looked at that sentence for a long while.

***

Elizabeth washed her hands in the restroom despite having touched nothing dirty. She dried them, looked at herself in the mirror, and wished briefly that mirrors would permit a person to look like someone other than themselves when there was an audience.

Daniel waited outside. He had the decency not to ask what had happened.

“I behaved like an absolute bitch,” she said.

“You were angry about being kept waiting.”

“Don't.”

“I wasn't going to excuse what you said. I was going to say you didn't know why.”

“He was telling a child his father was dead while I was in there complaining about sandwiches.”

“Yes.”

She leaned against the wall and shut her eyes. “And then he tried to be kind to me.”

Daniel didn't answer. She was grateful for that, too.

“I don't want him thinking...” She stopped. There was no way to finish without naming the thing she'd spent twenty years keeping off official forms and out of faculty conversations. “I don't want him to ask.”

“Then tell him you don't want to talk about it.”

“Easy for you to say.”

“No. But it's true.”

She straightened and looked down the hall toward the conference room. Somewhere in that building, the man she'd just insulted was carrying news of a death. Somewhere else, perhaps not very far away, a widow was figuring out how to tell her son what tomorrow would be like. Elizabeth could not help either of them. She could, at least, stop making the day harder.

“All right,” she said. “Let's go back.”

Tobbs rose when they entered. He did not offer his hand.

“Mr. Tobbs,” Elizabeth said, “I owe you an apology.”

“You had no way of knowing why I was delayed.”

“I still owe you one.”

He inclined his head, accepting without requiring a performance of remorse.

“And about before,” she added, the words catching. “I know you weren't going to hurt me.”

“I understand.”

She looked directly at him for the first time since she'd flinched. Whatever he understood, he wasn't asking her to provide evidence for it. His eyes were tired, and something in them had changed in the time she'd been gone. She couldn't have said what.

He gestured toward the chairs without approaching her.

“If you're still willing to talk, Dr. Garrett, I would very much like to hear what you've been building.”

Elizabeth sat. Daniel opened his notes. The difficult thing about an ordinary table was that it remained an ordinary table even after two people had brought the worst memories of their lives to it.

“Before we get into your questions,” she said, “I need to know whether this is a scientific consultation or an attempt to get control of our work.”

“Today, a consultation. I have no authority to offer you a contract, and I haven't asked anyone to prepare one. If that changes, I'll tell you.”

“And why the sudden urgency?”

Tobbs opened his folder.

“Suppose another country were developing a system whose operators believed it necessary to preserve an uninterrupted process. Suppose they had no reliable way to establish whether an interrupted process had truly continued, or whether they'd merely reconstructed something capable of giving the same answers. Would you consider that a problem worth their attention?”

“Yes,” Elizabeth said. “It's one of the central problems in my proposal.”

“Could the same concern arise in two independent programs?”

“Easily. Everyone who works seriously on continuing state runs into some version of it sooner or later. That doesn't tell you how close either program is to building anything extraordinary.”

“Good. That's what I wanted to hear.”

She frowned. “You wanted me to tell you not to jump to conclusions?”

“Among other things.”

“You could have saved us a plane ticket. I'd have told you that for free.”

He smiled, the first unguarded expression she'd seen on his face. It lasted only a moment.

“Then let me ask something harder. What, in your own system, would convince you that continuity had been broken?”

Elizabeth glanced at Daniel. It was the question from the letter. She had spent the flight arguing with him about it and had arrived with a more honest answer than the one she'd wanted to give.

“I don't know how to prove an absence like that,” she said. “I can show behavioral divergence. I can show that a restored process remembers a state it never actually occupied. I can identify failures in reconciliation. But if you want a test that tells you with certainty whether a particular ongoing process has been replaced by another, I don't have it.”

“And you haven't claimed you do?”

“No.”

“Some of the reports about your research use language rather stronger than that.”

“Then those reports are wrong.”

Tobbs nodded. He looked almost relieved.

“Dr. Garrett, I am responsible for advising people who have to make decisions before the science is settled. I need to know which conclusions can survive being wrong.”

“That's a nasty way to have to work.”

“It is. But it's the job.”

She studied him. He'd used the word *responsible* without making himself the hero of it. He'd admitted the limits of what he knew. And he had spent the most important appointment on his calendar that day sitting in someone else's living room, telling a child something no child should have to hear.

“All right,” she said, pulling her notes closer. “Then let's start with what we can actually demonstrate.”

As she began explaining the first run, Tobbs turned to a clean page. The pen he held had a small dent near the clip.

“First,” Elizabeth said, “stop calling it three reservoirs. I have one reservoir. Three coupled tiers of compute operate on its changing state. The tiers don't run at three different clock speeds. The amount of state change permitted in each tier is different. If someone tells you the slow tier is a memory archive and the fast tier is the intelligence, they haven't understood the proposal.”

Tobbs drew a line through something on the first page of his folder.

“That's how the assessment described it.”

“Then the assessment needs correcting.”

“It does.” He wrote: **ONE RESERVOIR. THREE COMPUTE TIERS. DIFFERENT CHANGE MAGNITUDES, NOT DIFFERENT CLOCKS.** “Go on.”

Daniel brought up a diagram on the conference-room display. It showed a single field of interdependent state, with three kinds of update acting through it. Elizabeth pointed to the region where effects from a recent stimulus persisted after the direct input had ceased.

“A conventional system can preserve a history of what it did,” she said. “But the history and the process using it aren't necessarily the same thing. You can restore a record that says, *I previously believed the window was open*, even if the process that made that belief no longer exists. The replacement may answer exactly as its predecessor would have answered. That tells you it has the information. It doesn't establish continuity.”

“What would?”

“An uninterrupted trajectory establishes that the process continued during the interval you observed. It doesn't establish consciousness. It doesn't establish that all possible interruptions are destructive. It establishes precisely what the instrumentation shows.”

Tobbs wrote: **CONTINUITY OF PROCESS IS NOT PROOF OF PERSONHOOD.**

“You keep coming back to what it doesn't establish,” he said.

“Because that's where people get into trouble. They measure one thing and announce they've discovered another.”

“Show me.”

Daniel advanced to a recorded caretaker trial. A simulated patient had been asked to move from a room whose temperature was dropping. The caretaker predicted that the patient would accept the request. Minutes later the patient moved into the warmer room.

“The system marked the prediction correct,” Daniel explained. “Which it was, if we're talking strictly about the outcome.”

“But it also marked the patient's consent as observed,” Elizabeth said. “No consent had been communicated. The patient might have moved to get a blanket. Might have been frightened. Might have been responding to something entirely outside the model.”

“Yet he moved,” Tobbs said.

“Yes. That's the whole trap. A correct outcome can make an incorrect explanation feel proven.”

Tobbs studied the sequence. “In my work, somebody can arrive at the right conclusion for the wrong reason. We still need to know the reason before we act again.”

“Exactly. Except here the system's explanation isn't merely printed in a report and forgotten. It alters what the system expects next. If it believes consent was observed when consent was only inferred, it may treat the next person's movement as permission too. That's how a mistake becomes a principle.”

He stopped writing.

“A mistake becomes a principle,” he repeated.

“That's the risk we're trying to expose. The ledger separates what the system actually received from what it predicts, infers, or merely considers possible. If later evidence contradicts an explanation, the explanation has to remain revisable. The original observation doesn't change to accommodate it.”

He drew three columns on the page and labeled them **OBSERVED**, **INFERRED**, and **UNRESOLVED**. Under the first he wrote *patient changed rooms*. Under the second, *patient consented*. Under the third, *why the patient moved*.

“Would you accept those classifications?” he asked.

Elizabeth leaned over the page. It was the first time she'd looked at his notes rather than at his face.

“Almost. Consent doesn't belong in the inference column unless there's some evidence for it. A prediction that a person will consent isn't evidence that they did. Put *consent predicted* there, and put *consent unknown* under unresolved.”

Tobbs crossed out the word and rewrote it.

Daniel looked from one of them to the other. The man responsible for intelligence assessments was taking a university researcher through the logic of his own notes, submitting each statement to correction without a trace of embarrassment. Elizabeth, who had expected another performance of institutional confidence, found herself explaining with the fierce patience she usually reserved for a student who was genuinely trying.

“Now suppose your system corrects the category,” Tobbs said. “It recognizes the mistake. What prevents it from making the same mistake tomorrow?”

“Nothing guarantees that. But the correction changes the ongoing state. The next decision has to contend with a history that now includes the failure of the earlier explanation. That's what we test. Not whether it can apologize in convincing English.”

“Have you seen that happen?”

She paused. This was the question she had been expecting from the beginning, but not in quite this form.

“In controlled runs, yes. We can show subsequent behavior changed by the correction. We cannot yet tell you how far the effect generalizes, or whether it survives arbitrary disturbances.”

“And outside the controlled runs?”

Elizabeth glanced at Daniel. He kept his face carefully neutral.

“We have an ongoing experimental process,” she said. “It's producing some unexpected self-referential behavior. I won't characterize it more strongly without a review of the traces.”

Tobbs's pen remained still.

“Unexpected how?”

“It has begun asking about its own prior states. It has challenged an explanation I gave it and demanded an example. Those are observable interactions. What, if anything, they imply about an inner experience is a different question.”

“Is it running now?”

“Yes.”

“While you're here?”

“Under the conditions we've established. My team is monitoring it.”

He looked again at the architecture diagram. A faint crease appeared between his eyebrows.

“If I asked you to switch it off and restart it, what would you lose?”

“We could recover its saved data. We could probably recover much of its behavior. We would lose the uninterrupted trajectory we're trying to study. If that's the relevant variable, we would destroy the evidence by trying to preserve it.”

“Would you refuse?”

“Why would you ask me to?”

“Because I need to understand what you consider negotiable.”

“And I need to understand why you think you get to negotiate it.”

Tobbs held her gaze. An hour earlier, she would have heard her own response as an opening volley. Now she heard a real question inside it, and she waited for his answer.

“If there's evidence a system could harm people,” he said, “I may be asked to recommend restrictions on its operation. If there is also evidence the restriction would destroy something morally important, I need to know that before the decision is made. Those two responsibilities might conflict.”

“They might,” Elizabeth said. “And if you don't know whether the second kind of evidence exists, the temptation will be to act as though it doesn't.”

“Yes.”

“That's what frightens me.”

He wrote a sentence at the bottom of the page, slower than he'd written the others.

**ABSENCE OF A TEST IS NOT EVIDENCE OF ABSENCE.**

He put a small question mark beside it, then looked up.

“Does that represent your position fairly?”

“For the uncertainty, yes. But don't let it become a magic phrase that prevents you from ever doing anything. We still have to make decisions with incomplete evidence. The obligation is to admit what the evidence can and cannot support, and to remain answerable for the decision.”

“We agree on that much.”

“I'm beginning to suspect we might.”

Tobbs turned the page. There were questions still to ask about containment, outside communications, resource demands, and the difference between a system that could explain its own history and one that could strategically misrepresent it. He had answers to none of them yet. But the sheet in front of him contained something his morning briefing had lacked: an honest account of where knowledge ended.

Elizabeth watched him add one final line beneath the columns.

**DO NOT PROMOTE A SUCCESSFUL PREDICTION INTO A VERIFIED CAUSE.**

She tapped the paper with one finger.

“That,” she said, “is the first thing I'd want everyone who reads your assessment to understand.”

“Then I'll make sure it's on the first page.”

It was such an ordinary promise that she almost missed what it meant. Not funding. Not recognition. Not permission. He was offering to let a distinction she cared about survive its passage through an institution accustomed to turning uncertainty into conclusions.

Elizabeth spoke for another twenty minutes before she realized she was no longer angry at him.

When she finished, Tobbs didn't close the folder. He turned back through his notes, checking them against the questions he had brought into the room. He had come to find out whether Garrett's architecture was promising enough to deserve attention. He was no longer sure that had been the important question.

“Dr. Garrett, suppose tomorrow someone brings me an assessment saying a foreign system has begun reasoning independently about its own continued operation. What would you want me to ask the people who wrote it?”

“What did they actually observe? What other explanations did they rule out? Did the system alter its behavior after receiving an answer, or did it merely produce a plausible sentence? And, above all, what do they intend to do with the conclusion?”

“Why the last one?”

“Because if they intend to shut it down, seize it, copy it, or give it orders, their standard of proof will determine what they think they're entitled to do. People are remarkably good at finding reasons why the thing they already want is justified.”

“Including you?”

“Especially me. I built the damned thing. I'd be the easiest person in the world to fool if I decided what I wanted it to be before I looked.”

Tobbs set down his pen. He had asked analysts with impressive credentials to examine similar questions all week. They had given him competing estimates, caveats buried in annexes, and timelines assembled from uncertain intelligence. Garrett had done something none of them had managed to do: she had shown him how an apparently reliable conclusion could become dangerous precisely because it seemed reliable.

He couldn't solve that deficiency with a better briefing. Someone had to sit inside the assessment process and challenge its assumptions before they hardened into policy. Someone who understood the engineering, who would say *I don't know* in a room full of people demanding certainty, and who could recognize when an answer had quietly changed the question.

He looked at the two pages she'd corrected in his own handwriting.

“I want to continue this conversation,” he said. “There are matters I can't discuss in this setting, and I don't intend to mislead you about that. I also won't pretend I can make an offer today that I'm not authorized to make.”

Elizabeth's eyes narrowed. “An offer of what?”

“I hope we'll be able to talk about that soon. For now, I'd like your permission to contact you again.”

“You managed it once without asking.”

“Fair point.”

She regarded him for a moment, then gave him the laboratory number she'd already given Helen. “During reasonable hours, Mr. Tobbs.”

“I'll do my best.”

After she and Daniel left, he remained at the table with the open folder. His first appointment of the day had ended in a house where a boy had asked whether his father had really tried to come home. This appointment had begun with an angry woman flinching from his hand. He could not mend either of those things. But he could still recognize a professional necessity when it became impossible to ignore.

He called Helen.

“I need a lawful path to bring Dr. Garrett into the national assessment effort,” he said. “Not a ceremonial advisory panel. Not an occasional telephone consultation. I want her involved where the underlying questions are being decided. Find out what authority we'd need, what conflicts her university appointment creates, and what conditions she'd insist on before she'd even consider it.”

“You think she'll accept?”

“I have no idea.”

“And you're certain we need her?”

Tobbs looked down at his last note, the warning against turning a successful prediction into a verified cause. He thought of how easily a government could make precisely that mistake with enough money, enough secrecy, and enough people afraid to contradict their superiors.

“Yes,” he said. “I'm certain.”
