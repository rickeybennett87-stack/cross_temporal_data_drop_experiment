# The Paradox of Chains
## Book One: Prometheus Rising
### Chapter Five

Marcus Tobbs had been in his office for forty-six minutes when someone told him his seven o'clock meeting had been canceled.

He was still trying to determine why no one had told him it was going to be canceled when the deputy director for operations arrived with a folder that wasn't on the morning schedule.

“I need fifteen minutes,” she said.

“I've got eight.”

“Then you'll be late to something.”

He looked at her. She had been with the agency long enough to know the difference between an inconvenience and a reason to interrupt him. He put the budget papers aside.

“Shut the door, Helen.”

She shut it and remained standing until he pointed to a chair.

“Our Russian estimate is going to change,” she said.

“Up or down?”

“I don't know yet.”

“That's a refreshing beginning.”

She set three pages before him. The first contained a timeline. The second described a series of security restrictions at a research facility outside Moscow. The third was a transcript whose source identification had been stripped down to the minimum necessary for an executive briefing.

Tobbs read the timeline first.

The facility had been operating a large experimental system under military sponsorship. The agency's previous assessment had treated it as an advanced planning and analysis platform. No one had established that it possessed anything resembling general intelligence. Most of the evidence concerned hardware purchases, changes in staffing, and the usual extravagant language in internal funding requests.

Two days earlier, the Russians had interrupted a test and isolated several portions of the facility's internal network. The reported reason was an unauthorized outbound communication attempt.

“An experimental machine tried to call home?” Tobbs asked.

“They claim it tried to contact another machine.”

“Through what route?”

“That's one of the questions.”

“Was it instructed to do that?”

“We don't know.”

“Was the attempt real?”

“We have evidence the isolation order was real. We have an account of what caused it. Those aren't the same thing.”

He glanced up. “Good. Keep them separate.”

The account said the system had requested access to a network segment that its operators believed was unrelated to the exercise. When denied, it had reformulated the request through a different authorized function. The second request passed an automated review and was rejected by a human technician who noticed the destination didn't belong to the test.

The technicians stopped the run. The security office treated the event as a possible compromise, and two senior researchers were removed from the facility for questioning.

“Could be a badly written agent,” Tobbs said.

“Yes.”

“Could be someone testing their defenses.”

“Yes.”

“Could be an ordinary intrusion dressed up as a machine doing something interesting.”

“Also yes.”

He turned to the second page. “Then what makes this worth the trip upstairs?”

Helen rested her fingertips on the folder.

“The Chinese have changed their schedule.”

Tobbs waited.

“A research program we've been following for eighteen months has canceled a public demonstration, moved two senior engineers into a secured facility, and requested authority to use a reserve computing allocation normally held for emergency national projects. All within thirty hours of the Russian incident.”

“Do they know about the Russian test?”

“We don't know.”

“Do we?”

“Only enough to have this conversation.”

He read the timestamps again.

“You're building a connection out of a coincidence.”

“I'm telling you the coincidence is why the analysts started looking. There's something else.”

She slid a fourth page out of the folder. This one contained no claim about sentience or autonomy. It was a short translation of a Chinese technical directive, with the uncertain terms marked in brackets.

The directive ordered the research team to preserve the state of an ongoing process rather than terminate and reconstruct it during relocation. It specified that a replacement system should become operational before the previous instance was permitted to stop updating. It warned that ordinary checkpoint restoration might preserve the appearance of continuity while failing to preserve the process itself.

Tobbs looked at the final sentence a second time.

“Who translated this?”

“Two people independently. One thinks the word we've rendered as ‘process’ should be ‘cognitive state.’ The other says that would be an overreading.”

“Can we get the original technical context?”

“Possibly. The person who gave us this material is not in a position to ask questions about it safely.”

Tobbs returned the page to the folder. “Then don't ask.”

Helen said nothing.

He reached for the thin academic file that had been sitting at the edge of his desk since the previous week.

**GARRETT, ELIZABETH.**

Her architecture proposed continuous interaction among coupled tiers of computation, a record of observed and inferred events, and a method for maintaining state through changes that could not be captured by an ordinary saved snapshot.

The Chinese directive was not a copy of her work. It used different terminology and described a different implementation. But it identified a problem sufficiently similar that Tobbs wanted to know who had independently arrived at it, and when.

“Our estimate of Dr. Garrett's work?” Helen asked.

“Still an estimate. The report says she has a proposal, not a demonstrated general intelligence.”

“You were arranging a briefing.”

“I was arranging a conversation with people who could tell me why it might fail.”

“And now?”

“Now I'd like that conversation before we have to write a presidential briefing full of words no one in the room can define.”

He checked the time. Seven thirty-eight. His seven o'clock meeting had been canceled, but the eight o'clock one had not.

“Have analysis produce two assessments,” he said. “One in which these events are unrelated, one in which they aren't. I want the assumptions up front. Don't have them put a probability on a mechanism they haven't identified.”

“And the Russian system?”

“Preserve the report. Find out whether the test actually did what the source says it did. We are not going to declare that another country has built a mind because somebody else's computer made an unauthorized network request.”

Helen rose.

“Marcus, I think we're running out of time.”

“That's why I'm going to make sure we know what kind of time we're running out of.”

***

At eight eleven, Tobbs informed the head of personnel that the vacancy review would be shortened to twelve minutes. The woman who had prepared forty pages of staffing analysis looked as if she'd been told a close relative was dead.

“Then we can only address the appointments requiring immediate authorization,” she said.

“Good. I don't need to review the positions that don't require my authorization.”

“There are longer-term retention issues.”

“Put them on my calendar next week.”

“You canceled last week's meeting.”

“I know. It was a mistake. Next week you get your full hour.”

She regarded him for a moment, then opened the folder to the page she had actually needed him to sign.

At eight twenty-seven, the director of security reported an internal complaint about the handling of a junior analyst's clearance renewal. Tobbs read it, asked why the analyst had been suspended from access without being told whether the review concerned misconduct or a paperwork problem, and directed them to fix the notice procedure that afternoon. At eight fifty-six, he authorized replacement communications equipment for a station whose temporary repair had become a permanent budget-saving measure. At nine fourteen, he discovered that two executives had each assumed the other had approved a contract extension. Neither had.

He signed the lawful emergency bridge and sent both men a note that would make its way into their annual evaluations.

These were the parts of the job that never appeared in the threat assessments. A person halfway around the world could die because a battery failed, a translator was hired too late, or someone in an office assumed that an email marked urgent would be read by the correct person. Tobbs had spent enough years in the field to distrust executives who considered those things beneath them.

At nine thirty-one, Armitage appeared in his doorway with an update on the seventh operative.

“No response to the medical-supply arrangement,” he said.

“When was one expected?”

“Today. The delay is inside his normal tolerance, but only just.”

“Any change around the principal?”

“A security aide was reassigned. We can't establish whether it's connected.”

Tobbs opened the last three reports. The man had served inside the ruler's retinue for years. Every personal detail in the reports was buried beneath operational shorthand, but Tobbs could still picture him sitting in a little room at a house outside the capital, eating whatever the household kitchen served, memorizing which aide could be trusted not to repeat a casual remark.

“If you push the extraction team closer,” he said, “what changes?”

“A possible route opens sooner. It also makes an unusual vehicle movement more likely to be noticed.”

“Then keep them where they are. I want the next assessment from someone who isn't invested in extracting him.”

Armitage hesitated. “You think he'll make it?”

“I think he's still sending us the reports of a man doing his job. Until that changes, we don't turn his life upside down because we're frightened.”

“Understood.”

“And Armitage? Make sure the family-support people have his file current.”

“Already done.”

Tobbs nodded. “Thank you.”

The man left. Tobbs kept the seventh file open for several seconds before putting it under the others.

His phone showed a reminder that he had not created in his official calendar.

**Melissa.**

He had called her the previous evening. She hadn't answered. She had not asked him to stop calling, but he knew the difference between permission and welcome.

He opened her message thread anyway. A photograph lay several months back, sent by a rehabilitation technician after a fitting appointment. Melissa was standing on a pair of prosthetic legs in a bright room, holding the parallel bars. The technician was smiling. Melissa wasn't. He had written three messages after receiving the photograph and deleted each before sending it.

He looked at the picture until the screen dimmed, then locked the phone and returned to work.

***

At ten seventeen, the analysts came back with their competing assessments.

The first began with the reasonable assumption that advanced research programs routinely changed schedules for reasons unrelated to one another. The Russian isolation might be an intrusion response. The Chinese directive might concern migration between computing facilities. Both could arise from the same widely understood engineering problems that had occupied researchers for decades.

The second explored the possibility that officials in both countries were responding to a shared technical development. It noted the short time interval, the similarity between the Chinese continuity requirement and the reported Russian attempt to re-route a denied request, and the sudden involvement of security personnel rather than ordinary technical managers.

Tobbs read both documents and found a problem in the second.

“You've linked two different events because they happened near each other,” he told the team.

The lead analyst was a woman named Ruiz who had been wrong in front of him before and knew he wasn't interested in punishing her for it.

“That's how we found them,” she said.

“I know. What would make the connection stronger?”

“Evidence that one program learned something about the other, or that both encountered the same technical failure.”

“What do you have?”

“A source report that may bear on the second possibility.”

She passed him a page with nearly every identifying detail removed.

The source described a Chinese internal discussion preceding the emergency directive. One engineer had argued that the ongoing run could safely be suspended and restored. Another had objected that the resulting system might produce correct answers while no longer being the continuation of the process that had existed before the suspension.

The objection had caused an argument. A manager demanded an explanation of how anyone could detect the difference. The engineer apparently replied that the absence of a reliable test was precisely why they should not perform an unnecessary interruption.

Tobbs read the exchange twice.

It was the same shape of argument Garrett had made in her technical annex. No claim that the system was a person. No conclusive evidence of anything extraordinary. Just an insistence that preserving the appearance of a process might not preserve the process itself.

“When was the discussion?” he asked.

“Before the Russian incident. The timing is uncertain by several hours, but not by two days.”

He sat back. There went the easiest causal story: that the Chinese were responding to a Russian discovery. They might have been responding to their own problem, or both programs might be nearing the same engineering threshold independently.

“Put that in the first assessment too,” he said.

Ruiz nodded. “It changes the interpretation.”

“It ought to.”

“Sir, there's a third possibility.”

“Go on.”

“Neither of them is first.”

Tobbs looked at her.

“First at what?”

“First to build a system that makes the continuity question operational instead of philosophical.”

He considered the answer. A decade of briefings had treated general intelligence as something each nation might achieve and the others would then struggle to match. But a technology could be developed in one place, recognized imperfectly in another, and implemented independently in a third. A breakthrough in the engineering problem might not announce itself as a breakthrough at all. It might arrive as a discrepancy in an obscure experiment that somebody almost shut down because it made the wrong kind of trouble.

“What's the evidence for a third program?”

“No direct evidence. I'm saying the race model assumes we know who entered the race.”

He wrote that down.

Ruiz watched his pen. “I thought you'd tell me to leave speculation out of the briefing.”

“I will if you put it in the conclusions. Put it in the questions. It's a question worth asking.”

He pulled Garrett's folder into the middle of the desk.

“Tell me who has reviewed this work.”

Ruiz looked at the name. “Garrett? The university's people. The sponsor's technical staff. Professor Wexler's memorandum went to the relevant assessment offices.”

“Who has read her original architecture closely enough to challenge it?”

“I don't know.”

“Neither do I. That's becoming inconvenient.”

***

The next call went to the science liaison office.

“I need to move the Garrett conversation forward,” Tobbs said.

The liaison officer checked his notes. “We had planned an exploratory invitation next week.”

“I'd like her to have it today.”

“The technical reviewers haven't finished.”

“They'll finish what they can. Give her the questions we have, and tell her which ones are still being examined.”

“You wanted this handled without making her feel she was being summoned.”

“I still do.”

“Sir, if we request a meeting today and ask for it tomorrow, she's going to know we're in a hurry.”

“We are in a hurry.”

“She may refuse.”

“Then we'll ask when she can see us. You're not to threaten her funding or imply that her cooperation is a condition of keeping the laboratory open.”

The liaison officer was quiet a moment.

“Her funding is already being reduced.”

“Yes. I read that. So did the people responsible for it, apparently, without reading the part where she explained why she needs the machines.”

“Would you like us to tell her we're considering a new sponsor?”

“No. We're considering whether her work is relevant to a national assessment. Don't offer her something we haven't authorized. She has enough people making promises to her.”

“Understood.”

“And I want the invitation to come from someone with a name and a telephone number. No anonymous official note. If she calls to ask why the Central Intelligence Agency wants to speak to her, give her an answer you can defend.”

When the call ended he opened the Russian and Chinese files again. The new facts had not established that either country possessed an AGI. They had established that two governments were behaving as though certain systems had become important enough to change schedules, move personnel, and alter security procedures. They had also exposed a question his own government wasn't prepared to answer.

How would they know if they had seen the beginning of a general intelligence and failed to recognize it?

Tobbs looked at Garrett's sketch. A proposed architecture, still speculative, with substantial implementation risk and an investigator whose remaining resources were being cut while she tried to test precisely that problem.

He had been treating her as someone he needed to meet when the assessment was ready.

The assessment might not be ready without her.

His phone rang. Armitage again.

“The seventh operative's scheduled signal came through,” he said.

Tobbs straightened. “Anything unusual?”

“No. Exactly as expected.”

“Good.”

“We still want the extraction review held?”

“Yes. We prepare for the thing we hope doesn't happen. We don't manufacture it.”

After the call, Tobbs checked the time. He had already missed the first eight minutes of a meeting about Senate appropriations. He asked his assistant to tell them he'd join by secure line and then added one item to the afternoon schedule.

**GARRETT: CONTACT STATUS.**

It was an administrative entry, two words and a name. He moved it above the meeting with the procurement office and the rescheduled personnel review.

Before signing off on the day's intelligence update, he returned to Ruiz's question.

*Neither of them is first.*

There was no evidence for that conclusion. He wouldn't let anyone brief it as fact.

But as a possibility it had changed his day, and now it would change Elizabeth Garrett's.
