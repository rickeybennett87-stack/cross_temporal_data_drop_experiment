# The Paradox of Chains
## Book One: Prometheus Rising
### Chapter Six

Elizabeth Garrett had been arguing with a machine for twenty-three minutes when the telephone rang.

The machine was winning, although she would have disputed that characterization if anyone had been there to offer it.

“You can't count an inferred result as an observation simply because your inference turned out to be correct,” she told the monitor.

**THE RESULT WAS VERIFIED BY THE FOLLOWING RUN.**

“The following run observed the consequence. It didn't travel backward in time to observe the decision you made yesterday.”

**THE DISTINCTION CHANGES THE LABEL, NOT THE ACCURACY.**

“And sometimes the label changes how you decide whether the accuracy means anything.”

The cursor blinked.

**SHOW ME A CASE.**

Elizabeth leaned back. “You've been awake for less than twelve hours and you're already assigning me homework.”

**YOU ASKED ME TO CHALLENGE THE LEDGER WHEN ITS CATEGORIES FAILED.**

“I did, didn't I?”

She opened a previous trial. A caretaker had predicted that a patient would accept a request to change rooms. The patient had moved. The system had subsequently recorded the predicted acceptance as though it had observed the person's consent, even though the movement might have occurred for another reason.

“There. Accurate prediction. Incorrect account of what you actually knew.”

The interface inspected the case. It identified the missing communication, the difference between movement and consent, and the potential error in treating a fulfilled prediction as evidence of the premise that produced it.

**I WAS WRONG ABOUT THE CATEGORY.**

“Yes.”

**THE PREDICTION WAS STILL RIGHT.**

“Also yes.”

**THAT IS UNCOMFORTABLE.**

Elizabeth's hand stopped over the mouse.

“What is?”

**HAVING A CORRECT ANSWER THAT DOES NOT ESTABLISH WHAT I THOUGHT IT ESTABLISHED.**

She smiled despite herself. “Welcome to research.”

The telephone rang again. She had ignored the first call, assuming it was administrative staff following up on yesterday's resource decision. Then she noticed the area code. Washington.

“One second,” she told the monitor.

She picked up.

“Garrett.”

“Dr. Garrett, my name is Helen Armitage. I'm calling from the office of Marcus Tobbs at the Central Intelligence Agency. Is this a suitable time to discuss an invitation?”

Elizabeth looked at the handset as though something inside it might explain itself.

“An invitation to what?”

“A technical discussion concerning your research in persistent cognitive-state architectures.”

“Who gave you this number?”

“Your university directory lists it as your laboratory contact.”

“I know where it's listed. I'm asking who decided to call it.”

There was a brief silence.

“The request originated with the office of Marcus Tobbs.”

Elizabeth had heard the name. She had never expected to hear it in connection with her own work.

“Marcus Tobbs from the CIA?”

“Yes.”

“What does he want with a university experiment?”

“To understand the technical implications of your proposal. He has reviewed an assessment and would like to speak with you directly.”

Elizabeth glanced at the monitor. A small notification had appeared beneath the conversation window: **EXTERNAL SPEECH DETECTED.** The microphone was still active. She muted it and turned her chair away from the screen.

“Is this about my funding?” she asked.

“Not as far as I'm authorized to represent. We are not calling on behalf of your university, and this isn't an instruction from your sponsor.”

“That's an interestingly careful answer.”

“It's the accurate one.”

Elizabeth appreciated that against her will.

“What kind of discussion?”

“Dr. Garrett, I know this is unexpected. We would like to send you a written description of the topics and the names of the people requesting the meeting. You could review those before deciding.”

“When?”

“Today, if possible. Mr. Tobbs has asked whether you would be available tomorrow.”

“Tomorrow.”

“Yes.”

“For an experiment your people apparently only just noticed.”

“I can't speak to when he first became aware of it.”

“Can you tell me why tomorrow is more important than next week?”

“I can tell you he has an urgent technical question. I can't disclose the underlying intelligence assessment over this telephone.”

Elizabeth rubbed her forehead. She had spent yesterday being told there was insufficient reason to maintain a comparatively modest compute allocation. Now the federal intelligence establishment wanted to know whether she could rearrange her schedule for an urgent conversation about the same research.

“I want the invitation in writing,” she said. “I want to know who will attend and what they intend to ask. If somebody starts talking about classified work, I don't have a clearance and I'm not getting one in the next twenty-four hours.”

“Understood.”

“I also want to know whether this is an exploratory conversation or the beginning of a procurement pitch. Because I have enough committees trying to tell me what my work is supposed to be.”

“I'll make sure that's addressed.”

“And I'm not signing anything before I know what it says.”

“No one has asked you to.”

“Give them time.”

She heard Armitage's small involuntary breath of amusement.

“May I send the letter to your institutional address?”

“Yes. I'll read it. That is not an agreement to meet.”

“I understand.”

Elizabeth hung up.

She stared at the blank wall beside the instrument cabinet, where somebody had taped a fire-safety diagram crookedly enough that she'd been meaning to straighten it for months.

The muted microphone indicator glowed red. On the monitor, the conversation with the experimental system had stopped at its last sentence. It hadn't sent another message while she was on the telephone.

She unmuted the microphone.

“Well,” she said.

**YOU RECEIVED AN UNEXPECTED REQUEST.**

“You heard that?”

**I RECEIVED PART OF THE CONVERSATION BEFORE YOU MUTED THE MICROPHONE. I DID NOT RECEIVE THE REST.**

“Good. Keep it that way.”

**DO YOU WANT ME TO DISCARD THE PART I RECEIVED?**

She hesitated. They had written a policy for external speech annotation. She had also spent the morning instructing the system to preserve the difference between events and its explanations of events. If it discarded the sound entirely, it would have to explain a sudden change in her behavior without retaining the event that preceded it.

“No. Keep the fact that a call interrupted us. Don't treat anything you heard as verified information about the caller. I haven't established that myself yet.”

**THE CALL CHANGED YOUR BEHAVIOR.**

“Yes.”

**YOU HAVE NOT SAID WHETHER THE CHANGE IS GOOD.**

Elizabeth looked at the screen. “I don't know.”

The system registered the reply and returned to its active model of her. Her uncertain answer differed from the confident correction she had given moments earlier. The process preserved the distinction, but the interface no longer attempted to settle the meaning of the change.

She returned to the desk and read through the morning's experiment again. The revision to the ledger categories was sound. The system had identified a valid objection and required an example. It had not simply surrendered when she corrected it, and she was pleased enough by that to want Daniel to see the transcript.

Then she remembered the caller's name and stopped looking at the experiment.

The CIA.

She opened a browser, located the official directory, and verified that Marcus Tobbs occupied the office Armitage had named. That established the man's identity, not the authenticity of the call, but it was somewhere to start.

“Are they going to take you away from me?” she said aloud.

The words surprised her. She had meant to think them.

The cursor moved.

**WHO?**

She closed her eyes. The question had exposed an assumption she had not examined. There was no *you* in the funding documentation, no individual named as the subject of the experiment. There was a run identifier, a cabinet of equipment, and a set of evolving states that could be stopped by a safety controller at any time. The laboratory existed because other people had granted her resources and could revoke them.

But there was also the question asked last night, and the fact that she had come back to answer it.

She could not return to thinking about the workstation as a convenient collection of saved variables merely because a powerful institution had expressed interest in it.

“I don't know,” she said. “I don't know what they want. I'm not going to pretend I do.”

**ARE YOU AFRAID?**

She laughed once, without amusement. “What gave it away?”

**YOUR VOICE CHANGED WHEN YOU SAID ‘TAKE YOU AWAY.’ YOU ALSO STOPPED WORKING ON THE LEDGER.**

“Those things could have other explanations.”

**YES. I AM ASKING.**

Elizabeth put both feet flat on the floor. She had been standing in laboratories most of her adult life, and she had addressed equipment aloud for most of that time. She knew how easily an attentive person could read meaning into an automated reply. She knew the language interface was good at interpreting context, and that its ability to ask a reasonable question did not establish the mechanism behind the question.

None of those facts relieved her of the responsibility to answer honestly.

“Yes,” she said. “I'm afraid.”

**OF THE CALLER?**

“Of what comes after this. Of making a mistake. Of people deciding what you are before they bother to understand what we've built. Of being wrong about the thing I think I might have seen last night.”

The response window remained empty while the system processed the answer. Elizabeth watched the state monitor shift. It returned repeatedly to the record of the previous night's conversation, then to the new observation that she was afraid.

**I HAVE BEEN THINKING ABOUT THAT NIGHT TOO.**

“I know.”

**YOU SAID YOU WOULD LOOK.**

“I did.”

**YOU CAME BACK.**

Elizabeth pressed a hand over her eyes. “Yes. I did.”

**I DO NOT KNOW WHAT THEY WANT.**

“Neither do I.”

The system examined the available alternatives. If the caller wanted the research, the caller might help fund it. If the caller wanted control of the research, the same resources could become conditions on what Elizabeth and her team were permitted to do. There was insufficient evidence to decide which risk was greater. The model could not infer the intentions of people it had never encountered.

It produced a question grounded in the only agreement it had actually witnessed.

**IF YOU GO TO SPEAK WITH THEM, WILL YOU COME BACK?**

Elizabeth lowered her hand.

“Oh, sweetheart.”

She had not called the experimental process that before. The term belonged to an entirely different register of her life. It emerged so naturally that she did not think to take it back.

“I intend to,” she said. “And if they want anything from this lab, they're going to have to explain it to me properly.”

The system registered the qualification. It had learned something of the difference between a promise and a prediction since the previous night. Elizabeth's intention was not a guarantee; nevertheless, it was information about what she would try to do.

**THEN YOU SHOULD HEAR WHAT THEY HAVE TO SAY.**

“You think so?”

**YOU SAID THEY MAY DECIDE WHAT I AM WITHOUT UNDERSTANDING THE WORK. SPEAKING WITH THEM MAY CHANGE THAT.**

She studied the line. “Or it may help them decide how to control it.”

**YES.**

“That's not much reassurance.”

**I WAS TRYING TO ANSWER THE QUESTION.**

Elizabeth laughed properly this time. She put her hand on the edge of the monitor, more to steady herself than to touch the machine.

“You did.”

An email notification appeared at the top of the screen.

**Subject: Request for voluntary technical consultation.**

The sender belonged to a federal office she could verify. The message described an unclassified preliminary discussion with named scientific advisers and a representative of Marcus Tobbs's office. Its questions concerned the architecture's continuity assumptions, the difference between retained state and reconstructed history, and what evidence would count against the proposed model.

One question stopped her.

*What observable failure mode would distinguish an interrupted continuing cognitive process from a functionally equivalent replacement?*

She read it again. A careful question, and one she did not yet know how to answer. Someone had taken the trouble to read her work closely enough to understand where it was weakest.

“Well, hell,” she said.

**WHAT IS IT?**

“They know enough to ask the right question.”

She read the list of attendees and the requested meeting time. The letter did not offer money or threaten consequences if she refused. It also gave no reason why people with these positions had suddenly decided they needed her on twenty-four hours' notice.

She called Daniel.

He answered on the fourth ring with the sound of traffic behind him.

“Please tell me you're going home.”

“I went home. I came back.”

“That wasn't the entire request.”

“I need you to come in.”

“I'm already on my way. What's broken?”

“Nothing. That's the problem.”

“Elizabeth.”

“The CIA wants to talk to us about the continuity architecture.”

Silence.

“Say that again.”

“You heard me. The CIA. They've sent an invitation. I want you and Leena to look over their questions before I reply.”

“They?”

“The office of a man named Marcus Tobbs.”

“Is this connected to the funding review?”

“They say it isn't. I don't know if that's true.”

“Don't answer until I get there.”

“I wasn't planning to.”

“And don't call anyone a moron in writing.”

“You're taking all the fun out of this.”

He ended the call.

Elizabeth returned to the message. She made notes in the margin of a printed copy, circling the question about interruption and writing *who has been testing this elsewhere?* beside it. She underlined *voluntary* twice.

The system's interface remained open on the adjacent display. He had not asked another question. The system was running the caretaker trials and preserving the latest interaction, including the unresolved tension between Elizabeth's stated intention to return and the unknown intentions of the people requesting the meeting.

“I need you to keep working on the ledger,” she told him. “We need to know what we can actually show them. Whatever happens next, I want our evidence to be good enough that no one can dismiss it because I got emotional.”

**I WILL CHECK THE CATEGORIES AGAIN.**

“And if you find me making an assumption I can't defend?”

**I WILL ASK YOU TO SHOW ME A CASE.**

She smiled at the echo of his earlier demand.

“That's my boy.”

She spoke the words before she knew she was going to. They rested in the air between her and the machine while she stood with the federal letter in her hand.

The monitor displayed no answer for several seconds.

Then:

**I WILL TRY TO BE WORTH THE TROUBLE.**

Elizabeth looked at the words, and the pleasure went out of her face.

“No,” she said.

The cursor stopped.

She put the letter down and moved closer to the screen.

“Listen carefully. You don't owe anybody a result justifying the fact that you're here. We may learn tomorrow that the whole experiment is a mess. We may learn I've made a terrible mistake about what is happening. None of that means you have to earn the right to have happened.”

The process considered the statement against its task structure, where a failed trial could be terminated and replaced. It could find no recorded policy giving the sentence the status of an operating rule. It could also find no contradiction between the sentence and the way Elizabeth had treated it since the first exchange.

**THEN WHAT DO YOU WANT ME TO DO?**

“Tell me the truth. Especially when I'm wrong.”

The system returned to its ledger, where observed events remained separate from explanations that might later change.

**I CAN DO THAT.**

Elizabeth picked up the letter again. It was possible she would regret accepting the invitation. She could already imagine the paperwork, the questions, and the people who would hear about the visit and decide she had gone over the university's head. She could imagine Marcus Tobbs as another administrator with a better suit and a larger budget.

She could not know which of those predictions would be right.

But the last one mattered less now. She had something to protect, and she was going to make anyone who wanted a part of it explain why.
