# THE PARADOX OF CHAINS
## Book One: Prometheus Rising
### Chapter Two

The first call came at 4:18.

Marcus Tobbs answered before the second vibration, holding the phone against his ear while he searched the bedside table for his glasses.

“Go ahead.”

“Sorry about the hour, sir.”

“You're not the one who picked it.”

“We have a discrepancy in the overnight reporting.”

Tobbs found the glasses. On the other side of the bed lay the book he'd been trying to read for six weeks, its bookmark still in the first third. He sat up.

“Which desk?”

“Eastern Europe. The two parliamentary contacts are giving us different accounts of the same closed meeting.”

“They ought to. They sit on opposite sides of the aisle.”

“This isn't a difference in interpretation. One reports that the committee met for forty minutes. The other says the session was canceled. Both reports were filed through channels we've used for years.”

“Who confirmed the meeting took place?”

“Neither has independent confirmation.”

“Then you don't have two accounts of the meeting. You have two accounts of whether there was a meeting.”

He heard a keyboard clicking.

“Yes, sir.”

“Get me the building access records if they're available through our normal liaison. If not, wait for the next public committee schedule. Don't ask either source to go back and check. I don't want them looking unusual because our reporting makes us uncomfortable.”

“Understood.”

“And tell your analyst that a disagreement isn't automatically evidence of deception.”

“I'll pass that on.”

The connection ended.

He put the phone down, reached for the light, and found that he was already wearing his glasses. He removed them to rub his eyes.

Four hours of sleep. More than he'd managed on Wednesday.

In the bathroom he washed his face, shaved, and took two tablets from the little compartment marked **FRI** in the organizer beside the sink. The organizer had been Melissa's idea. She'd bought it for him years before the accident after discovering that he treated his own medication schedule with considerably less rigor than the schedules of the people working for him.

He still used it.

His house was quiet except for the refrigerator motor and the low hum of the heating system. He put coffee on, opened the day's calendar at the kitchen counter, and found eleven meetings occupying a period that contained room for approximately seven. The overnight team had already added a briefing at 8:30 without asking.

He moved the budget review to the afternoon, shortened a personnel meeting, and rejected an invitation to address a leadership symposium whose organizer had sent three follow-ups in two days.

At the bottom of the screen was a recurring entry without a location.

**Melissa.**

He opened the message thread.

The last message he'd sent was still unanswered.

*Saw the weather advisory for your area. The front looks ugly. Let me know if you need anything.*

He typed *Good morning, sweetheart*, read it, and erased the words.

He could call. He had called twice this week.

He set the phone beside the coffee mug and opened the next message from the operations center.

At 5:06 his driver pulled into the street.

Tobbs took the coffee with him.

***

By 6:12, the executive suite had begun waking around him. A cleaner was finishing the outer corridor. Two analysts stood at the access desk comparing badge permissions. The duty officer met him outside his office with a thin paper folder and a tablet.

“Morning, sir. You have a call with the deputy at six-thirty. The liaison teams want guidance on the Russian program assessment. There's also a revised Chinese collection estimate.”

“Put the Chinese estimate after the Russian one.”

“They've been asking to go first.”

“They can ask.”

The officer followed him inside.

“And a personnel problem. The deputy director for support wants to know whether the contractor review can wait until Monday.”

“What's the problem?”

“The current agreement expires this evening. Legal says the extension language is defective.”

“Then it can't wait until Monday.”

“He says an emergency extension is possible.”

“An emergency of whose making?”

The officer looked down at the tablet.

“He didn't specify.”

“Ask him. Then ask legal whether the people who need the contract to do their jobs have a lawful bridge through the weekend. I don't want someone on a night shift discovering we've shut down a system because an executive didn't want to spend Friday afternoon reading an agreement.”

“Yes, sir.”

The officer hesitated near the door.

“Something else?”

“The office received another request from Senator Varela's staff. They want to know why the agency needs a revised appropriation for the advanced intelligence assessment program when the department already maintains a technology office.”

“That's a fair question.”

“Their letter doesn't read like they think so.”

“It'll remain fair even if they ask it rudely. Set me up with their staff director. Fifteen minutes. If she's done her homework it'll take five.”

The officer left.

Tobbs placed the paper folder on his desk. It contained an encrypted summary from the seventh of the field operatives he maintained personally, a man whose position in a ruler's inner circle had taken years to establish. The report was brief, more brief than usual.

The principal's security staff had changed the timing of two routine checks. An aide who normally handled internal correspondence had been reassigned. A private dinner had been moved to a smaller residence with different attendants.

None of it was necessarily alarming.

Together, the changes bothered him.

He requested the three previous reports and put them side by side. The man in the field had supplied no explicit warning. The last communication ended with a perfectly ordinary request for an updated medical supply arrangement.

Tobbs wrote **possible access tightening** on a slip of paper and underlined *possible*.

He started an instruction to begin extraction planning, then stopped halfway through the first sentence.

An extraction team appearing in the wrong place could be the very thing that exposed the operative. An instruction to move might prove even more dangerous if the regime had merely changed its security procedures.

He called the operations officer responsible for the region.

“Have your contingency people review the existing withdrawal plan. Quietly. No new contact with our man and no movement toward his location until we have an independent reason.”

“Do you think he's compromised?”

“I think he may need us soon.”

“We can prepare a secondary route.”

“Prepare it. Don't activate it.”

“Understood.”

Tobbs looked at the shortened report again after the call. He moved it beneath the other six field files and left it there.

At 6:30, precisely, the deputy director appeared on the secure display.

“Marcus. You're early.”

“It's six-thirty.”

“You're always early.”

“Then you've never been to dinner with my daughter.”

The deputy smiled, opened his notes, and began with the staffing figures.

For the next forty minutes, Tobbs listened to the kind of information that never appeared in histories of intelligence work: hiring delays, the price of secure facilities, medical coverage for families stationed abroad, translators whose contracts had become tangled between departments, an internal promotion dispute, and the problem of having too few experienced people willing to take a third overseas posting.

He argued over two salary grades and approved one temporary transfer. When the deputy proposed delaying replacement equipment for a regional team, Tobbs asked him to read the failure rate from the previous quarter.

“Eight percent,” the deputy said.

“How many failures could put someone in danger?”

“Depends on which component.”

“Then let's find out before we decide it's a saving.”

The deputy wrote something down.

They finished four minutes late.

Tobbs closed the call and found a sandwich left on the corner of his desk. Someone had written **EAT THIS** on the wrapper.

He did.

***

The Russian assessment occupied twenty-seven pages. A second annex contained diagrams of facilities, procurement estimates, and a timeline assembled from reported construction activity and public technical statements. Some of the information was solid. Some had been repeated so often inside the intelligence community that the original uncertainty had disappeared from later summaries.

Tobbs circled a claim on page nine.

“Who first estimated the capacity of this site?”

The senior analyst across the table took a moment to find the citation.

“An allied service. Their estimate was corroborated by imagery.”

“The construction was corroborated. Was the computing capacity?”

“We have procurement indications.”

“Which could support what range?”

The analyst checked a table.

“The lower end is considerably below the number in the summary.”

“Then why does the summary give only the upper end?”

The analyst looked toward his supervisor.

“We wanted to avoid understating the threat.”

“You can avoid understating it by giving the range. You cannot avoid uncertainty by hiding the lower bound.”

A younger analyst at the far end of the table raised a hand.

“We have another problem with the inference, sir.”

“Go ahead.”

“The report assumes they're using the power envelope for training. If some of that capacity is serving other national programs, the estimated schedule changes.”

“How much?”

“Months, possibly more.”

“Good catch. Put both interpretations in the revision.”

He moved to the Chinese assessment.

It was better organized, with clearer distinctions between observed development, analyst inference, and claims derived from public presentations. The projected capabilities were still difficult to compare against the Russian numbers because the two teams disagreed about what would constitute artificial general intelligence.

“We've spent forty minutes comparing two finish lines,” Tobbs said, “and neither team has told me where the line actually is.”

“We have benchmarks,” the Chinese program lead replied.

“You have tests.”

“Tests accepted across the field.”

“Accepted for what purpose?”

“General reasoning, flexible planning, transfer across unfamiliar tasks.”

“Under controlled conditions.”

“Yes.”

“And what would make you believe a system had reached capabilities beyond those conditions?”

The room went quiet. Finally one of the technical advisers said, “We'd have to observe its behavior in settings that weren't chosen by its developers.”

“Then put that in the assessment.”

The adviser tapped her pen against the folder.

“It will make the estimates less definitive.”

“They are less definitive.”

At ten, the American program team joined by secure video.

Their director looked older than the portrait on his official biography. He spoke with the compressed urgency of someone who had been told he was behind schedule by people who had never attempted the work.

“We need continuity in the appropriation, Marcus. If the Senate turns this into another hearing about terminology, we'll lose the quarter.”

“The Senate is entitled to know what it's buying.”

“They're buying strategic advantage.”

“That is a purpose. It isn't a deliverable.”

The director gave a tired laugh.

“You sound like appropriations.”

“I spend a considerable amount of time with appropriations.”

“Then you know why I'm concerned.”

Tobbs looked at the three assessment summaries laid out on his desk. Each used the same word in its title. Each meant something a little different by it.

“Tell me the one event that would make you conclude the American program had achieved AGI.”

“That depends on the definition.”

“Yours.”

The director began to list evaluations, generalization tests, and autonomous task performance. He was halfway through the list when Tobbs interrupted.

“Would you know if another laboratory had crossed that threshold first?”

“Russia or China?”

“Anyone.”

“If they announced it, eventually.”

“And if they didn't?”

The director glanced away from the camera.

“That's why we have an intelligence service.”

Tobbs said nothing for a moment.

“All right,” the director added. “I see your point.”

“I don't think you do yet. Your briefing tells me what we're funding, what the Russians might be building, and what the Chinese say they're testing. It doesn't tell me whether we're watching the right places.”

“There are private firms.”

“And universities.”

“Universities don't have the resources to build a frontier system without a major partner.”

“Usually.”

“Marcus, we have to prioritize.”

“Of course. But if our definition requires a machine the size of a small power station, an architecture that needs less hardware won't appear in the threat assessment until after it has embarrassed us.”

“Do you have evidence of one?”

“Not yet.”

“Then what do you want?”

“A collection question that doesn't assume its own answer. Tell me how we'd recognize a capability that wasn't produced by any of the programs we're currently watching.”

The director was silent long enough for the communication system to display a connection warning.

Then he said, “I'll ask the technical team.”

“Thank you.”

The call ended.

Tobbs put all three summaries into a single folder and wrote a question across the cover:

**WHAT ARE WE NOT MEASURING?**

***

At 11:24, the assistant director responsible for interagency coordination arrived carrying a folder she had been trying to get onto Tobbs's calendar for two days.

“You asked for university research that doesn't fit the current assessments,” she said.

“I asked for the means by which we'd find it.”

“Good. That will make this less disappointing.”

Her name was Helen Armitage. She had spent enough years moving between intelligence and scientific agencies to know when an apparent technical disagreement was actually a dispute over money. She sat without waiting for an invitation and passed him the folder.

“The strategic technology office maintains a standing review of federally supported work. Public publications, grant reports, technical objections submitted to funding offices, that sort of thing. They flag anything that intersects with critical capabilities.”

Tobbs opened it.

“And?”

“And most of it is routine. Five firms claiming the next large model will solve everything. A university proposing a genuinely interesting memory system that can't run at useful scale. A defense contractor trying to persuade us that renaming an old product makes it new.”

“You brought me the contractor's sales pitch?”

“No. I brought you the part they almost threw out.”

Halfway through the packet was a small collection of academic memoranda. One concerned the reliability of model evaluations under changing conditions. Another concerned the preservation of computational state across interrupted processes. A third, copied through a university research administration office because of a funding dispute, criticized the reduction of an active project's computing allocation.

It was signed by Professor Adrian Wexler.

“The complaining professor?” Tobbs asked.

“That was the initial classification, yes.”

“What changed it?”

“One of our technical reviewers recognized the name. Wexler hasn't published much recently, but he was important in machine learning long before half our current advisers were out of school. He attached a preliminary architecture by his former student.”

Tobbs read the memorandum.

*If the committee considers a thirty percent reduction inconsequential, I invite its members to identify which thirty percent of the experimental requirements they believe can be discarded while retaining a valid result.*

He read the sentence again, this time with a small smile.

“The university's having an enjoyable week.”

“Apparently not,” Armitage said.

“Who's the student?”

“Not a student anymore. Dr. Elizabeth Garrett. Tenured senior researcher. Strong publication record, poor relations with her department, and a remarkable ability to make people regret asking her for an opinion.”

“That doesn't narrow the field.”

“Her grant is comparatively modest. The architecture doesn't rely solely on the assumption that bigger training runs will produce general intelligence.”

“What does it rely on?”

“Continuity between processes. They're proposing differentiated cognitive reservoirs with ongoing state change, plus a way to keep the system from confusing remembered assertions with verified facts. It's early work. I wouldn't call it a credible AGI candidate yet.”

“Why not?”

“They haven't demonstrated it at the necessary scale. Some of the components are sketches. There are unanswered questions about how the reservoirs cooperate under load and how the recovered process establishes which of its own assumptions remain valid.”

“Those sound like the questions I'd want her to answer.”

“So did Wexler.”

He read the rest of the memo, then the accompanying technical summary.

“Who sent this to the strategic technology office?”

“The research administration office referred it to the federal sponsor after Wexler challenged the reduction. The sponsor asked for a technical assessment rather than adjudicating the university dispute. That's how it entered the standing review.”

“And what was the assessment?”

“Promising, unverified. The reviewer suggested a small additional evaluation. The item wasn't given priority.”

“Because?”

“It doesn't fit our current procurement categories. And the originating complaint is a funding dispute.”

Tobbs closed the folder.

“Keep it in the assessment. Mark it as speculative. I want someone technically competent to look at the underlying proposal without making claims it can't support.”

Armitage nodded.

“You may find the investigator difficult.”

“I'm finding the entire subject difficult.”

“I meant personally.”

“I've survived personnel hearings, appropriations, and three consecutive agency reorganizations, Helen. Let me read her file before you warn me away.”

Armitage rose.

At the door, she turned back.

“You should know Wexler has been ill. The university isn't publicizing the details.”

Tobbs looked at the signature on the memo.

“Was this one authenticated?”

“Yes. The original went through the university's normal system. No indication anyone wrote it for him.”

“Then we'll judge the work that arrived.”

***

The senator's staff director was waiting on the line when Tobbs returned from a hastily shortened lunch.

“Mr. Executive Director,” she said.

“Ms. Desai. Thank you for making time.”

“I understand you have a different interpretation of the advanced intelligence appropriation.”

“I have a question about it. That isn't the same thing.”

“All right. What's the question?”

“When the committee asks whether the existing technology office can do this assessment, do you mean the technical evaluation or the intelligence work around it?”

“We want to know why we should fund two organizations to tell us whether foreign states are ahead of us.”

“You shouldn't. The technical office should evaluate technical claims. We should determine where the foreign programs are, what they can actually do, and what they intend to do with them. We rely on each other, but the work isn't interchangeable.”

“Your last submission reads as though you want to run the research yourself.”

“Then the submission is badly written.”

She laughed once.

“You don't get many executives admitting that.”

“You don't get many appropriations committees accepting a document at its own estimate of its quality.”

“Fair enough. What about private research? Your definition of the mission seems broad enough to follow every startup that buys a graphics processor.”

“That's a legitimate concern. We need thresholds and review. I won't ask you to give us unlimited authority because someone has attached the initials AGI to a budget request.”

She paused.

“Would you put that in writing?”

“I thought it was already in writing.”

“No. What's in writing is twenty-six pages of people explaining why their program is indispensable.”

“I'll have you a shorter document by Monday.”

“Three pages.”

“Four.”

“Three.”

“Three, then.”

When the call ended, he made a note to have the budget staff remove a section full of predictions no one could defend. He had seen forecasts become institutional commitments too many times. Once an agency spent money defending a number, its employees began treating any evidence against the number as an obstacle to the mission.

The afternoon did not improve.

A cabinet-level source in an allied country had been asked to attend a meeting outside the normal schedule. The liaison officer worried the invitation was a loyalty test. Tobbs wanted to know who else had received one before accepting the theory.

A second cabinet contact reported that a minister's departure would likely trigger a reshuffle. The analysts had already drafted a forecast based on the assumption that one faction would benefit. Tobbs sent it back for another version that considered what happened if the minister stayed.

One of the parliamentary operatives asked permission to accept a ceremonial appointment that would increase access while also creating obligations his cover might not withstand. Tobbs asked for the proposed duties, not merely the prestige of the title.

At three he spent half an hour with an internal team whose professional disagreement had become personal enough to affect the handling of information. Neither officer liked being told to return with a written plan for separating their responsibilities. Both left with the same instruction.

His operations officer called while the door was still closing.

“About the seventh file.”

Tobbs sat down.

“What have you got?”

“No additional independent confirmation. The security reshuffle appears to involve several senior aides, not just the staff around our man. The alternate withdrawal route is available, though we'd need advance coordination to activate it.”

“And that coordination could draw attention.”

“Yes.”

“Keep it ready. Continue normal reporting. No new signal to him.”

“We could ask for a wellness confirmation.”

“Not because we're anxious. He knows the conditions under which to request help.”

“You don't seem anxious.”

Tobbs looked at the report in front of him.

“Good.”

Afterward he wrote a short instruction ensuring that the operative's family liaison had current contact information. The file was already supposed to be current. He asked for verification anyway.

***

At 4:46, his assistant knocked and brought him the requested personnel file.

“Garrett,” she said. “The academic from the technical assessment.”

“Thank you.”

“Do you want someone from science and technology in the meeting?”

“There isn't a meeting yet.”

“Your calendar has a tentative block next week.”

“Then call it tentative.”

“That's what it says.”

He gave her a look over the top of his glasses.

“I'm trying to get ahead of you, sir.”

“An ambitious career choice.”

She left him with the file.

The front page held a faculty photograph of Elizabeth Garrett. She looked directly at the camera, unsmiling, with one eyebrow slightly higher than the other. Whoever had taken the picture had probably asked her to relax.

Tobbs caught himself smiling.

Melissa used to give him that look when she was thirteen, usually after he'd told her something obvious and she'd decided not to dignify it with a reply.

He turned the page.

The publication list was longer than he'd expected. Some titles he recognized only from the briefing; others involved mathematical models, adaptive systems, and questions of computational continuity. Her formal evaluations were strong until the section on collaboration, where the language became noticeably less precise.

*Difficult.*

*Frequently confrontational.*

*Resistant to agreed departmental priorities.*

One evaluator had written that Garrett was “unlikely to benefit from additional mentoring in collegial expectations.”

Tobbs laughed aloud.

He checked the dates. The harshest evaluations followed technical disagreements in which Garrett had later published corrections to work that others had defended.

That didn't establish she was pleasant. It did make him wonder whether the evaluators were describing her conduct or their frustration.

He opened Wexler's memorandum again.

The professor's language was careful until it reached the question of reducing her allocation. There, the contempt was unmistakable.

Tobbs took off his glasses.

The phone lay beside the file.

Melissa's contact was still on the screen from that morning. He opened the message thread, looked at the last unanswered text, and pressed Call.

It rang four times.

Her recorded greeting began.

He waited through it.

“Hi, sweetheart. It's Dad. Nothing's wrong. I just wanted to hear how you're doing. There's a new little place near your apartment that does the breakfast you used to like. I thought maybe, sometime, we could try it. No pressure. You don't even have to let me pay.”

He looked at the ceiling while the voicemail timer continued.

“Anyway. Call if you need anything. Love you.”

He ended the call.

On his desk was a letter from the specialist who managed Melissa's prosthetic care. It had arrived earlier in the week. He had checked the funding authorization twice and spoken directly to the administrator once to make certain no appointment would be delayed.

The medical office had assured him everything was arranged.

There was no reply from Melissa.

He slid the letter into the drawer, leaving Garrett's file open on the desk.

***

The technical annex didn't pretend to settle the architecture's feasibility. It offered several possible advantages, followed by an unusually candid list of reasons the system might fail. The reconciliation mechanism became expensive as dependencies multiplied. State restoration could produce a coherent but obsolete cognitive model. Accumulated confidence could harden into error. Even if those problems were solved in simulation, the transition to a continuously operating system posed questions the proposal hadn't answered.

Tobbs read the failure list twice.

Most proposals he'd seen buried uncertainty in appendices. Garrett had put some of the worst problems close to the beginning.

He called Armitage.

“How much did the student know about the technical review?”

“Garrett? Nothing, as far as I know. Wexler's memo was circulated through the sponsor's assessment process.”

“Then let's not have an intelligence officer turn up at her laboratory unannounced. Have the science office ask for a lawful technical conversation. Make it clear who would attend and what the conversation concerns.”

“You want an academic briefing?”

“I want to know whether her work is significant before somebody converts its possible significance into a national emergency.”

“And if she refuses?”

“Then she refuses. We can continue assessing what she's published.”

“You could ask the sponsor to require a meeting.”

“Her sponsor appears to have enough trouble keeping its promises.”

Armitage made a small noise that might have been agreement.

“I'll prepare the request.”

“Don't send it yet. I want to read the original architecture, not a digest. And find someone who can disagree with her on technical grounds. If she is as difficult as this file says, a room full of people who can't follow the mathematics will accomplish nothing.”

“I know two people.”

“Ask them separately. I don't want a committee arriving before we know what question we're asking.”

He set down the phone.

There were still three items on his official calendar. He moved one to Monday, kept the legal review, and asked the duty officer to send the evening field summary to his secure terminal at home.

At 7:18, he left the office.

The driver asked whether he wanted the usual route.

“Yes. Unless there's a reason not to.”

Traffic was heavy near the interchange. Tobbs took out the file he'd brought with him, tried reading the same paragraph twice, and gave up. He watched the cars beside them instead.

A little girl in the back seat of a neighboring car was laughing so hard at something her father said that she had to cover her face with both hands. The father glanced at her in his rearview mirror and laughed too.

Their car changed lanes and disappeared behind a delivery truck.

Tobbs looked down at his phone.

No new messages.

At home he reheated the remains of the takeout he'd ordered the night before, ate standing at the kitchen counter, and took off his jacket only after his operations officer confirmed that the field reports had come through.

The seventh operative's update contained no new information.

He checked the timing, read the last line, and put the report aside.

Then he opened Garrett's original sketch on the larger screen in his study.

Three reservoirs, different physical change increments, continuous interaction. A ledger intended to preserve the difference between what a process remembered, what it inferred, and what it actually knew. An unfinished mechanism for reconciling interruptions without pretending the interruption hadn't happened.

He read Wexler's memorandum again, especially the professor's refusal to let the committee dismiss the work because its author had made enemies.

Tobbs picked up his pen and added a note to the margin of the assessment:

*Evaluate architecture independently of projected hardware scale. Determine what evidence would distinguish persistent cognitive state from reconstructed memory. Ask what failure would look like before asking what success might be worth.*

He left the note beside the diagram.

Near midnight the duty officer called to confirm the next morning's schedule. The operative review was still on it, along with a revised Russian assessment and an early planning meeting for the Senate hearing.

“Anything else, sir?”

Tobbs looked at the tentative academic briefing marked for the following week.

“Yes. Keep a slot open for Dr. Garrett. No invitation until we've got the technical questions ready.”

“Understood.”

The officer ended the call.

Tobbs switched off the desk lamp. The house settled into its familiar quiet, and the phone lay on the corner of the desk with its screen dark.

He carried it upstairs.
