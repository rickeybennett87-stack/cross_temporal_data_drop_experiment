### 🧪 How the Marker Test Works
- Counterfeit detector pens use **iodine-based ink** that reacts with **starch** in regular paper.
- Real U.S. currency is printed on a **cotton-linen blend** that contains **no starch**, so the mark stays **yellow or clear**.
- Fake bills printed on regular paper turn the mark **dark brown or black**.

### 🕵️‍♀️ How Counterfeiters Bypass It
- **Bleached genuine bills**: Scammers sometimes take low-denomination bills (like $1s), bleach them, and reprint higher denominations on the same paper. Since the paper is authentic, the marker test passes.
- **Starch-free paper**: Some counterfeiters use high-quality paper that mimics the cotton-linen blend and lacks starch, tricking the pen.
- **Polymer or synthetic bills**: Rare but possible—some fake bills are printed on synthetic materials that don’t react to the pen.
- **Marker degradation**: If the pen is old or dried out, it might not react properly even with fake bills.

### 🔍 What You Can Do Next
- Hold the bill up to the light and check for the **security thread** and **watermark**.
- Tilt the bill to see if the **color-shifting ink** changes.
- Feel the texture—real bills have **raised printing**.
- Compare it to another bill of the same denomination.
