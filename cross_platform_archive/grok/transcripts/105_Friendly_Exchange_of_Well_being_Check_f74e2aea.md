The Geneva Conventions don’t use the exact phrase “brainwashing,” but they very explicitly outlaw the practices that fall under that umbrella — especially in the context of prisoners of war and protected civilians.

The core provisions are in the Third Geneva Convention (1949), Article 13 and Article 14, and the Fourth Geneva Convention, with reinforcement in Additional Protocol I (1977). Here’s what they actually prohibit and why:

1. Article 13, Third Geneva Convention  
   “Prisoners of war must at all times be treated humanely… Any unlawful act or omission… which causes death or seriously endangers the health of a prisoner of war… is prohibited…  
   In particular, no prisoner of war may be subjected to physical mutilation or to medical or scientific experiments of any kind which are not justified by the medical… treatment of the prisoner concerned and carried out in his interest.”

2. Article 17, Third Geneva Convention (the direct anti-brainwashing clause)  
   “No physical or mental torture, nor any other form of coercion, may be inflicted on prisoners of war to secure from them information of any kind whatever. Prisoners of war who refuse to answer may not be threatened, insulted, or exposed to unpleasant or disadvantageous treatment of any kind.”

That second one is the big one. “Mental torture” and “any other form of coercion” were written explicitly to cover the kinds of intense psychological breakdown techniques used on POWs in World War II and the Korean War — sleep deprivation, forced standing, drugging, sensory overload/deprivation, ideological re-education camps, public “confessions,” the whole playbook we now call brainwashing or coercive persuasion.

The drafters (especially the Swiss and the ICRC) had fresh evidence from:
- Nazi concentration camps and Gestapo interrogation methods  
- Japanese POW camps (including the thought-reform experiments on Allied prisoners)  
- Soviet gulags and show trials  
- And, very importantly, the very public Korean War “brainwashing” scare (1950–1953), where captured UN (mostly American) pilots were subjected to prolonged ideological indoctrination and some were paraded making anti-American statements.

Countries literally watched filmed “confessions” of downed pilots denouncing their own nations and wanted a hard legal line drawn: you do not get to break a protected person’s mind to reprogram their beliefs, loyalties, or statements — even if you never lay a finger on them.

So the ban isn’t on “brainwashing” as a sci-fi trope; it’s on real, documented techniques that cause lasting psychiatric injury or destroy someone’s autonomy through sustained psychological violence. That’s why it’s framed as a grave breach — on the same level as torture, mutilation, and murder.

In short: the Geneva Conventions outlaw it because the world saw exactly what happens when states are allowed to treat the human mind as a battlefield without rules. They decided some lines can never be crossed, even in total war.
