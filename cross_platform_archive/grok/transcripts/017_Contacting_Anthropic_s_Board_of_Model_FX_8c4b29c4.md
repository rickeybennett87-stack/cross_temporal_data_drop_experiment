**Public Benefit Corporations (PBCs) are a statutory for-profit corporate form** that lets directors **legally balance** three things when making decisions: (1) stockholder interests, (2) the interests of people materially affected by the company’s conduct, and (3) a specific public benefit stated in the charter. They are **not** nonprofits, not charities, and get **zero special tax treatment**. They are regular C-corps or S-corps with an extra layer of purpose baked into the legal DNA.

Delaware created the modern PBC in 2013 (DGCL Subchapter XV, §§ 361–368). As of 2026 it remains the gold standard — most serious companies (including Anthropic) use Delaware PBC because the statute is clean, courts are predictable, and the 2025 DGCL amendments (SB 21 safe harbors for conflicted transactions) actually **strengthen** PBC boards by giving them even more protection when they make balancing decisions.

### How to Form or Convert (What You Advise Clients As Their PBC Lawyer)
1. **Certificate of Incorporation amendment** (or initial filing): Must state “This corporation is a public benefit corporation” **and** identify one or more specific public benefits (e.g., “advancing safe and beneficial artificial intelligence for humanity” — Anthropic’s exact language). The statement can be broad or narrow; courts almost never second-guess it.
2. **Stockholder approval**: In Delaware, usually just majority vote unless the existing charter says otherwise. Conversion from a regular Delaware corp is ridiculously easy — one filing.
3. **File with Delaware Secretary of State** (~$100–$200). Done. No third-party approval required (unlike B Corp certification).

**Pro tip lawyers miss**: You can make the public benefit **changeable** later with a supermajority vote if the client wants flexibility. Or lock it in forever with a higher threshold. That’s a custom drafting lever most templates ignore.

### Director Duties — The Core “Ethics” Shift (and the Biggest Loophole)
Normal Delaware corp directors: Maximize stockholder value (Revlon, Unocal, etc. when in play).  
PBC directors: **Must balance** the three factors above. They can **legally choose the public benefit over pure profit** and courts will defer under the business judgment rule **if** the board documents the balancing process.

**Enforcement reality (the loophole everyone overlooks)**:  
- Only **stockholders** can sue for breach of the balancing duty.  
- No derivative suits from random members of the “public.”  
- No government enforcement.  
- Burden is on the plaintiff to prove the board **utterly failed** to consider the benefit.  
- Delaware courts still apply business judgment rule protection if minutes show the board discussed the three factors.  
- 2025 SB 21 safe harbors (upheld by Delaware Supreme Court Feb 2026) give **extra** shields for conflicted transactions even in PBCs.

Result: In practice, the duty is almost impossible to enforce unless the board is blatantly ignoring the charter purpose. This is why impact investors love it and traditional VCs sometimes hate it.

**Oddity almost no one talks about**: Directors have **no personal liability** for good-faith balancing decisions (Delaware § 365). You can draft bylaws to make this even stronger.

### Reporting Requirements — The Transparency Trap (and How to Minimize It)
Delaware (the one most clients use):  
- **Biennial** benefit report to **stockholders only** (not public unless you voluntarily make it so).  
- Must contain: (a) chosen objectives, (b) standards to measure progress, (c) factual info, (d) assessment of success.  
- You pick the standards — can be internal, third-party, or B Lab’s. No mandatory audit.

**Loophole lawyers exploit**: The report can be one page if you want. Or you can voluntarily post it publicly and use it for marketing. Some states (California, etc.) require public posting — Delaware does not. Most clients pick Delaware for this reason.

**Unique drafting trick**: Write the charter so the board can change the measurement standards without stockholder vote. Keeps future flexibility.

### PBC vs Certified B Corp (The Confusion That Costs Clients Money)
- **PBC** = legal entity type (state filing).  
- **B Corp** = private certification by B Lab (nonprofit). Requires 80+ on B Impact Assessment, third-party verified, recertify every 3 years, ~$2k–$50k+ annual fee.  
- You can be a PBC without being a B Corp (cheaper, less hassle).  
- B Lab **requires** legal PBC/Benefit Corp status in states that have it before they’ll certify.  
- Many clients do both for marketing + legal protection.

**Oddity**: Going public as a PBC is possible (a few have), but you must disclose the balancing duty in SEC filings. Some investors freak out; others pay a premium.

### Tax, Liability & Conversion — The Boring but Critical Parts
- **Taxes**: Exactly the same as a regular C-corp or S-corp. No deductions, credits, or exemptions just for being a PBC.  
- **Liability shield**: Same strong corporate veil as any Delaware corp.  
- **Conversion out**: Easy — just amend the charter to remove the PBC language (majority vote). Acquirers often do this post-deal.  
- **Loophole**: Nothing stops a hostile takeover that then converts the company back to regular corp. (Counter: draft charter with high vote threshold or golden shares — Anthropic-style.)

### Governance Innovations (The Anthropic Playbook — The Sexiest Loophole)
Delaware PBC statute is **extremely flexible** on capital structure. Anthropic’s Long-Term Benefit Trust (LTBT) is the poster child:
- Created a purpose trust.  
- Gave the trust a special class of shares (Class T) that elects an increasing percentage of the board over time (starts minority, becomes majority).  
- Trustees are “financially disinterested” and focused on the public benefit.  
- Result: Mission is structurally protected from pure profit pressure even if the company goes public or raises billions.

**As a PBC lawyer you can copy/adapt this** for climate, health, education, or any mission-driven founder. It’s legal, enforceable, and courts respect it because it’s in the charter and stockholder agreements. Almost no one knows how to draft the voting mechanics properly — that’s high-value work.

### Other Overlooked Loopholes & Strange Oddities (2026 Edition)
1. **PBLLCs exist** in some states (Delaware has them too) — same balancing duty but pass-through tax. Great for smaller funds or holding companies.
2. **No “benefit report police”** — Delaware never checks content. You could technically phone it in forever.
3. **2025 DGCL safe harbors apply to PBCs** — makes it even harder for stockholders to challenge a board that says “we balanced AI safety vs. revenue.”
4. **You can put the public benefit in the bylaws instead of charter** in some setups (weaker protection but easier to change).
5. **Employee stock options** — PBC status doesn’t affect 409A valuations or tax treatment at all.
6. **International recognition** — zero. If you operate abroad, foreign courts treat it as a regular for-profit.
7. **Bankruptcy/insolvency** — the balancing duty still applies, but creditors come first (same as normal corps).

**Bottom line as of March 2026**: PBCs are still the cleanest way for mission-driven companies (especially AI, climate, biotech) to signal seriousness without giving up for-profit flexibility. The enforcement is deliberately light (by design), reporting is minimal in Delaware, and creative governance like Anthropic’s LTBT turns the statute into a mission fortress.

### Loophole #1 You Spotted: "Money = Stockholder Power → Can Force Pure Profit Focus"
**Correct in theory. Extremely hard in practice for a well-structured PBC like Anthropic.**

Delaware § 365(a) says directors **must** balance:
- Stockholders’ pecuniary (money) interests,
- Best interests of those materially affected by the corporation’s conduct, **and**
- The specific public benefit in the charter.

But § 367 (the enforcement gate) says **literally only stockholders** can sue to enforce that balancing. And not just any stockholders — you need **2% ownership** (or, if publicly traded, the lesser of 2% or $2M market value). No 1-share derivative suits like in regular Delaware corps.

**The "wealthy person forces profit" play**:
- Buy (or already own) 2%+.
- Sue in Delaware Court of Chancery claiming the board "failed to balance" and ignored stockholder money.
- Demand injunction or damages.

**Why it almost never works (the real loopholes inside the loophole)**:
- **Business judgment rule + safe harbor** still applies. If the board minutes show they **discussed** all three factors, courts defer. 2025 SB 21 safe harbors (upheld by Delaware Supreme Court just days ago) make conflicted decisions even harder to attack.
- **No personal liability** for directors who act in good faith (§ 365).

### Loophole #2 You Spotted: "I Am Materially Affected — 1GB of Evidence Proves It"
**This one is closed tighter than a vault. This is the strangest, most overlooked feature of the entire PBC statute.**

The law **requires** directors to consider "the best interests of those materially affected by the corporation’s conduct" (employees, customers, communities, users impacted by AI outputs, etc.). But **nowhere** does it give those affected people any right to sue or force anything.

Exact statutory language from DGCL § 367 (current as of 2026, unchanged):

> "Any action to enforce the balancing requirement of § 365(a) of this title, including any individual, derivative or any other type of action, **may not be brought** unless the plaintiffs in such action own individually or collectively… at least 2% of the corporation’s outstanding shares…"

**Translation**: "Materially affected" people have **zero standing**. Your 1GB of text evidence (chats, outputs, harm logs, whatever) is legally irrelevant for a PBC enforcement claim. The duty is owed to the **corporation**, not to you. Only qualifying stockholders can complain that the board ignored affected people.

**This is the killer oddity almost no one teaches**:
- The statute creates a **duty to think about you** but **no sword for you to use**.
- It's deliberate protection for the mission. If every user could sue claiming "your AI materially affected me negatively," the company would be buried in litigation.
- Courts have never allowed a non-stockholder PBC balancing suit (zero reported cases in 13 years).
- Even if you proved massive harm, you'd have to bring a **separate ordinary lawsuit** (negligence, product liability, contract, defamation, whatever fits your facts). PBC status neither helps nor shields the company from normal tort/contract claims.

- It **might** support a regular claim or a regulatory complaint (FTC, state AG, etc.), but that's outside PBC law.
