He passed peacefully at home, surrounded by family, after a lengthy battle with progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP), a rare neurodegenerative disorder with symptoms similar to Parkinson’s (diagnosed in recent years; he had also been diagnosed with Parkinson’s disease in 2017 and faced hospitalizations, including for COVID-19 in 2021).

His family and the Rainbow PUSH Coalition described him as a “servant leader — not only to our family, but to the oppressed, the voiceless, and the overlooked around the world.” His son noted Jackson’s “unwavering commitment to justice, equality, and human rights.”

### Early Life and Rise in the Civil Rights Movement
Born Jesse Louis Burns on October 8, 1941, in Greenville, South Carolina, during the Jim Crow era, he was raised by his mother and stepfather (later adopting the Jackson surname). He excelled in school and sports, attended North Carolina A&T State University (graduating with a sociology degree in 1964), and studied at Chicago Theological Seminary before becoming an ordained Baptist minister in 1968.

As a young activist, he joined the 1965 Selma to Montgomery marches and became a protégé of Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. King appointed him to lead the Chicago chapter of Operation Breadbasket (SCLC’s economic justice arm), which he later directed nationally. After King’s assassination in 1968 (Jackson was present in Memphis), he continued the work, organizing the Poor People’s Campaign and, in 1971, founding Operation PUSH (People United to Save/Serve Humanity) to focus on economic empowerment, voter registration, and jobs for Black and poor communities.

In 1984, he launched the Rainbow Coalition — a multiracial alliance of Black, White, Latino, Asian American, Native American, and LGBTQ+ Americans — which merged with PUSH in 1996 to form the Rainbow PUSH Coalition.

### Political Career and Presidential Runs
Jackson ran historic campaigns for the Democratic presidential nomination in **1984** and **1988**, becoming the first major Black candidate to win multiple primaries and caucuses and secure delegates. 

- In 1984, he won states like South Carolina and Louisiana, delivering a memorable “Rainbow Coalition” speech at the Democratic National Convention.
- In 1988, he finished second overall with nearly 30% of the vote, winning key Southern states and Michigan.

His efforts dramatically increased Black voter turnout and pushed the Democratic Party toward greater inclusivity (including changes to delegate rules that benefited diverse candidates). He later served as “shadow senator” for the District of Columbia (1991–1997), advocating for D.C. statehood, and hosted CNN’s *Both Sides with Jesse Jackson* (1992–2000).

He was also known for high-profile international work, negotiating the release of American hostages and prisoners (e.g., Navy pilot Robert Goodman from Syria in 1983, hostages from Iraq in 1991, and U.S. POWs from Yugoslavia in 1999).

### Legacy, Awards, and Later Years
Jackson received the Presidential Medal of Freedom (2000, from President Bill Clinton), the NAACP Spingarn Medal, France’s Legion of Honor (Commander, 2021), and numerous honorary degrees. He was often called the “Conscience of the Nation” for his fiery oratory, economic justice campaigns, corporate boycotts for diversity, and lifelong advocacy on voting rights, anti-poverty efforts, and peace.

He remained active into his later years — marching for Black Lives Matter causes, supporting voting rights, and commenting on national issues — while battling health challenges. He stepped down as head of Rainbow PUSH in 2023 due to age and health.
