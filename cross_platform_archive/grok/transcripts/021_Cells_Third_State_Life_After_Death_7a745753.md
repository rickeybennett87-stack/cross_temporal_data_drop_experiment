### Simplified Explanation
The post refers to research on cellular behavior after an organism dies. Traditionally, we think of cells as either alive (in a living body) or dead (fully inactive). But some studies show a "third state": certain cells from a dead organism don't just die off quietly. Given the right lab conditions (like nutrients and cues), they can reorganize into entirely new, multicellular structures with novel abilities.

- **Xenobots**: Made from skin cells of frog embryos that were prevented from developing further (essentially "deceased"). These tiny bots self-assemble, move using hair-like cilia, heal themselves, and even replicate by gathering loose cells into copies of themselves.
- **Anthrobots**: Similar, but from human lung cells (e.g., from a donor). They form small, mobile clusters that can navigate, self-repair, and even help heal damaged nearby nerve cells by bridging gaps.

These aren't zombies or revived organisms—the original frog or human is still dead. It's about individual cells retaining surprising plasticity and "creativity" to form new functional entities never seen in nature.

### Potential Future Uses
Researchers hope to engineer patient-specific biobots for medicine: delivering drugs precisely, clearing artery plaque, or repairing tissues—then safely breaking down without triggering immune reactions.
